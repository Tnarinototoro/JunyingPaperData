Efforts like these to restore biodiversity via the introduction of species, draw inspiration from the notable ecological recovery observed at Yellowstone National Park (US) following the reintroduction of grey wolves, which triggered beneficial trophic cascades affecting multiple species and habitat recovery. The emergence of rewilding as a tool to help restore functioning ecosystems has gained considerable traction over the past decade. Large herbivores play a pivotal role in such interventions, and considerable progress has been made in understanding their role in, for example, acting as surrogates for extinct species. An example of such extinct animal surrogacy in the UK is the steppe bison. Initially present in the UK until its extinction around 8,000Â years ago, the steppe bison, Bison priscus, shares a close genetic lineage with the European bison released into the Blean woodlands. These European bison were introduced as âecosystem engineersâ, whose natural behaviours such as grazing, wallowing, and soil disturbance have been demonstrated to enhance plant and arthropod diversity significantly As well as structuring plant and arthropod communities; habitat restoration and mitigating climate change. Less attention has been paid to the implications of releasing herbivores on the microbiomes of the animals themselves and the habitats they are intended to manage. This is despite the fact microbiome rewilding may have a significant impact on food security, human health and well-being as well as functioning ecosystems.
