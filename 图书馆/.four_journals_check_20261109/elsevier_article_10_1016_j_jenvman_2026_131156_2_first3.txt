A B S T R A C T
Accelerating urbanisation challenges biodiversity and human wellbeing, demanding effective urban nature-based 
solutions. Urban wildflower meadows are increasingly popular interventions that may offer biodiversity and 
wellbeing benefits. However, realising their potential depends on public support, which requires robust evidence 
on which meadow design attributes the public prefers and values most. We conducted a choice experiment with 
urban greenspace users across three UK cities to quantify willingness-to-pay (WTP) for some of the key char-
acteristics of wildflower meadows in public parks, namely flower colour diversity, native species composition, 
plant species richness, and pollinator habitat quality. Respondents generally preferred wildflower meadow op-
tions over the status quo of mown amenity grass and were willing to pay for improvements to meadow attributes 
through increased local taxation. Respondents had positive WTP for more colourful, pollinator-friendly meadows 
with more native species, but not for additional plant species. Preferences were largely consistent across re-
spondents interviewed at different meadow types (annual, perennial, or control sites), suggesting that enhanced 
attribute salience from direct exposure to existing meadows had minimal influence on stated preferences. 
Methodologically, this suggests that in situ surveys of preferences for urban greenspace are broadly robust to 
salience effects and can inform management beyond the surveyed sites. Attributes that offer positive outcomes 
for biodiversity, such as supporting pollinators and including native species, were aligned with, rather than 
conflicting with, public preferences. Urban greenspace managers can enhance public support while achieving 
ecological objectives by designing meadows with moderate colour diversity, high native species content, and 
demonstrable pollinator benefits.
1. Introduction
The proportion of the world's population living in cities is projected 
to rise to 68% by 2050 (UN Habitat, 2022). In developed regions, this 
percentage is much higher; over 80% of the UK population is urban 
(DEFRA, 2021). Urbanisation increases pressures on biodiversity (Hahs 
et al., 2023), ecosystem service provision (Karimi et al., 2021) and 
people-nature interactions (Soga and Gaston, 2022). Amidst these 
challenges, opportunities remain to enhance both biodiversity and 
human wellbeing through integrating natural features into new and 
regenerated urban landscapes (Artmann et al., 2017; Seto et al., 2012).
Stakeholders and policy makers interested in urban planning are 
increasingly using nature-based solutions that can deliver multiple 
benefits for both nature and people (Pineda-Pinto et al., 2021). Biodi-
versity in urban greenspaces can provide a plethora of benefits e.g., 
climate change resilience and pollution mitigation (Nilon et al., 2017); 
and supporting a greater diversity and abundance of insect pollinator 
species (Llodra-Llabr´es and Cari˜ nanos, 2022; Theodorou et al., 2020; 
Wenzel et al., 2020). Urban greenspaces also contribute to human health 
and wellbeing by reducing psychological stress (e.g., Cox et al., 2017), 
having restorative effects (Hoyle et al., 2017a), enabling positive in-
teractions with biodiversity (e.g., Fisher et al., 2022, 2023) and creating

to experiencing nature in situ over virtual experiences. Thus, urban 
greenspaces may enhance biodiversity while providing opportunities for 
beneficial interactions with nature.
The design and management of urban greenspaces can prove chal-
lenging when balancing stakeholder goals (Jia et al., 2023). For 
instance, rather than ecological function (Mach´aˇc et al., 2022), some 
users value the recreational benefits of an urban greenspace, (e.g., Kefale 
et al., 2023; Palliwoda and Priess, 2021), whereas other users may prefer 
spaces with vegetation that is colourful, florally and structurally diverse 
(e.g., Austen et al., 2021; Lindemann-Matthies and Bose, 2007; Southon 
et al., 2017). Practical management interventions in urban areas need to 
balance human needs and perceptions, alongside the goal of maintaining 
ecological processes (Aronson et al., 2017; Reyes-Riveros et al., 2021). 
However, urban greenspace managers often overlook the needs and 
preferences of users, particularly the role of nature in their wellbeing 
(Ugolini et al., 2022). Moreover, the concepts used by scientists to 
describe ecological quality may not correspond with the cultural con-
cepts of aesthetic quality valued by the public (Austen et al., 2023; Pett 
et al., 2016). To create successful, effective, and long-lasting nature-- 
based solutions, we must understand where these concepts align or 
diverge.
The introduction of urban wildflower meadows for insect pollinators 
is an increasingly popular form of ecological intervention in urban 
greenspaces (Roguz et al., 2023). Southon et al. (2017) found site users 
preferred urban wildflower meadows to herbaceous borders, bedding 
plants and mown amenity grass. Species-rich meadows can therefore 
potentially provide a win-win strategy for biodiversity and urban 
greenspace users (Aronson et al., 2017; Southon et al., 2017). Critically, 
urban greenspace management affects publicly owned, shared spaces 
serving many stakeholders, and are designed and implemented using 
public funds. It is, therefore, imperative for decision-makers to under-
stand and represent public preferences and attitudes towards environ-
mental interventions (Graves et al., 2017; King et al., 2025). 
Management of urban greenspace requires a multi-faceted approach 
to balance social, environmental, and economic needs, particularly as 
budget constraints impose trade-offs in urban greenspace management 
(Rambhia et al., 2024). A key challenge for policy makers is to under-
stand public preferences for trade-offs between different meadow at-
tributes. Stated preference methods, such as Choice Experiments (CEs), 
can address this challenge by quantifying respondents' preferences for 
the different attributes of urban meadows in willingness-to-pay (WTP) 
terms (Christie et al., 2006; Hanley and Barbier, 2009). In contrast, most 
prior work on individuals' preferences has used qualitative measures 
(Fischer et al., 2020; Hoyle et al., 2017a) with less evidence quantifying 
preferences for urban meadows in WTP terms (Tomitaka et al., 2022). 
This points to a need to understand respondents’ preferences for changes 
in the attributes of urban meadows.
An important methodological consideration is whether preferences 
elicited in situ, i.e., adjacent to existing meadows, differ from those eli-
cited in hypothetical contexts because of enhanced attribute salience 
(Haghani et al., 2021a, 2021b). Salience describes how noticeable or 
apparent an attribute is to an observer. For example, the range of flower 
colours in a meadow is likely to be more salient to a respondent by a 
flowering meadow than to a respondent by mown amenity grass. The 
management of urban wildflower meadows may, therefore, create a 
natural contrast in salience. At annual or perennial meadows, aesthetic 
attributes such as flower colours are directly observable (Hoyle et al., 
2018; Junge et al., 2009; King et al., 2025; Lindemann-Matthies and 
Bose, 2007), whereas at sites of mown amenity grass the same attri-
butes are hypothetical to respondents. Prior work has shown that stated 
preferences are often sensitive to the information provided about the 
policy context or attributes (Needham et al., 2018; Welling et al., 2022). 
Here, we examine whether preferences also differ with the physical
environment in which the survey is conducted, by comparing re-
spondents interviewed at annual meadows, perennial meadows, and 
control sites of mown amenity grass. Understanding such salience effects 
is important for judging whether in situ surveys at existing meadows can 
inform planning at other sites.
Therefore, we posed two interlinked research questions. Firstly, we 
investigated which attributes of urban meadows people relatively 
preferred as understanding relative preferences is important for man-
agers balancing multiple objectives within budget constraints. Secondly, 
we assessed whether preferences varied by the meadow type adjacent to 
the interview location, testing for potential salience effects in in situ 
stated preference surveys. As urban meadow management practices can 
vary both within and between urban areas, we tested the differences in 
preferences across different parks in different cities. Our contributions 
are both methodological, relating to the assessment of context effects 
during in situ stated preference fieldwork, and practical, informing the 
management of urban nature-based solutions.
2. Methods
2.1. Study system
Although short, mown grassland, managed primarily for recreation, 
is the dominant form of urban greenspace in many temperate regions, it 
results in low plant diversity that does not support a diverse invertebrate 
community (Norton et al., 2019). Urban wildflower meadows (hereafter 
urban meadows) may deliver biodiversity, provisioning, and cultural 
ecosystem service provision benefits (Klaus, 2013). Urban meadows 
with diverse flowering and non-flowering plant species can increase the 
quality of urban habitats for invertebrates and pollinating species con-
servation (Hall et al., 2017). Replacing short-mown amenity grassland 
with sown urban meadows has thus become increasingly popular, both 
in the UK (e.g., Buglife, 2017; Hoyle et al., 2018; Southon et al., 2017) 
and internationally, including across continental Europe (Süle et al.; 
Zajdel et al., 2025), North America (Pham et al., 2025), and Australia 
(Horsfall et al., 2024). However, the biodiversity and amenity benefits of 
urban meadows depend in part on whether greenspace users accept or 
oppose new meadows (Nassauer et al., 2009). Evidence on which 
meadow attributes the public values most is, therefore, informative for 
meadow design and management, and may support the wider adoption 
of urban meadows.
2.2. Experimental design
To understand respondents’ relative preferences for attributes of 
urban meadows, we conducted in situ interviews at three meadow types: 
urban meadows (annual or perennial) and control sites of equally sized 
amenity grass areas. In situ surveys ensured that respondents had direct 
experience of these greenspaces. In-person face-to-face interviews may 
be an appropriate survey modality for eliciting values for environmental 
goods, given their advantages in motivating respondents and ability to 
use visual aids (Arrow et al., 1993). Although contemporary guidance 
suggests that ex situ approaches, such as online surveys, are also able to 
visually describe the survey context (Dillman et al., 2014; Johnston 
et al., 2017; Mariel et al., 2021), we chose an in situ approach to 
ensure that respondents were aware of the meadow types under study. 
Although preferences recovered through in situ surveys may be suscep-
tible to interviewer effects through social desirability bias (e.g., Leggett 
et al., 2003), this risk is mitigated when multiple well-trained in-
terviewers are used (Lindhjem and Navrud, 2011; Loosveldt, 2012). As 
such, our surveys were conducted by six trained interviewers using a 
consistent method of guiding respondents through the survey. In-
terviews were conducted during the peak flowering season (July–August 
2014), with site visits scheduled across four timeslots, weekdays, and 
weekends, as well as daytime and early evenings, to capture a broad 
range of greenspace users. Although the COVID-19 pandemic likely

By conducting interviews at three meadow types, we created varia-
tion in how directly respondents could observe the attributes being 
valued. At control sites of amenity mown grass, managed through 
intensive mowing to maintain short, uniform vegetation, respondents 
experienced only the baseline management condition: no flower colours 
beyond green grass, minimal pollinator activity, and low structural 
complexity. When presented with choice scenarios describing alterna-
tive management that would enhance flower diversity, native species 
content, or pollinator habitat, the effects of these management changes 
were hypothetical. Instead, respondents relied on our visual aids and 
written descriptions rather than direct observation of these enhanced 
attributes. Conversely, at annual and perennial meadows, the outcomes 
of alternative management were directly observable and potentially 
more salient. Prior research on hypothetical bias suggests that such 
direct exposure can enhance attribute salience, which can ultimately 
influence stated preferences (Aoki et al., 2010; Haghani et al., 2021b). 
This point is relevant to the management of urban wildflower meadows, 
because if preferences are sensitive to attribute salience, then evidence 
from in situ surveys at existing meadows may have limited general-
isability to planning decisions about other sites. We explored this by 
comparing preferences across meadow types while controlling for 
respondent characteristics.
As alterations in management practices can occur both within and 
between urban areas, we sampled respondents across 17 sites within 11 
public parks across three cities, based on sites being publicly accessible 
and, therefore, likely to receive sufficient visitors. Sampling across 
multiple parks allowed us to fully represent the diversity of preferences 
for urban meadows. Sampling across multiple cities allowed us to un-
derstand whether the effect of meadow type on preferences was robust 
to spatial variation across parks and cities (Glenk et al., 2020).
Our sampling covered three of the UK's largest cities (ONS, 2016); 
Leeds, Bristol, and Edinburgh (Fig. 1), each with parks in parts of the city 
with different levels of affluence (supplementary material: Table B.1). 
All selected sites within parks were surrounded by residential areas and 
were visited by local people. The annual meadows contained a mix of 
native and non-native wildflowers, perennial meadows contained pre-
dominantly native wildflowers, while the control areas had not been 
turned into meadows and were managed as frequently cut amenity 
grassland. We provide example pictures of all three meadow types 
(supplementary material, Figure B.1) with further details of the 
meadows described in Hicks et al. (2016). Meadows were 300 m 2 in size 
and rectangular in shape. Respondents were asked to provide verbal 
informed consent and made aware that their participation was volun-
tary, and that no compensation would be provided. All respondents were 
over 18 and we received ethical approval from the University of Kent 
before proceeding with the study.
2.3. Survey design
Stemming from Lancaster's (1966) characteristic theory of value, CEs 
assume that individuals derive utility from the attributes of a good. 
Under Random Utility Theory (McFadden, 1974), respondents are 
assumed to choose the alternative that maximizes their utility. By 
observing trade-offs between attributes, CEs can recover the marginal 
utility for changes in attributes and can express these in WTP terms 
when a monetary attribute is included (Adamowicz et al., 1998). We 
aimed to elicit WTP for attributes of urban meadows known to be 
relevant to the public, including aesthetic quality (Junge et al., 2009), 
biodiversity (Fischer et al., 2011), and pollination resources (Breeze 
et al., 2015).
Our CE (Fig. 2) presented respondents with a hypothetical scenario
in which the city council (i.e., a local level of government) proposed 
changes to the management of grassy areas to enhance aesthetic appeal, 
safeguard native plant species, and support pollinating insects. Each 
choice task had four options (A, B, C, and D), with D representing the 
status quo option through an image of amenity grass with low levels of 
all attributes. Each option was described by five attributes: one mone-
tary attribute (a form of local tax) and four non-monetary attributes. 
Given the importance of visual stimuli in the experience of green-
spaces, we expected that the aesthetics of urban meadows were an 
important attribute to respondents (Akers et al., 2012; Austen et al., 
2021; Hoyle et al., 2018). While visual appearance varies with flower 
shape, size, planting structure, coverage, and seasonality (Austen et al., 
2023; Southon et al., 2017), we focused specifically on colour diversity 
(King et al., 2025), as visitors often prefer brightly coloured flowers 
(Junge et al., 2009; Lindemann-Matthies and Bose, 2007).
Our CE attribute called ‘appearance’ allowed us to investigate pref-
erences for the range of flower colours. This attribute was depicted 
pictorially by digitally manipulating an image of a perennial meadow 
containing white flowers (Leucanthemum vulgare). The status quo level 
was amenity grass, representing low visual variety with no white 
flowers. Three additional levels were included: a meadow with only 
white flowers, a meadow with three colours (white, yellow, blue), and a 
meadow with five colours (white, yellow, blue, pink, red). These cate-
gories broadly reflected the colour diversity of established meadows, 
such as Malva moschata (pink) and Centaurea cyanus (blue). Using four 
levels facilitates an investigation into how sensitive preferences were to 
the scope of changes in the diversity and variety of flower colours (King 
et al., 2025; Veisten et al., 2004). As control sites had no additional
