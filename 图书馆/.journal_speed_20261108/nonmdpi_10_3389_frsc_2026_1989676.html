Urban lifelines and resilience beyond the Strait of Hormuz

  • AI

    Abdulaziz I. Almulhim *

  • Department of Urban and Regional Planning, College of Architecture and Planning, Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University, Dammam, Saudi Arabia

Article metrics

View details

Abstract

Cities depend on infrastructural lifelines that extend far beyond their territorial boundaries, yet urban resilience is often conceptualized through capacities located within cities themselves. This Perspective uses disruptions surrounding the Strait of Hormuz to reconsider how urban resilience is shaped by geographically dispersed infrastructure networks operating across multiple spatial and institutional scales. Drawing on emerging rerouting responses in the Gulf, particularly Saudi Arabia, the discussion traces how flows can be reconfigured across pipelines, Red Sea ports and overland logistics corridors when established maritime routes are constrained. We argue that such reconfiguration represents a potential mechanism of adaptation and should not itself be interpreted as evidence of resilience. Alternative pathways can provide additional options for maintaining essential flows, but they can also shift pressure, costs and dependencies toward other infrastructures, jurisdictions and chokepoints. Urban resilience should therefore account not only for capacities within cities, but also for the wider infrastructural connections through which essential urban functions are sustained and reorganized under disruption.

Introduction

Conflict-related disruptions to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz beginning on 28 February 2026 (Reuters, 2026a) have exposed a critical vulnerability in the infrastructural networks sustaining contemporary urban systems (Al-Sarihi, 2026). Located between the Arabian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman, the Strait is a strategically important maritime chokepoint through which a substantial share of global energy flows passes (IEA, 2026), with limited alternative routes available during periods of geopolitical disruption (Lawale and Ahmad, 2025). While much attention has focused on the economic shocks and rising costs associated with such disruptions (Allam et al., 2026), less attention has been given to what they reveal about infrastructural lifelines, understood here as the interconnected systems and networks that sustain essential urban functions, and about how these lifelines can be reconfigured when established routes are disrupted. Urban life depends on continuous and largely invisible flows of fuel, food, medicine and industrial materials, many of which move through highly concentrated maritime routes. When these routes are disrupted, the consequences are not confined to abstract markets; they can manifest in supply shortages, rising costs and operational strain within cities themselves.

The potential consequences of disrupted urban supplies raise a question of resilience: how can essential urban functions remain supported when the infrastructure networks sustaining them are disrupted? Although these networks create vulnerabilities through their interdependencies, they are not spatially fixed. Disruption can expose such vulnerabilities while prompting adaptive responses, including the redirection of flows across alternative routes. Within urban resilience frameworks, such reconfiguration can be understood as an expression of adaptive capacity (Antonenko et al., 2026), but it should not itself be equated with demonstrated resilience, which depends on whether essential urban functions remain supported (Kong et al., 2019). This perspective also reflects recognition that cities are embedded in wider infrastructural and knowledge networks extending beyond their territorial boundaries (Robin and Acuto, 2018). Using the disruptions surrounding the Strait of Hormuz as an illustrative case, this Perspective contributes to urban resilience debates by examining how the reconfiguration of distant infrastructure networks can provide adaptive options while redistributing vulnerabilities across spatial and institutional scales.

We argue that this relational dependence requires urban resilience to be considered beyond the territorial boundaries of cities themselves, understood here as their administrative and jurisdictional limits. The lifelines sustaining urban life may extend through ports, pipelines, transport corridors and logistical networks that connect urban functions to infrastructures operating at national and regional scales. Viewed from this perspective, the conflict-related disruptions affecting the Strait of Hormuz in 2026 reveal how the continuity of urban functions may depend partly on the capacity of geographically dispersed infrastructure networks to reconfigure essential flows under stress. This process of rerouting is not merely a logistical response to disruption; it reshapes the spatial organization of essential urban flows. At the same time, such reconfiguration may redistribute rather than eliminate vulnerability, creating new dependencies and risks along alternative routes. Understanding these dynamics shifts attention from whether cities simply withstand external shocks toward how the lifelines connecting cities to wider infrastructural networks are reorganized when those connections are disrupted.

As a Perspective article, this paper does not undertake a systematic empirical case-study analysis of the Strait of Hormuz. It uses the recent disruptions surrounding the Strait as a contemporary illustrative lens through which to reconsider urban resilience when the infrastructures sustaining cities extend across multiple geographical and institutional scales. The discussion draws on energy data from the IEA, contemporary reporting from sources such as Reuters and Arab News on infrastructure disruptions and rerouting responses, and relevant scholarship on urban resilience, infrastructure interdependence, and supply-chain reconfiguration. These sources are used to trace connections across urban, national, and regional scales; the article does not seek to evaluate the performance of individual cities or infrastructure systems. Geopolitical disruption is treated here as the context and source of infrastructural disturbance, while the focus remains on how such disturbance affects and reconfigures the infrastructural connections through which essential urban functions are sustained.

From vulnerability to reconfiguration

Cities are not self-sufficient entities but deeply externalized systems, sustained by distant and interconnected networks through which essential resources and services circulate (Rokem et al., 2017). In regions where domestic production is constrained, whether by environmental limitations, economic specialization or demographic pressures, urban dependence on imported goods can become particularly acute. Yet urban resilience frameworks often remain bounded by municipal jurisdictions, while the geographically dispersed systems that sustain urban life receive less attention (Huck and Monstadt, 2019). This creates an important conceptual gap: the functioning of cities may depend on infrastructures located far beyond the places in which their consequences are ultimately experienced. Urban vulnerability, therefore, cannot be understood solely through conditions within the city itself, but must also account for the external networks on which urban continuity depends.

The concentration of global flows through a limited number of chokepoints further amplifies this relational vulnerability. Narrow maritime corridors, strategic straits and major logistics hubs act as bottlenecks through which substantial quantities of goods and energy pass. While such concentration may enhance efficiency under stable conditions, it can also create systemic fragility when disruption occurs. Because infrastructure systems are interconnected and mutually interdependent, disturbances can propagate across networks and sectors rather than remaining confined to the point at which they originate (Kong et al., 2019). A disruption in one location can therefore connect distant geographies through shared exposure, affecting the wider networks through which urban functions are sustained. The vulnerability of cities is, in this sense, relational: it is produced not only by what happens within cities, but also by the configuration and interdependence of the networks connecting them to distant infrastructures and flows.

Yet relational dependence does not imply that these networks are fixed. Disruption can also bring into view the capacity of infrastructure systems to redirect and reorganize flows through alternative routes, nodes and modes. Such reconfiguration can alter existing dependencies by shifting flows between infrastructures, territories and jurisdictions. Its significance therefore lies not simply in the availability of an alternative route, but in whether that alternative can sustain essential flows and in the new dependencies or vulnerabilities it may create (Amico et al., 2024). From this perspective, infrastructure is not simply a collection of physical assets but an ongoing and relational process whose configurations can be reshaped under changing conditions (Monstadt et al., 2025).

Rerouting flows in practice

The availability of infrastructure capable of bypassing the Strait differs across Gulf states, creating uneven capacities to respond when established maritime flows are disrupted. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates possess operational pipeline infrastructure that can redirect part of their crude exports to terminals outside the Strait (IEA, 2026).

Recent developments in Saudi Arabia illustrate how infrastructural reconfiguration can unfold in practice. Saudi Aramco reportedly activated plans to ramp up operations on the East-West Pipeline on 28 February 2026. By 4 March, the pipeline was reportedly operating close to full capacity, with tankers normally bound for Arabian Gulf terminals redirected toward the Red Sea (Gornall, 2026). The pipeline was subsequently reported to be carrying around 7 million barrels per day from Saudi Arabia’s Eastern Province to Yanbu, partially offsetting flows normally passing through the Strait (Gornall, 2026). At the same time, Yanbu has gained significance as an outlet for redirected oil flows, while Red Sea ports such as Jeddah provide alternative gateways for goods moving through regional logistics networks. Overland logistics corridors connect Saudi Arabia’s western ports with neighboring Gulf countries, including the United Arab Emirates and Oman (India Shipping News, 2026). These connections illustrate how geographically dispersed infrastructures may offer alternative pathways when established flows are disrupted, without establishing that such reconfiguration constitutes resilience in itself.

These shifts are particularly consequential in a region where imported goods play an important role in sustaining everyday urban functions. Gulf countries depend substantially on food imports (El Sirgany and Robertson, 2026), while cities also rely on externally sourced medicines, industrial inputs and other essential materials. Under disruption, the question is therefore not simply whether an alternative route exists, but whether essential flows can be redirected across different infrastructures without unacceptable losses in capacity, time or accessibility. Research on supply-chain disruption shows that reroute flexibility can mitigate supply deficits and extend the time before shortages become critical, although the use of alternative paths may also increase distribution complexity and delays (Amico et al., 2024). In this sense, alternative routes should be understood as adaptive capacity rather than evidence of resilience outcomes.

Reconfiguration has also extended beyond maritime and pipeline networks to overland redistribution. Saudi Arabia, leveraging its geographic position between the Gulf and Red Sea, has facilitated cross-border road movements, with more than 94,000 outbound trucks reported during the first weeks of the disruption (El Sirgany and Robertson, 2026). As illustrated in Figure 1, Jeddah Islamic Port is one of the Saudi Red Sea gateways within the overland logistics network linking western ports to neighboring Gulf countries, including the United Arab Emirates and Oman. The operation of these corridors depends not only on physical connectivity, but also on cross-border logistical and institutional coordination. Such movements illustrate how ports, pipelines and road corridors can operate together as alternative pathways through which regional flows are reorganized. Importantly, however, the availability and reported use of these pathways do not establish that previous levels of supply or urban functioning were fully maintained. The reported movements nevertheless illustrate the infrastructural capacity to shift flows between routes and modes when established connections become constrained.

The operation of these alternative pathways depends on more than physical infrastructure alone. Redirecting goods between ports, pipelines, roads and inland destinations requires available capacity, operational flexibility, cross-border coordination and governance across multiple jurisdictions. Recent maritime-logistics research identifies route diversification and the use of alternative ports as priority strategies for strengthening supply-chain resilience to disruptions, while emphasizing that alternative routes must be assessed against geopolitical risk, transit time, cost and operational feasibility (Koray et al., 2025). The capacity to reroute urban lifelines is therefore both infrastructural and institutional: alternative connections must be available to sustain essential urban flows, while their activation requires coordination among the infrastructure operators and public authorities involved.

The risks of rerouting

Rerouting does not eliminate vulnerability; it can redistribute it. Shifting flows away from one constrained corridor may reduce dependence on that route while increasing pressure on alternative ports, pipelines, roads and maritime passages. These alternatives are not neutral substitutes. They have their own capacity limits, geopolitical exposures, security conditions and environmental constraints. The resilience value of reconfiguration therefore depends not simply on whether flows can be redirected, but on where risk moves, which infrastructures absorb additional demand, and whether new dependencies emerge in the process (Sanchez et al., 2018). Recent analysis of maritime chokepoint disruptions reinforces this point by showing that their systemic consequences depend not only on exposure to a chokepoint, but also on the availability and length of alternative routes and the delays and additional costs associated with rerouting (Verschuur et al., 2025).

The limits of reconfiguration are evident in the capacity of alternative energy routes. Although pipeline systems provide options for redirecting some crude flows away from the Strait of Hormuz, the IEA estimates that only 3.5–5.5 million barrels per day of pipeline capacity has the potential to redirect crude flows to avoid the Strait, compared with around 20 million barrels per day of seaborne oil trade normally transiting it (IEA, 2026). Alternative infrastructure can therefore provide additional routing options without fully substituting for a disrupted chokepoint.

This is particularly important when alternative pathways remain exposed to other forms of disruption. Greater reliance on Red Sea gateways, for example, may reduce exposure to the Strait of Hormuz while increasing dependence on a maritime system facing its own security and geopolitical risks. The Red Sea–Bab al-Mandab corridor has itself faced repeated attacks on commercial vessels by Houthi forces since late 2023, disrupting maritime traffic and contributing to higher insurance costs (Middle East Institute, 2024; Reuters, 2025). Overland redistribution can similarly relieve pressure on maritime routes while creating additional demands on road capacity, border procedures and cross-jurisdictional coordination. The scale of this logistical redistribution is illustrated by activity along Saudi Arabia’s western logistics corridor: Jeddah Islamic Port handled around 100,000 trucks and more than 90,000 containers within nearly a month as regional cargo flows were redirected through the Kingdom, while further increases in container arrivals through Jeddah and other Red Sea gateways were anticipated as flows shifted away from the Strait of Hormuz (Saudi Gazette, 2026). Viewed at a smaller spatial scale, Jeddah illustrates how disruption at a regional maritime chokepoint can redirect logistical activity toward alternative port and overland connections, potentially placing additional demands on these infrastructures. The consequences of rerouting are therefore differentiated across scales: while alternative corridors may provide additional options at the regional level, particular ports and transport networks may absorb greater volumes, operational pressures and coordination demands at the local level.

Rerouting can also impose substantial economic costs. Maritime chokepoint disruptions can generate losses through delays, longer routes, additional operating expenditure, insurance premiums, trade interruptions and increases in freight rates (Verschuur et al., 2025). These effects are evident in recent regional disruptions. Maersk estimated that disruptions to Middle Eastern maritime routes increased its fuel costs by approximately US$500 million per month, while several shipping companies redirected vessels around Africa or suspended selected Gulf services amid escalating regional risks (Reuters, 2026b). Route diversification itself therefore requires assessment of geopolitical risk, conflict exposure, port accessibility, operational feasibility, time and cost (Koray et al., 2025). Rerouting consequently changes the geography through which vulnerability and its associated costs are distributed instead of producing a risk-free network.

This distinction matters for how resilience is interpreted. Alternative routes may provide redundancy and adaptive options (Mitoulis et al., 2023), but their existence alone does not demonstrate that essential functions have been maintained or that systemic vulnerability has declined. Evidence on maritime chokepoints similarly indicates that the implications of disruption vary according to the feasibility and length of rerouting, with some alternative maritime routes requiring detours of 5,000 to approximately 12,000 km and a week or more of additional travel, potentially increasing costs and inventory pressures (Verschuur et al., 2025). The planning challenge is therefore not to replace one optimized corridor with another, but to create multiple viable pathways across regional infrastructure networks while recognizing the trade-offs and new vulnerabilities that each configuration produces.

Discussion: Reframing urban resilience

The implications extend beyond the immediate management of disruption. If essential urban functions depend on infrastructures and flows extending far beyond municipal boundaries, then urban resilience cannot be conceptualized solely through assets and capacities located within the city. It should also account for the relational lifelines through which cities remain connected to energy, food, medicines and material flows across wider infrastructural networks. This interpretation is consistent with research emphasizing that urban resilience requires attention to interdependence with external partners, cross-boundary coordination and the multiple spatial scales through which resilience is implemented (Coaffee et al., 2018; Monstadt and Schmidt, 2019). This does not mean treating ports, pipelines or international corridors as urban infrastructure simply because cities depend on them. It means recognizing that the continuity of essential urban functions depends partly on infrastructures operating across multiple spatial and institutional scales. Such continuity, however, should not be equated with broader systemic resilience when it is achieved by transferring costs or vulnerabilities to other territories (Atteridge and Remling, 2018).

Such a perspective shifts the planning question from where urban infrastructure is located to how urban functions remain connected under disruption. For planners, this broadens attention toward the dependencies linking cities to ports, transport corridors, energy networks and logistics systems beyond their jurisdictions, even where local authorities have limited capacity to govern these interdependencies directly (Monstadt and Schmidt, 2019). This makes diversification, multimodal connectivity and coordination across jurisdictions relevant to urban resilience even when the infrastructures involved are geographically distant from the cities they sustain.

Viewed across multiple spatial scales, these relationships connect disruptions and responses operating at different levels. A constraint at a regional maritime chokepoint can alter flows through pipelines, ports and transport corridors operating at national and regional scales, with potential consequences for the availability, timing and cost of essential resources on which urban functions depend. Rerouting can therefore reshape urban dependencies without occurring within the city itself, as capacities and responses at regional and national scales reconfigure the connections through which cities are supplied.

This relational perspective also complicates the conventional pursuit of efficiency. Concentrating flows through highly optimized routes can reduce costs and travel times under stable conditions while simultaneously limiting alternatives when those routes fail. Evidence from transportation networks demonstrates that efficiency under normal conditions does not necessarily imply resilience under disruption (Ganin et al., 2017). Redundancy, diversity and distributedness are likewise recognized as important principles for critical-infrastructure resilience (Trump et al., 2025). Planning for urban resilience may therefore require treating redundancy not simply as inefficiency, but as a means of retaining options when established connections are disrupted.

The disruptions surrounding the Strait of Hormuz make this relational condition visible. Their significance for urban planning lies not in demonstrating that rerouting has made Gulf cities resilient, but in revealing how disturbances at a geographically distant chokepoint can expose dependencies embedded in everyday urban functioning and how responses can reorganize those dependencies across other infrastructures and territories, subject to geopolitical constraints and cross-border relations. In the case examined here, dependence on maritime passage through Hormuz can be partially reconfigured through nationally managed pipeline capacity, Red Sea ports and overland logistics corridors. Yet these alternatives do not remove dependency; they can shift it toward other infrastructures, jurisdictions and chokepoints, each with its own capacity constraints and exposure to disruption. Hormuz is therefore useful not as proof of resilience, but as a lens through which to examine where urban lifelines extend, how they can be reconfigured, and how vulnerability may shift when they are disrupted.

Future research should examine when and under what conditions the reconfiguration of distant infrastructure networks maintains essential urban functions, and when it instead redistributes vulnerability across places, sectors and populations. Such work could help distinguish the availability of alternative routes from demonstrated resilience outcomes and clarify how these relationships operate across urban, national and regional scales. From this perspective, the central lesson is not that cities can overcome geopolitical disruption simply by rerouting flows. Urban resilience must instead be understood partly through the capacity to maintain essential connections while avoiding the reproduction of concentrated dependencies elsewhere. For planning, this means looking beyond the territorial city toward the networks that sustain it, while remaining attentive to the new risks, trade-offs and uneven consequences that reconfiguration may create. Urban resilience, in this sense, is not defined by the absence of disruption, but by the capacity to sustain essential urban functions through changing configurations of interconnected lifelines.
