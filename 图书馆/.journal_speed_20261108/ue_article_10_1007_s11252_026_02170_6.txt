Grubesic 1 Abstract Urban rat infestations represent a persistent public health challenge, yet municipal pest management programs often operate reactively and with limited resources. This study applies a suite of spatiotemporal statistical approaches (geographically weighted regression, spatial lag modeling, Random Forest, and Extreme Gradient Boosting) to predict the spatial distribution of rat sightings across Chicago, Illinois, over a fifteen-year observation window (2011–2025). Using 311 service request data linked to a hexagonal grid (150 m cells) and a set of environmental, demographic, and property-value predictors, we assess the individual and ensemble performance of these models on held-out 2025 rat-sighting data. Our comparative analysis demonstrates that the machine learning frameworks and the spatial lag model generally outperformed geographically weighted regression. Specifically, Random Forest, the spatial lag model, and XGBoost each explained approximately 72% of the variance in rat sighting counts, while the ensemble model achieved the highest overall R 2 (0.73). Variable importance analysis identified recent historical sighting counts as the dominant positive predictor, followed by food establishment density and vacant property rates (both positively associated), and median household income and property values (negatively associated). Spatial hotspot analysis revealed that persistent rat activity hotspots were concentrated in high-density mixed-use corridors characterized by dense commercial food clusters, connected public alleyway infrastructure, and older residential housing stock with elevated vacancy rates. These findings suggest that proactive, data-driven approaches can identify areas in need of resource targeting to create more efficient interventions for cities facing chronic urban rodent challenges.
