School of Economics and Management, Jiangxi Agricultural University, Nanchang, Jiangxi, China Article metrics View details Abstract Promoting rural industrial integration with leading agricultural enterprises as the primary driver is one of the key pathways for advancing agricultural green transformation, and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises serves as an important indicator for evaluating the role and effectiveness of this pathway. However, research examining the association between rural industrial integration and green production among leading agricultural enterprises remains limited. Using panel data from 711 leading agricultural enterprises in Jiangxi Province from 2021 to 2023, this study empirically examines the association between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises. To address potential endogeneity and model specification concerns, a Mundlak/CRE model is employed, which decomposes the association into within-firm and between-firm effects. The findings reveal that the between-firm effect of rural industrial integration is significantly positive, while the within-firm effect is not statistically significant, indicating that firms with higher levels of industrial integration tend to have larger green production scale, whereas within the same firm, changes in industrial integration are not significantly associated with changes in green production scale. This conclusion remains robust after a series of checks, including sample transformation, winsorization, and lagged one-period regression. Boundary condition analysis indicates that firm profitability and debt level do not constitute significant boundary conditions, whereas provincial capital status appears to moderate the between-firm association, suggesting that the between-firm positive association may be stronger among firms in provincial capital cities. Accordingly, three policy recommendations are proposed: actively promoting rural industrial integration; guiding leading agricultural enterprises to expand green production scale alongside integration; and considering regional institutional differences when designing industrial integration policies. 1 Introduction Rural industrial integration is a key strategy for promoting agricultural green transformation and sustainable development, and leading agricultural enterprises are key actors in implementing rural industrial integration. In 2026, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of China issued the Measures for the Identification and Operational Monitoring of National Key Leading Enterprises in Agricultural Industrialization, which added green, ecological, and low-carbon operational monitoring indicators for leading agricultural enterprises, requiring them to simultaneously achieve resource conservation, environmental friendliness, and ecological value enhancement while advancing rural industrial integration. Therefore, examining the relationship between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises is of great significance. Currently, the ecological effects of rural industrial integration have received extensive attention in the academic community, and related studies can be broadly divided into macro and micro levels. At the macro level, early studies generally suggest that rural industrial integration positively promotes agricultural green production and the ecological environment. Tian et al. (2024) confirmed, using county-level data from 579 counties in the Yangtze River Economic Belt, that rural industrial integration significantly promotes agricultural green development with a “leapfrog” growth pattern; Gu et al. (2022) found a coupling coordination relationship between rural industrial integration and the ecological environment in China, with the coupling coordination degree showing a fluctuating upward trend; Luo and Wei (2022), from a “scale–capital–technology” framework, found that industrial integration promotes agricultural scale operation, enhances rural human capital, and advances technological progress, thereby reducing the intensity of polluting production behaviors such as chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and agricultural films. In terms of pathways, rural industrial integration significantly inhibits agricultural carbon emissions by promoting land scale operation and agricultural technological progress (Xiong et al., 2025), improves agricultural environmental total factor productivity (Zheng et al., 2023); through multifunctional expansion, it also promotes agricultural environmental sustainability (Chen et al., 2020); moreover, digital village development strengthens the promoting effect of industrial integration on agricultural green total factor productivity (Zeng et al., 2024). However, some scholars have found from the carbon emission perspective that the relationship between industrial integration and agricultural green production may be nonlinear. Lu et al. (2025) revealed an inverted U-shaped relationship between rural industrial integration and agricultural carbon emissions, with agricultural technological progress as a mediator and environmental regulation and population aging as positive and negative moderators, respectively. Yi et al. (2024) also found an inverted U-shaped relationship between agricultural industrialization and agricultural carbon emissions, with the digital economy and human capital promoting carbon reduction after crossing the threshold. Feng et al. (2025) found that agricultural carbon emission efficiency exhibits a three-stage trajectory of “rise, decline, and rise again” with rural digital economy development, in which industrial integration plays a key mediating role. These studies extend the ecological effects of rural industrial integration from linear to nonlinear, revealing the complexity of the relationship. From the theoretical logic of the above studies, rural industrial integration may provide conditions for firms to expand green production scale through mechanisms such as resource integration, technology sharing, and economies of scale. However, existing studies are mainly based on macro-regional data, making it difficult to reveal firm-level micro mechanisms; meanwhile, the existence of nonlinear relationships also suggests that the green effect of industrial integration may vary across data levels, variable measurements, and research contexts, awaiting further examination at the micro firm level. In recent years, some scholars have begun to move the level of analysis down to micro-level subjects. He et al. (2026), using panel data from the China Land Economic Survey for 2020–2022, examined the impact of rural industrial integration on green production at the farmer level and found that rural industrial integration significantly promotes farmers’ adoption of green production, with internal integration, multifunctional expansion, and technological penetration having significant promoting effects on farmers’ green production, while the effect of industrial chain extension is not significant. This study provides micro-level evidence for understanding the environmental benefits of rural industrial integration. However, farmers and agricultural enterprises differ fundamentally in resource endowments, decision-making logic, and green production behavior: farmers rely more on family labor and self-owned land, while leading agricultural enterprises involve more complex industrial chain integration and capital investment. Therefore, findings at the farmer level may not necessarily apply to the firm level, and the key indicator of firm green production scale remains to be further examined. As important actors in rural industrial integration, leading agricultural enterprises’ green production behavior not only directly affects their own environmental performance but may also exert demonstration effects on upstream and downstream farmers and cooperatives through industrial chain transmission. To further understand the association between industrial integration and corporate green production, it is necessary to first clarify the drivers of corporate green production. First, at the strategic level, green strategic orientation is an important driver of corporate green production. Zhu (2018) found that green entrepreneurial orientation of leading agricultural enterprises helps improve growth performance, competitive performance, and financial performance; Zhang and Li (2021) further confirmed that green entrepreneurial orientation not only positively affects financial performance but also helps improve environmental performance; Zhang et al. (2023) found that green entrepreneurial orientation and green market orientation can promote financial and growth performance through green production synergy. Second, at the management level, enhancing ecological innovation capability is an important driver of corporate green production. Sołoducho-Pelc and Sulich (2026) found a close relationship between sustainable strategic management, eco-innovation, and business performance; Oduro (2024) found that SME eco-innovation positively affects sustainable performance; Wu et al. (2026) found that environmental regulation can enhance firm performance through eco-innovation; Khoshnaw et al. (2024) found that corporate social responsibility can also affect environmental performance through environmental strategy and green innovation. In addition, Mohajan and Zhao (2026) and Bag et al. (2022) found that cultivating and enhancing corporate ecological innovation capability is an important pathway to improve firm performance and achieve circular economy and sustainable development. Finally, at the practical level, green technology innovation is an important driver of corporate green production. Tao et al. (2025) confirmed that green technology innovation is an important variable moderating the relationship between ESG performance and firm performance; Deng et al. (2025) confirmed that green technology innovation positively affects financial and social performance; Lu et al. (2026) found that digital capability reduces fertilizer use through two pathways: supporting the adoption of green production concepts and encouraging farmers to use green production technologies; Cao et al. (2026) found that food safety certification, planting technology training, and lower perceived price volatility are important pathways through which enterprises promote farmers’ green production behavior. These studies have focused on the driving effects of corporate green strategy, ecological innovation capability, and green technology innovation on firm performance, but have paid less attention to the role of industrial integration as an external contextual variable and to “green production scale” as a direct green production outcome, and therefore cannot directly reflect firms’ green production levels and the actual effectiveness of agricultural green transformation. In summary, existing research has the following limitations. First, studies on the ecological effects of rural industrial integration are mostly conducted at the macro level, making it difficult to reveal firm-level micro mechanisms. Second, existing studies mostly examine the impact of industrial integration on green production from the farmer perspective, but their analytical unit is farmers rather than agricultural enterprises. Third, studies on the drivers of green production in agricultural enterprises mostly focus on internal factors such as green entrepreneurial orientation, ecological innovation capability, and green technology innovation, overlooking the role of industrial integration as an external contextual variable and the analysis of green production scale. To address these gaps, this study moves the level of analysis down to agricultural enterprises, using panel data from 711 leading agricultural enterprises in Jiangxi Province from 2021 to 2023 to examine the association between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises, and further explores the boundary conditions of firm profitability, debt level, and location, aiming to understand the association between industrial integration and green production scale and to provide micro-level firm evidence for the scientific formulation of industrial integration policies. 2 Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses 2.1 Association between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises Based on the resource-based view, the value, rarity, inimitability, and non-substitutability of a firm’s resources are the sources of its competitive advantage (Barney, 1991). In the process of rural industrial integration, leading agricultural enterprises can break through their own resource constraints and obtain more strategic resources from outside, thereby providing conditions for expanding green production scale. First, industrial integration builds a collaborative network among leading agricultural enterprises and the government, research institutes, cooperatives, and farmers, enabling access to key resources such as agricultural subsidies, green production technologies, and specialized farmers (Chen and Li, 2025). Second, industrial integration promotes industrial chain integration and agricultural scale operation, enabling firms to achieve economies of scale in green technology adoption, reduce the environmental governance cost per unit of output, and thereby help improve agricultural environmental total factor productivity (Zheng et al., 2023). Third, rural industrial integration is accompanied by the expansion of the green agricultural product consumer market, green agricultural product sales premiums, and brand value enhancement, further incentivizing agricultural enterprises to expand green production scale and promoting the improvement of green production levels (He et al., 2026). Fourth, through technological penetration and interest linkage mechanisms, industrial integration promotes the diffusion of green production technologies and standards along the industrial chain, driving more farmers and cooperatives to participate in green production, thereby expanding the overall scale of firms’ green production (Lu et al., 2024). Based on the above analysis, rural industrial integration may be positively associated with the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises through multiple mechanisms such as resource acquisition, economies of scale, market incentives, and technology diffusion. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H1: There is a significant positive association between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises; that is, as the degree of rural industrial integration increases, the green production scale of enterprises tends to expand. 2.2 Boundary condition analysis of the association between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises The positive association between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises may not be uniform, and differences in firm profitability and debt level may lead to variations in the strength of this association. From the perspective of resource constraints, financing constraints inhibit firms’ market performance and are detrimental to long-term sustainable development (Wu et al., 2026). Firms with lower profitability face stronger financial constraints, making it difficult to balance production inputs and green production inputs; their ability and willingness to participate in industrial integration may be limited by resource constraints, resulting in a weaker positive association between industrial integration and green production scale. In contrast, firms with higher profitability have relatively sufficient financial resources and can allocate more to green production while maintaining production inputs, thereby being more capable of expanding green production scale through industrial integration, making the positive association more pronounced. From the perspective of risk-bearing capacity, highly indebted firms have lower risk tolerance and face stronger monitoring and intervention from creditors; they prioritize cash flow and debt-servicing capacity in resource allocation, which may compress green production inputs, thus weakening the positive association between industrial integration and green production scale in highly indebted firms (Ren and Wang, 2025). In contrast, low-debt agricultural firms face weaker credit constraints and greater financial flexibility, and have stronger autonomy in adopting green production technologies and maintaining a certain proportion of green production inputs during industrial integration, making the positive association more pronounced among low-debt firms. From the perspective of regional institutional environment, provincial capital cities typically enjoy advantages in policy support, resource agglomeration, market conditions, and infrastructure, enabling firms to more easily access green production technologies, capital, and market opportunities (Cao and Ren, 2026). Therefore, the positive association between industrial integration and green production scale may be stronger among firms in provincial capital cities, while firms in non-capital cities may face resource and institutional constraints that limit their ability to expand green production scale through industrial integration. Based on the above analysis, the positive association between rural industrial integration and green production scale may differ across leading agricultural enterprises with different profitability levels, debt levels, and locations. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H2: Different levels of profitability, debt, and provincial capital status may constitute boundary conditions of the positive association between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises. 2.3 Theoretical framework Based on the above theoretical analysis, this study constructs a theoretical framework for the association between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises, as shown in Figure 1. The framework consists of three core elements: first, the four dimensions of rural industrial integration, namely, industrial chain extension, multifunctional expansion, technological penetration, and interest linkage; second, the resource-based view and transaction cost theory, which provide the theoretical foundation for understanding the association between rural industrial integration and green production scale; and third, boundary condition exploration, which examines whether firm profitability, debt level, and provincial capital status moderate the positive association between the two. The framework suggests that rural industrial integration may be positively associated with green production scale through multiple mechanisms such as resource acquisition, economies of scale, market incentives, and technology diffusion, while whether this association differs across firm characteristics and regional conditions remains to be empirically tested. Since this monitoring data became relatively complete only after 2021, the period 2021–2023 represents the longest continuous panel data currently available. The primary research subjects of this study are “leading agricultural enterprises,” specifically referring to enterprises whose main production and operation activities are rooted in rural areas, involving multiple stages such as crop cultivation, processing, storage, transportation, and sales. Although some enterprises have their headquarters in cities, their production and operation sites are mainly located in rural areas, and the related crop types cover a variety of agricultural products including grains, fruits, vegetables, and livestock. Some agricultural enterprises’ planting bases have obtained green agricultural product certification, and the monitoring data include the area of green certification obtained by leading agricultural enterprises, which can reasonably reflect the green production scale of each leading agricultural enterprise. The monitoring data also cover core indicators such as rural industrial integration practices, corporate financial conditions, and quality management levels, which are well aligned with the theme and objectives of this study. To ensure data scientific rigor and standardization, this study excluded enterprises that were not continuously monitored for 3 years, ultimately obtaining a valid sample of 711 enterprises with a total of 2,133 observations. 3.2 Variable descriptions 3.2.1 Dependent variable The dependent variable in this study is the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises. Following Zhang et al. (2025), we adopt the “planting area of green food and organic agricultural products” as a proxy for the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises, in order to reflect the actual scale and level of green production of leading agricultural enterprises. The data are obtained from the monitoring database of leading agricultural enterprises maintained by the Jiangxi Provincial Department of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, which records the certified green and organic planting area of each monitored enterprise. 3.2.2 Core independent variable The core independent variable in this study is rural industrial integration. Following the studies of Sun et al. (2024) and Zhou and Zhu (2026), we construct a measurement index for rural industrial integration that includes four core dimensions: industrial chain extension, multifunctional expansion, technological penetration, and interest linkage. The composite score of the four dimensions is used as a proxy for the level of industrial integration. (2024), and Li et al. (2022) in selecting firm size, ownership type, firm age, total planting area, and local economic development level as control variables. Firm size is measured by the number of employees; ownership type is measured by a 0–1 dummy variable (0 = state-owned, 1 = private); firm age is measured as the difference between the monitoring year and the registration year; total planting area is measured as the sum of self-owned base area and contracted base area; and local economic development level is measured by the GDP of the city where the firm is located. These variables are closely related to the resource acquisition capacity, production efficiency, market transaction efficiency, and technological innovation capability of leading agricultural enterprises, and may affect firms’ green production capacity and level; therefore, they need to be controlled for. 3.2.4 Boundary condition variables Following Wang et al. (2025), Zhou (2026), and Dai and Zhang (2023), this study selects firm profitability, debt level, and provincial capital status as boundary condition variables. The rationale for this selection is as follows: firm profitability reflects the capacity for resource acquisition and reinvestment; debt level is related to financing constraints and risk-bearing capacity; and provincial capital status reflects systematic differences in institutional environment and resource endowments. These three variables influence firms’ willingness and ability to invest in green production technologies and equipment during industrial integration from the perspectives of firm resource capacity, financial constraints, and regional institutional environment, respectively. In terms of variable measurement, firm profitability is measured by the sales profit margin, debt level is measured by the debt-to-asset ratio, and provincial capital status is measured by a dummy variable indicating whether the firm is located in a provincial capital city (1 = provincial capital, 0 = non-provincial capital). This study mainly examines whether these three variables constitute boundary conditions of the positive association between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises. 3.3 Model specification This study adopts the Mundlak/CRE model as the baseline specification. This model adds the group means of the explanatory variables to the random-effects model, allowing it to control for the correlation between individual effects and explanatory variables while simultaneously utilizing both within-firm and between-firm variation and decomposing between-firm and within-firm effects (Mundlak, 1978; Schunck, 2013); compared with the fixed-effects model, it not only controls for individual effects but also reports between-firm effects, which is more consistent with the research question of this study focusing on between-firm associations. In the model specification diagnostics, the Hausman test yields χ2 = 62.67, p = 0.0000, suggesting a possible systematic correlation between individual effects and explanatory variables and theoretically favoring the fixed-effects model. However, the data in this study constitute a short-and-wide panel (2021–2023, 711 firms), and the fixed-effects model may fail to effectively identify the core effect due to limited within-firm variation; meanwhile, the fixed-effects model absorbs all time-invariant firm characteristics and cannot report between-firm effects. Considering the suitability of the model specification and its alignment with the research question, this study adopts the Mundlak/CRE model as the baseline specification. This model is methodologically equivalent to the fixed-effects model but can report both within-firm and between-firm effects. The coefficient of RII captures the within-firm effect, while the coefficient of the group mean of RII captures the between-firm effect. Standard errors are clustered at the firm level. In addition, year fixed effects are included to control for common time trends. To mitigate the influence of extreme values, the dependent variable, green production scale, along with the control variables firm size and total planting area, and the boundary condition variable firm profitability, were winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles. To address right-skewed distributions, the natural logarithm was taken after adding 1 to the winsorized green production scale, firm size, and total planting area. Regional economic development level was scaled by dividing GDP by 1000. Provincial capital status was measured by a dummy variable indicating whether the firm is located in Nanchang (1 = Nanchang, 0 = otherwise). Firm debt level and firm age were kept in their original form. The descriptive statistics of the main variables are reported in Table 4. The dependent variable, green production scale, has a mean of 4.283 and a standard deviation of 3.659, with a minimum of 0 and a maximum of 10.607, indicating substantial variation in green production scale across sample firms and providing sufficient variation for examining the green effect of industrial integration. The core independent variable, rural industrial integration, has a mean of 0.197 and a standard deviation of 0.135, ranging from 0 to 0.654, suggesting that the overall level of industrial integration among sample firms is relatively low with notable inter-firm variation; this distribution is conducive to identifying the marginal effect of integration intensity on green production. In addition, the control variables and boundary condition variables show no obvious outliers, and their overall distributions are reasonable, providing a solid data foundation for subsequent regression analysis. Specifically, the between-firm effect is significantly positive, while the within-firm effect is not statistically significant. This positive between-firm association remains robust after sample transformation and winsorization. This study extends the analytical perspective from economic performance to green production, examining the association between industrial integration and green production. This finding is logically consistent with macro-level conclusions regarding the positive relationship between industrial integration and agricultural green development (Tian et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2022), offering micro-level firm insights that complement related macro-level studies. Moreover, unlike some studies that have found an inverted U-shaped relationship between industrial integration and carbon emissions (Lu et al., 2025; Yi et al., 2024), this study does not find a significant nonlinear relationship between industrial integration and green production scale, which may be related to differences in data level, variable measurement, and research context. The finding that the positive association appears to be primarily driven by between-firm variation suggests that firms with higher levels of industrial integration tend to have larger green production scale, while within the same firm, changes in industrial integration do not appear to be significantly associated with changes in green production scale. These findings provide a new reference for understanding the association between leading agricultural enterprises’ participation in industrial integration and the expansion of green production scale. 5.2 Discussion of boundary condition tests The boundary condition tests in this study show that firm profitability and debt level do not constitute significant boundary conditions of the positive association between rural industrial integration and green production scale, whereas provincial capital status appears to moderate the between-firm effect in this sample. This result partially differs from some studies that have found heterogeneous effects of firm characteristics on eco-innovation performance (Oduro, 2024; Mohajan and Zhao, 2026). The non-significant results for profitability and debt level may be attributed to several reasons. First, at the level of variable measurement, sales profit margin and debt-to-asset ratio, though widely used in the literature (Wu et al., 2026; Ren and Wang, 2025), may not fully capture the complexity of firm resource capacity and financial constraints. Second, at the level of sample characteristics, the sample consists of leading agricultural enterprises in Jiangxi Province, where inter-firm differences in resource endowments and institutional environment may be relatively limited. Third, at the level of statistical power, interaction term tests require relatively large sample sizes and high statistical power, and small moderating effects may be difficult to detect. In contrast, provincial capital status appears to positively moderate the between-firm effect, suggesting that the between-firm positive association may be stronger among firms in provincial capital cities. This may partly reflect the more developed institutional environment, resource agglomeration, and market conditions in provincial capital cities. Finally, other key boundary conditions affecting this association may reside in additional dimensions, such as firm strategic orientation, managerial environmental awareness, or industry characteristics, which await further investigation (Wang et al., 2025; Zhou, 2026). Overall, while profitability and debt level do not exhibit significant moderating effects, the potential moderating effect of provincial capital status suggests that the between-firm positive association may be contingent upon regional institutional environment, providing preliminary evidence for understanding the applicability of this association. 5.3 Limitations and future directions Based on the above analysis, this study identifies a positive between-firm association between the degree of leading agricultural enterprises’ participation in rural industrial integration and their green production scale, and finds suggestive evidence that provincial capital status may moderate this between-firm association, offering some reference for expanding the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises. Nevertheless, several limitations remain. First, the sample is limited to Jiangxi Province, and caution is needed when generalizing the findings to other regions. Second, the time span of the data is limited (2021–2023), and the long-term dynamic features of this association cannot be examined. Third, the within-firm effect is not statistically significant, which may be related to the limited within-firm variation in rural industrial integration over the short panel period. Therefore, the positive association identified in this study is primarily driven by between-firm variation, and future research with longer panels could further examine whether a within-firm association exists. Fourth, this study primarily examines the association between industrial integration and green production scale, without conducting strict causal identification or exploring potential mediating pathways. In addition, the exploration of boundary conditions remains preliminary. Future research could expand the sample scope, employ longer time-series data, adopt stronger identification strategies, and further explore potential mediating mechanisms and additional boundary conditions (He et al., 2026; Cao et al., 2026), so as to more comprehensively reveal the relationship between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises. 6 Conclusion and policy implications 6.1 Research conclusions Based on panel data from 711 leading agricultural enterprises in Jiangxi Province from 2021 to 2023, this study empirically examines the association between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises, drawing on the resource-based view and transaction cost theory, and further explores the boundary conditions of firm profitability, debt level, and provincial capital status. The main conclusions are as follows. 6.1.1 Association between rural industrial integration and green production scale of agricultural enterprises The baseline regression results suggest that the between-firm effect of rural industrial integration is significantly positive, while the within-firm effect is not statistically significant. Specifically, the between-firm effect is significantly positive (β = 5.544, p < 0.01), indicating that firms with higher levels of industrial integration tend to have larger green production scale. The within-firm effect is positive but not statistically significant (β = 0.325, p = 0.537), suggesting that, within the same firm, changes in industrial integration do not appear to be significantly associated with changes in green production scale. This conclusion remains robust after sample transformation (excluding 2021), 5% winsorization, and the lagged one-period specification. In the lagged specification, the between-firm effect remains significantly positive (β = 6.206, p < 0.01), while the within-firm effect remains non-significant (β = −0.568, p = 0.659), largely consistent with the baseline results in both direction and magnitude. These results suggest that the positive association between rural industrial integration and green production scale appears to be primarily driven by between-firm variation, providing suggestive evidence that this association may be reasonably robust after partially accounting for the potential influence of reverse causality. 6.1.2 Positive association between rural industrial integration and green production scale is reasonably robust The boundary condition tests indicate that firm profitability and debt level do not constitute significant boundary conditions of the positive association between rural industrial integration and green production scale, whereas provincial capital status appears to moderate the between-firm effect in this sample. Specifically, the interaction between provincial capital status and the between-firm effect is significantly positive (β = 5.010, p < 0.01), suggesting that the between-firm positive association may be stronger among firms in provincial capital cities. In contrast, the interactions between profitability and debt level with both the within-firm and between-firm effects are not statistically significant. Overall, the positive association between rural industrial integration and green production scale does not appear to differ significantly across firms with different profitability levels or debt levels; however, it may be contingent upon regional institutional environment. Admittedly, non-significant interaction terms do not necessarily imply the absence of moderating effects; they may also be related to variable measurement, sample characteristics, or statistical power. Therefore, the boundary condition analysis should be regarded as exploratory, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. 6.2 Policy implications As this study is primarily based on leading agricultural enterprises in Jiangxi Province, the policy implications are mainly applicable to regions with agricultural and institutional conditions similar to those of Jiangxi Province. Accordingly, three policy recommendations are proposed. 6.2.1 Actively promoting rural industrial integration This study provides suggestive evidence of a positive between-firm association between rural industrial integration and the green production scale of leading agricultural enterprises. This finding may imply that encouraging and guiding leading agricultural enterprises to participate in industrial integration could potentially help expand green production scale. Policymakers could consider reducing the cost of integration through special funds, tax incentives, and credit support, thereby incentivizing more firms to participate in industrial integration through industrial chain extension, technological penetration, and interest linkage. However, given the associational nature of this study, these implications should be interpreted as suggestive rather than prescriptive. 6.2.2 Guiding firms to expand green production scale alongside integration The positive between-firm association identified in this study may also provide a reference for policies related to green production. Specifically, changes in green production scale could be incorporated into the evaluation and support of industrial integration projects, with priority support potentially given to firms that expand green production scale alongside integration. Green production standards could be integrated into industrial chain extension, multifunctional expansion, interest linkage, and technological penetration. Through e-commerce platforms and contract farming, green production requirements could be transmitted upstream, potentially encouraging more farmers to participate in green production. However, these policy suggestions should be understood as tentative and subject to further empirical validation. 6.2.3 Consideration of regional context Given that provincial capital status appears to moderate the between-firm association in this sample, policymakers may consider paying attention to regional institutional differences when designing industrial integration policies. Firms in provincial capital cities may benefit from more developed institutional environments, resource agglomeration, and market conditions. For firms in non-capital cities, complementary support mechanisms, such as improving integration infrastructure and providing technical support and market information services, could be considered. However, given the exploratory nature of the boundary condition analysis, these suggestions should be interpreted with caution.
