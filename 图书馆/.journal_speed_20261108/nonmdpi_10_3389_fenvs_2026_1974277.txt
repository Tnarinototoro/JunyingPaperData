Environmental Economics and Management Volume 14 - 2026 | https://doi.org/10.3389/fenvs.2026.1974277 Does the carbon emission trading scheme enhance enterprises’ green total factor productivity? Evidence from China DZ Deliang Zhou * SW Sutian Wen DZ Dingyin Zhang School of Accounting, Lanzhou University of Finance and Economics, Lanzhou, China Article metrics View details Abstract With growing global attention to climate change and sustainable development, the green and low-carbon transition has become a key pathway to advancing high-quality economic development. However, whether the carbon emission trading scheme can enhance enterprises’ green total factor productivity while simultaneously achieving emission reductions still requires further investigation. Utilizing relevant data of Chinese A-share listed companies spanning from 2011 to 2023, the present study conceptualizes the carbon emission trading scheme as a quasi-natural experiment and establishes a Difference-in-Differences (DID) model to scrutinize its impact on enterprises’ green total factor productivity (GTFP). The findings reveal that the carbon emission trading scheme notably elevates enterprises’ GTFP, with the outcomes retaining robustness even after addressing endogeneity concerns and conducting rigorous robustness checks. Mechanism tests elucidate that the carbon emission trading scheme primarily propels enterprises’ GTFP by alleviating financing constraints, curtailing agency costs, and promoting digital transformation. Moderating effect tests demonstrate that market competition plays a pivotal role in amplifying the positive influence of the carbon emission trading scheme on enterprises’ GTFP. Heterogeneity analysis suggests that the policy effects of the trading scheme are more noticeable among non-state-owned enterprises, pollution-intensive enterprises, and enterprises situated in the eastern region. Furthermore, an examination of economic consequences unveils that the carbon emission trading scheme not only elevates their GTFP but also fosters their high-quality development. These research outcomes not only diversify the theoretical literature concerning the trading scheme and its economic consequences but also furnish vital theoretical guidance and policy recommendations for refining the carbon emission trading scheme, advancing the construction of the trading market, and bolstering enterprises’ GTFP. 1 Introduction Promoting green and low-carbon economic and social development is a key component of high-quality development. Green enterprise development requires both reductions in energy consumption and pollutant emissions and improvements in production efficiency. Green total factor productivity (GTFP) incorporates energy consumption and pollutant output into productivity assessment, reflecting both the economic and environmental performance of enterprises. Carbon emission management is an important part of green enterprise development, and the carbon emission trading scheme is a major policy instrument for carbon emission management in China. Whether this scheme can improve enterprises’ GTFP while encouraging enterprises to reduce emissions, and through which channels this effect operates, warrant further investigation. Therefore, examining the impact of the carbon emission trading scheme on enterprises’ GTFP has important theoretical and practical implications. As global climate change attracts increasing attention, scholars have extensively investigated the determinants of carbon emissions and measures for managing them. The existing literature primarily addresses the following areas. First, energy consumption and technological innovation. Non-renewable energy consumption increases carbon emissions in India in the long run (Subhan et al., 2023), whereas increased renewable energy consumption reduces carbon emission intensity (Alam et al., 2026a). Technological innovation can mitigate the pressure on carbon emissions associated with economic growth (Alam et al., 2026b). Second, financial development. Financial development significantly increases carbon emissions in India in the long run (Subhan et al., 2023), but green finance helps reduce emissions. Green bond issuance can send a credible signal of an enterprise’s environmental commitment to the market. As this commitment is fulfilled, the enterprise reduces its carbon dioxide emissions, obtains higher environmental ratings, and becomes more attractive to green investors (Flammer, 2021). Third, institutional quality. Institutional quality in China is negatively associated with carbon emission intensity in the long run (Alam et al., 2026a). High-quality institutions can facilitate the transfer and diffusion of environmentally friendly technologies through foreign direct investment, reducing carbon emissions in host countries (Boateng et al., 2024). Higher institutional quality also mitigates the adverse impact of carbon emissions on economic growth in the BRICS countries (Alam et al., 2026c). Scholars have also examined specific policies to assess their effects on carbon emission management. In China, the carbon emission trading scheme reduces energy consumption and carbon emissions in regulated industries within pilot regions (Hu et al., 2020). It also improves energy use efficiency among listed companies in pilot regions (Liu et al., 2025). These findings indicate that the carbon emission trading scheme contributes both to emission reduction and to improved energy use efficiency. Building on these findings, whether enterprises can improve production efficiency while reducing their environmental burden requires further discussion. Research on enterprises’ GTFP and its drivers provides a framework for examining this question. Drawing from existing literature, scholars have primarily investigated the determinants of enterprises’ GTFP from the following perspectives: First, regarding the digital economy, relevant studies indicate that the economy facilitates the integration of digital technologies with traditional industries, diminishes reliance on tangible resources, and fosters the transition and progress of traditional industries, thereby elevating enterprises’ GTFP (Liu et al., 2022; Lyu et al., 2024). Moreover, a number of scholars suggest that the development of the digital economy can mitigate information imbalance, facilitate greater factor mobility, and optimize factor allocation, thereby improving enterprises’ GTFP (Goldfarb and Tucker, 2019; Lyu et al., 2023). Second, regarding foreign investment, the influx of foreign investment can drive industrial investment and technology transfer, potentially exerting a positive influence on enterprises’ GTFP if appropriately screened (Qiu et al., 2021); however, some studies note that unfiltered foreign investment inflows may exacerbate environmental pollution in China, thereby inhibiting enterprises’ GTFP (Zhou et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021). Additionally, the influence from foreign investment on such total factor productivity of host countries is contingent upon the environmental regulation, financial development, investment, and innovation heterogeneity, thereby introducing uncertainty and time-varying effects (Kukulski and Ryan, 2011; Yue et al., 2016). Third, regarding environmental regulations, varying types of environmental regulations accelerate enterprises’ GTFP through distinct mechanisms. Command-and-control environmental regulations and public participation environmental regulations notably foster corporate technical efficiency (Cheng and Kong, 2022), while market-oriented environmental regulations primarily drive technological progress (Li et al., 2022). Prior theoretical research data on the factors influencing enterprises’ GTFP provides a crucial theoretical foundation and reference for the present study; however, few studies have delved into the correlation between the carbon emission trading scheme and enterprises’ GTFP, particularly the underlying pathways through which the trading scheme affects enterprises’ GTFP. In view of this, the present study utilizes data from A-share listed companies (2011–2023) to delve into the influence from the trading scheme on GTFP, uncover the underlying pathways of this impact, and explore the economic consequences of the trading scheme. This diversifies the research on the economic consequences of the trading scheme and offers empirical evidence for elevating enterprises’ GTFP and achieving high-quality development. The present research makes the marginal contributions as follows: First, employing the carbon emission trading scheme as a starting point, it investigates its impact on enterprises’ GTFP, diversifying the research on the economic consequences of the scheme. Second, it profoundly reveals the underlying pathways through which the scheme affects enterprises’ GTFP, providing a new theoretical perspective for understanding the correlation between the trading scheme and enterprises’ GTFP, while offering important insights for elevating their GTFP and promoting high-quality development. Third, it further explores the economic consequences of the trading scheme’s impact, providing references for subsequent related research and offering beneficial policy implications for elevating enterprises’ GTFP. 2 Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses 2.1 Carbon emission trading scheme and enterprises’ green total factor productivity The carbon emission trading scheme confers commodity attributes to carbon emission rights, allowing various trading entities to legally trade carbon emission rights in the market, thereby internalizing the external costs of carbon emission through price mechanisms. In the short term, due to stringent carbon emission constraints, some high-pollution, high-energy-consuming enterprises may rely on reducing output to control carbon emission, leading to a decline in enterprises’ GTFP (Gray, 1987); however, in the long term, the implementation of the trading scheme will accelerate enterprises’ GTFP. The promotional effect of the carbon emission trading scheme on enterprises’ GTFP is primarily manifested in the following ways: First, regarding the “compliance cost effect”, carbon quotas determine carbon trading prices; when carbon quotas are insufficient to meet carbon demand, abatement costs increase. To maximize profits, enterprises will actively adopt clean energy, upgrade equipment, and improve technology, establishing a win-win pattern for production efficiency and green development (Feng et al., 2020; Arimura and Abe, 2021), thereby accelerating enterprises’ GTFP. Second, regarding the “innovation compensation effect”, the “coercive mechanism” formed by the supervision of ecological and environmental management departments will accelerate technological innovation activities in high-energy-consuming and high-polluting enterprises (Zhang and Wang, 2021). Additionally, government support through tax incentives and innovation subsidies will provide appropriate compensation to low-carbon enterprises, aiding in quality and efficiency improvements (Wu and Wang, 2022; Li et al., 2022), thereby elevating their GTFP. In view of this, it is available to propose a hypothesis: Hypothesis 1The carbon emission trading scheme is capable of elevating enterprises’ GTFP. 2.2 Carbon emission trading scheme, financing constraints, and enterprises’ green total factor productivity The carbon emission trading scheme creates an open and transparent carbon trading environment, reduces internal and external information imbalance and signals strong corporate sustainability, thereby alleviating corporate financing constraints. The inclusion of enterprises in the carbon market makes information such as compliance status, carbon quota transaction volumes, emissions reduction measures, and performance more transparent, enabling outsiders to more accurately assess the enterprises’ operating conditions and growth potential, thereby attracting more external economic resources (Wang et al., 2022) and improving financing conditions. Intriguingly, according to organizational reputation theory, enterprises’ active participation in carbon emission trading and their efforts and achievements in environmental protection are showcased to stakeholders and the media, elevating their brand image and value, gaining excess returns and legitimacy, and fostering good correlations with the government, financial institutions, investors, and creditors, thereby obtaining recognition and support (Dan et al., 2023), ultimately alleviating financing constraints. The alleviation of financing constraints can elevate enterprises’ GTFP. Easier access to financing allows enterprises to increase investments in production equipment, technological innovation, employee training, and market expansion, improving production processes and efficiency and optimizing resource allocation efficiency (Hsieh and Klenow, 2009; Kalatzis et al., 2025), thereby generating “output compensation effects” from newly added factors invested in production activities and “innovation compensation effects” from newly added factors invested in innovation activities, ultimately elevating enterprises’ GTFP. In view of this, it is available to propose a hypothesis: Hypothesis 2The carbon emission trading scheme is capable of alleviating corporate financing constraints, thereby elevating enterprises’ GTFP. 2.3 Carbon emission trading scheme, agency costs, and enterprises’ green total factor productivity As an environmental regulatory mandate, the carbon emission trading scheme imposes institutional-level controls on enterprises’ environmental-related activities, and accelerates information exchange among various parties by transmitting market signals and reducing information rents, thereby reducing the enterprises’ first-type agency costs (Zhou et al., 2018), the carbon emission trading scheme effectively transmits market signals, allowing creditors and other stakeholders to better understand the enterprises’ green operating conditions through the platform, reducing the likelihood of adverse selection (Velte et al., 2020), prompting agents to value carbon emission trading, improving resource allocation efficiency, and reducing private motives, thereby elevating internal governance. Intriguingly, in the presence of information imbalance, to incentivize and monitor the authenticity of high-level information disclosure, enterprises need to establish a compensation mechanism for bonuses, i.e., information rents, which occupy the enterprises’ R&D investment resources. The carbon emission trading scheme elevates information symmetry, suppresses information rents, and reduces managers’ and management’s private surpluses in high-level technological R&D (Zhu et al., 2022; Canarella and Miller, 2022). The reduction in agency costs can effectively elevate enterprises’ GTFP. The reduction in agency costs optimizes the internal governance structure and resource allocation within the organization, and improves overall action and decision-making efficiency, thereby elevating enterprises’ GTFP. Intriguingly, the reduction in agency costs diminishes internal managers’ ability to accumulate liquid assets for private gain (Huang and Zhang, 2012), narrowing the space for management to seek private benefits and increasing investment in corporate green innovation, thereby elevating enterprises’ GTFP. In view of this, it is available to propose a hypothesis: Hypothesis 3The carbon emission trading scheme can reduce corporate agency costs, thereby elevating enterprises’ GTFP. 2.4 Carbon emission trading scheme, digital transformation, and enterprises’ green total factor productivity The carbon emission trading system drives enterprises to achieve digital transformation through dual drivers: mandatory accounting requirements and innovation in digital emission reduction. On one hand, the carbon emission trading system requires enterprises to track and locate their carbon footprint, and digital transformation can help enterprises meet this requirement. By applying digital technologies, enterprises can establish comprehensive digital accounting and management systems to record and monitor carbon emissions (Gao and Huang, 2024), thereby achieving dynamic supervision of their carbon footprint. On the other hand, although the carbon emission trading system imposes certain cost pressures on enterprises, according to the “Porter Hypothesis,” enterprises can offset compliance costs through technological improvements and efficiency enhancements. This incentivizes enterprises to adopt more advanced digital technologies, promoting energy-saving upgrades and iterations in production and operations, and reducing their carbon emissions (Wang et al., 2023; Shang et al., 2023), thereby meeting the quota requirements of carbon emission trading. Additionally, the digital business models spurred by digital technologies and the expansion of enterprise digital transformation also contribute to promoting carbon emission reduction (Rong et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024). Digital transformation promotes the improvement of enterprise green total factor productivity. First, through technologies such as the Internet of Things and the Industrial Internet, digital transformation enables intelligent operation, maintenance, and parameter optimization of energy equipment and production processes, indirectly reducing energy consumption per unit of output and operational costs (Xue and Zhu, 2025; Li and Cao, 2025). Second, by deploying digital management platforms, enterprises break down internal data silos (Li et al., 2023), facilitating data-driven precise decision-making and resource allocation, thereby significantly enhancing resource utilization efficiency. Finally, during the process of digital transformation, the continuous improvement in information capture and intelligent analysis capabilities helps enterprises identify internal redundant resources, integrate them with external resources (Kerpedzhiev et al., 2021), transform them into heterogeneous resources, and apply them to green innovation activities, thereby boosting enterprise green total factor productivity. In view of this, it is available to propose a hypothesis: Hypothesis 4The carbon emission trading system promotes enterprise digital transformation, thereby elevating enterprises’ GTFP. 3 Research design 3.1 Data sources The present study selects data from A-share listed companies (from 2011 to 2023). The research data are sourced from the CSMAR database, official corporate websites, and the WIND database. Following existing research, the following criteria are used to screen and process the sample: (1) Exclude financial and real estate industry listed ones; (2) Exclude samples with missing data; (3) Exclude ST and *ST ones; (4) To remove the influence from extreme values, the main continuous variables are winsorized at the 1% and 99% levels. Ultimately, 35,299 observations of listed companies are obtained, and empirical tests are conducted using STATA 18.0. 3.2 Variable selection 3.2.1 Core explanatory variable: carbon emission trading scheme We use a dummy variable to represent the pilot status of the carbon emission trading scheme, defined as the interaction between the policy implementation time dummy and the pilot province indicator and denoted by DID. DID equals 1 if the province or municipality in which enterprise i is located implements the carbon emission trading scheme in year t, and 0 otherwise. Specifically, the carbon markets in Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, and Guangdong were launched in late 2013, whereas those in Hubei and Chongqing were launched in 2014. Following the policy timing convention adopted by Wu F. et al. (2021) for annual data, we set 2014 as the initial policy year for all six regions. According to information released by the Fujian Provincial Development and Reform Commission, trading in Fujian’s carbon market began in June 2016. We therefore set 2016 as the initial policy year for Fujian. Trading in the national carbon emission trading market officially began on 16 July 2021. Accordingly, we code all sample enterprises as treated from 2021 onward. After determining the policy implementation year for each region, we construct the enterprise-year explanatory variable DID based on the province or municipality in which each enterprise is registered. DID equals 1 from 2014 onward for enterprises registered in Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, Guangdong, Hubei, or Chongqing, and from 2016 onward for enterprises registered in Fujian. All sample enterprises are assigned DID = 1 from 2021 onward, while all remaining enterprise-year observations are assigned DID = 0. 3.2.2 Dependent variable: enterprises’ green total factor productivity We use MaxDEA software to measure GTFP. Capital input is measured by year-end net fixed assets, deflated using the fixed asset investment price index. Labor input is measured by the number of employees. Energy input is measured by standard coal consumption. We then estimate standard coal consumption for each industrial enterprise according to its revenue as a share of regional gross industrial output. Expected output is measured by operating revenue, deflated using the industrial producer’s ex-factory price index. Undesirable outputs include industrial SO2 emissions, industrial wastewater discharges, and industrial soot emissions. To facilitate subsequent analysis, this paper calculates the absolute values of GTFP in percentage terms. 3.2.3 Intermediary variables3.2.3.1 Corporate agency cost (ATO) Following Ang et al. (2000), we measure corporate agency cost from the perspective of the efficiency with which management deploys assets to generate revenue. Specifically, asset turnover, calculated as operating revenue divided by average total assets, is used as a proxy for corporate agency cost. A higher value indicates more efficient asset utilization and lower corporate agency cost. 3.2.3.2 Corporate financing constraints (FC) We use the FC index to measure the degree of corporate financing constraints faced by enterprises. The index is constructed directly from three financial variables: enterprise size, enterprise age, and the cash dividend payout ratio. It captures the availability of external financing and the difficulty of obtaining funds. Specifically, we first standardize these three variables within each year and rank the listed companies in ascending order according to the mean of their standardized values. The lower and upper tercile thresholds are then used to define the financing constraint dummy variable, QUFC. Listed companies above the 66th percentile are classified as having low corporate financing constraints and assigned QUFC = 0, while those below the 33rd percentile are classified as having high corporate financing constraints and assigned QUFC = 1. We then estimate the model using a Logit regression to obtain the predicted probability, P, of corporate financing constraints for each enterprise in each year. This probability is defined as the corporate financing constraints index, FC. A higher FC value indicates more severe corporate financing constraints. 3.2.3.3 Digital transformation (DT) We consider digital transformation to be a major strategy for promoting high-quality enterprise development in the new era. Information on this strategy is likely to appear in annual reports, which summarize past activities and provide guidance for future development. The vocabulary used in annual reports can reflect an enterprise’s strategic characteristics and future outlook, as well as its management philosophy and the development path shaped by that philosophy. It is therefore reasonable to measure digital transformation using the frequency of related terms in listed companies’ annual reports. Following Wu Q. et al. (2021), we select 76 keywords covering five areas: artificial intelligence technologies, big data technologies, cloud computing technologies, blockchain technologies, and applications of digital technologies. We use their word frequencies to construct an indicator system for enterprise digital transformation. 3.2.3.4 Moderating variable market competition (HHI) We use the industry Herfindahl index as a proxy for market competition. The index is calculated as the sum of the squared ratios of each enterprise’s main business revenue to total main business revenue in its industry. A lower index value indicates more intense market competition. Because this is an inverse indicator, we follow Du and Li (2020) and take its reciprocal to convert it into a positive indicator. 3.2.4 Variables for heterogeneity analysis3.2.4.1 Heterogeneity analysis by ownership structure Enterprise ownership is classified as state-owned or non-state-owned according to the nature of the ultimate controller. We use this criterion to divide the sample into state-owned and non-state-owned enterprises. Specifically, enterprises whose ultimate controller is the state or a state-owned legal entity are classified as state-owned, while those whose ultimate controller is an individual or a non-state-owned legal entity are classified as non-state-owned. 3.2.4.2 Heterogeneity analysis by pollution intensity We identify pollution-intensive listed enterprises with reference to the Catalogue for Industry Classification and Management of Environmental Verification of Listed Companies issued by the Ministry of Environmental Protection in 2008 and the Guidelines for Industry Classification of Listed Companies issued by the China Securities Regulatory Commission in 2012. Pollution-intensive enterprises are mainly concentrated in coal, mining, textiles, leather manufacturing, papermaking, petrochemicals, pharmaceuticals, chemicals, metallurgy, and thermal power generation. Specifically, enterprises in the following industries are classified as pollution-intensive: coal mining and washing (B06); petroleum and natural gas extraction (B07); ferrous metal ore mining and processing (B08); non-ferrous metal ore mining and processing (B09); non-metallic mineral mining and processing (B10); textile manufacturing (C17); leather, fur, feather products, and footwear manufacturing (C19); paper and paper products manufacturing (C22); petroleum processing, coking, and nuclear fuel processing (C25); chemical raw material and chemical product manufacturing (C26); pharmaceutical manufacturing (C27); ferrous metal smelting and rolling (C31); non-ferrous metal smelting and rolling (C32); and electricity and heat production and supply (D44). 3.2.4.3 Heterogeneity analysis by regional distribution We divide the sample into eastern and non-eastern groups according to the province in which each listed company is registered. The eastern group includes Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Liaoning, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Shandong, Guangdong, and Hainan. The non-eastern group includes Shanxi, Jilin, Heilongjiang, Henan, Hubei, Hunan, Anhui, Jiangxi, Inner Mongolia, Chongqing, Sichuan, Guangxi, Guizhou, Yunnan, Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia, and Xinjiang. 3.2.5 Control variables To control for other factors, the following control variables are selected: (1) Return on Assets (ROA): measured by net profit divided by average total assets; (2) Growth Rate of Revenue (Growth): measured by the current year’s operating revenue divided by the previous year’s operating revenue minus 1; (3) Tobin’s Q (TQ): (market value of tradable shares + number of non-tradable shares × net assets per share + book value of liabilities)/total assets; (4) Equity Concentration (Top10): measured by the ratio of shares held by the top ten shareholders to the total shares; (5) Management Expense Ratio (Mfee): measured by management expenses divided by operating revenue; (6) Capital Intensity (Cap): measured by total assets divided by operating revenue. The regression outcomes are exhibited in Columns (1) and (2) within Table 3 Column (1) presents the net benefit estimation outcomes without control variables, exhibiting an impact coefficient of 0.121, which is notable at the 0.05 threshold. Column (2) includes control variables, and the influence from the carbon emission trading scheme on enterprises’ GTFP exhibits a notable positivity at the 0.01 threshold, with the impact coefficient increasing to 0.142, suggesting that the implementation of the carbon emission trading scheme notably elevates enterprises’ GTFP, supporting Hypothesis 1. A possible explanation for this phenomenon is that the carbon quota in carbon emission trading determines carbon trading prices. When an enterprise’s carbon demand exceeds its carbon quota, abatement costs increase. To maximize profits, enterprises will adopt measures such as using clean energy, upgrading equipment, improving technology, reducing abatement costs, and promoting green development and production efficiency, thereby effectively elevating enterprises’ GTFP. Conversely, the “coercive mechanism” formed by the ecological and environmental management department supervision forces high-pollution and high-energy-consuming enterprises to accelerate technological innovation. Given the high environmental governance costs, pollution-intensive enterprises have a more urgent desire to reduce carbon emission and will more proactively use clean energy and promote technological innovation, thus resulting in a stronger policy effect of the carbon emission trading scheme. 4.7.3 Heterogeneity analysis by regional distribution China’s vast territory leads to differences in resource endowments, industrial structures, levels of industrialization, and digitalization across regions, resulting in varying impacts of the trading scheme on the GTFP of enterprises. Therefore, the current study carries out a heterogeneity analysis based on whether enterprises are situated in the eastern region or the central and western regions, delving into how varying regional distributions affect their GTFP. The regression results are presented in Columns (5) and (6) of Table 14. These findings reveal that carbon trading policies have a positive and statistically significant impact (at the 0.05 threshold) on enhancing the GTFP of enterprises in the eastern region, whereas the impact in the central and western regions is negative, albeit not statistically significant. The likely reason is that eastern regions have larger cities, developed economies, high levels of digitalization, and high degrees of industrialization, attracting a notable influx of high-skilled human capital. These regions possess obvious technological advantages, supporting the use of market-incentivized environmental regulation tools and enabling them to fully leverage their policy effects. The carbon emission trading scheme has a noticeable promoting effect in these regions. In contrast, the economic and technical foundations in the central and western regions are relatively weak, with a higher proportion of high-energy-consuming, mid-to-low-end industries and obvious characteristics of extensive production. There is a lag in responding to market-based environmental regulations, hence the effect of the trading scheme on the GTFP of enterprises in the central and western regions cannot be observed in the short term. Finally, this paper employs Fisher’s permutation test to conduct between-group DID coefficient difference tests from three dimensions: ownership nature, pollution degree, and regional distribution, with 1,000 permutations. The specific results are reported in Table 14. According to the p-values from the permutation test, the coefficient differences between the ownership nature and regional distribution groups are significant at the 5% level, while the coefficient difference between the pollution degree groups is significant at the 10% level. These results indicate that the coefficients across different subsamples are statistically significantly different, confirming the statistical reliability of the heterogeneity analysis in this paper. 5 Further research: economic consequences test The above sections verify that the carbon emission trading scheme is capable of notably elevating enterprises’ GTFP from multiple perspectives. The question then arises: Does this research conclusion achieve the expected effect of improving total factor productivity (TFP)? To further explore this issue, the present study uses corporate high-quality development as the research object, analyzing how the scheme promotes enterprises’ GTFP and, in turn, drives high-quality corporate development. Drawing upon previous research, the present study selects corporate total factor productivity to measure high-quality corporate development. In light of this, the current study has developed Models (11) and (12) to delve into the economic ramifications stemming from the influence of the scheme on enterprises’ GTFP. The detailed regression outcomes are demonstrated in Table 15. Column (1) exhibits that the parameter estimate of the scheme’s impact on high-quality corporate development registers 0.053 and is notable at the 0.01 threshold, revealing that the scheme can promote high-quality corporate development, consistent with previous research (Cao et al., 2023). Column (2) includes both the scheme and enterprises’ GTFP in the regression model. The outcomes demonstrate that the parameter estimate of the carbon emission trading scheme registers 0.029 and is statistically notable at the 0.10 threshold, while the parameter estimate of corporate total factor productivity is also 0.158 and exhibits a notable positivity at the 0.01 threshold, suggesting that the scheme can elevate enterprises’ GTFP and promote high-quality development ultimately. A possible explanation for this phenomenon is that the cost effect and innovation compensation effect of the scheme, along with government policy support and encouragement for low-carbon enterprises, guide them to engage in green innovation and resource optimization activities actively, thereby improving GTFP. Intriguingly, as their GTFP improves, not only do production efficiency and environmental adaptability increase, but the enterprise’s environmental image and brand value in the eyes of consumers also improve, enabling the enterprises to achieve economic benefits while ensuring environmental benefits, thereby promoting high-quality corporate development. The study finds that the trading scheme can notably elevate enterprises’ GTFP. Mechanism tests reveal that the trading scheme primarily elevates enterprises’ GTFP by alleviating financing constraints, reducing agency costs and promoting digital transformation. Moderating effect tests indicate that market competition serves as a positive moderating role in the correlation between the scheme and enterprises’ GTFP. Heterogeneity analysis demonstrates that the policy effects of the trading scheme are stronger in non-SOEs, pollution-intensive enterprises, and enterprises in the eastern region. Economic consequences tests reveal that the trading scheme can further promote high-quality corporate development by accelerating enterprises’ GTFP. 6.2 Policy-related recommendations In view of the above research conclusions, the present study proposes the following policy-related recommendations: It is recommended to improve the carbon emission trading scheme and advance the construction of the carbon emission trading market. Although China’s national carbon market officially launched in 2021, its coverage is still limited, including only the power generation industry, and has not fully leveraged the potential of carbon trading for emissions reduction. Therefore, the government may expand the coverage of industries in the carbon market, gradually incorporating more high-emission industries such as steel, chemicals, cement, non-ferrous metals, and papermaking into the national carbon trading system to elevate overall emissions reduction effects. Meanwhile, the governments may gradually introduce a paid allocation mechanism for carbon quotas, optimize the trading mechanism, and elevate the internal driving force for enterprises to reduce emissions, improving the effectiveness and activity of the carbon emission trading market. Additionally, the governments may establish a sound system for monitoring, reporting, and verifying carbon emission data to ensure the accuracy and transparency of carbon emission data, providing data support for the effective operation of the carbon market. Moreover, it is recommended to implement differentiated strategies to fully leverage the positive effects of the trading scheme on GTFP. The heterogeneity analysis in the present study demonstrates that the scheme strongly elevates GTFP in non-SOEs, pollution-intensive enterprises, and enterprises in the eastern region. Therefore, implementing differentiated strategies is conducive to fully leveraging the positive effects of the scheme on GTFP. Specifically, the governments may strengthen environmental regulation of SOEs and non-pollution-intensive enterprises, avoiding excessive allocation of carbon quotas to better leverage the “backstop effect” of policies. Non-SOEs and pollution-intensive enterprises, constrained by funds and resource availability, may be forced to allocate resources originally intended for improving total factor productivity to non-productive carbon reduction projects. Therefore, the governments can provide incentives such as tax reductions and green financial support to elevate their motivation for emissions reduction. For the purpose of enterprises from the central and western regions, given their relatively weak technological foundations and traditional industrial structures, the governments may take a combination of support and constraint measures, encouraging green development in the central and western regions and fully leveraging the positive effects of the carbon emission trading scheme. Finally, it is recommended to fully leverage the positive effects of carbon emission trading to promote high-quality corporate development. The economic consequences test in the present study demonstrates that the carbon emission trading scheme further enhanced high-quality corporate development by elevating GTFP. Therefore, it is necessary to fully utilize the positive effects of carbon emission trading to promote high-quality corporate development. As for enterprises willing and able to improve their green production efficiency but lacking sufficient funds, the governments may provide appropriate financial and tax support to alleviate the financial constraints of corporate low-carbon development and sustainable development. Second, commercial banks and other financial institutions should increase their investment and financing support for carbon reduction projects, adjust the structure of credit resources promptly, and direct more financial resources toward green, low-carbon, and innovative projects. By adjusting the allocation of credit resources, they are allowed to alleviate the financial constraints and boost their GTFP. Third, benefiting from policies and financial resources, enterprises may better identify and seize investment opportunities in their production and operations, strengthen internal communication and cooperation, align shareholders and professional managers in their pursuit of low-carbon corporate development, reduce agency costs, and support their high-quality development.
