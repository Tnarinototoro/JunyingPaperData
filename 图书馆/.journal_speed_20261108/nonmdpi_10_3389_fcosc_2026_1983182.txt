You can register here Notify me Abstract Ecological grief is grief for the loss of species, ecosystems, landscapes, and places. Solastalgia is a similar type of distress that occurs when unwanted change occurs in the environment of a person's home. These ideas were initially intended to capture place-based experiences, but ecological distress is increasingly becoming digital media-mediated. Individuals are now flooded with continuous streams of information, such as climate-related disasters, loss of biodiversity, headlines, personal stories, images, and algorithmically selected content. This perspective suggests that doomscrolling goes beyond being just a symptom of greater recognition of ecological crises. Over time, compulsive digital behavior can change how people feel and express ecological grief through their emotions and behaviors. Distress or discomfort with the digital environment should not be mistaken for real-world loss. Despite this, over time digital exposure may increase climate anxiety and sense of powerlessness, add to symptoms of vicarious trauma, and displace restorative activities like sleep, social connection, and nature contact. Presently, there is no definitive evidence to conclude that social media plays an independent role in climate anxiety, as the available evidence remains mostly cross-sectional. Digital media may also support environmental learning, collective action, peer support, and conservation engagement. I thus suggest a differentiated framework of three inter-related pathways: direct ecological loss, mediated ecological witnessing, and behavioral displacement due to compulsive media use. Lastly, this perspective emphasizes the need for follow-up studies, standardized metrics, ethically framed climate communication, and approaches to climate action in conservation science.
