We fitted a Random Forest model to monthly temperature and precipitation normals and the multi-year mean leaf area index (LAI) derived from a satellite-informed machine-learning urban tree product across 21,590 urban cells, then projected end-of-century change under six Shared Socioeconomic Pathways. Leave-one-continent-out validation gave \(R^2 = 0.846\) (95% CI \(0.813\) – \(0.873\) from spatial-block resampling), but that spatial skill does not transfer to time: driven by observed reanalysis anomalies over exactly the satellite record, the same relationship reproduces neither the magnitude nor the pattern of observed change ( \(\rho = +0.087\) ). The response variable is itself a machine-learning product built from overlapping climate predictors, so the spatial fit is not independent evidence of that relationship either. Under modified Mann–Kendall tests with false-discovery-rate control, 19.8% of cells greened and 5.3% browned during 2000–2022, roughly half the browning obtained without those corrections. Global mean change is positive under all scenarios, yet 32.6% of cells decline under SSP5-8.5, with declines concentrated in tropical humid cities and gains at high latitudes. Two further results bound them: 10.6% of cells fall outside the fitted climate domain under SSP5-8.5, and four algorithms agree on the sign of change at only 68.1% of cells. We report these as changes implied by transferring a spatial relationship into future climates, not forecasts: the zonal contrast is the most reproducible result across the evaluated algorithms; cell-level magnitudes and city orderings are not. Environmental Geography Forest Ecology Urban Ecology Environmental Sciences Tree Biology Urban Economics Introduction More than half of the world’s population lives in cities, and cities concentrate both the hazards of a warming climate and the interventions intended to reduce them Tuholske et al. ( 2021 ). Urban heat islands raise air temperatures in built environments well above their rural surroundings (Santamouris 2020 ), and urban trees are among the few interventions that address several of the resulting problems at once, moderating surface and air temperature, intercepting rainfall, sequestering carbon and improving amenity (Bowler et al. 2010 ; Livesley et al. 2016 ; Endreny et al. 2017 ; Turner-Skoff and Cavender 2019 ; Schwaab et al. 202 ), and the magnitude of the cooling benefit has grown measurably over the satellite era (Zhao et al. 2023 ). How much of that capacity survives the century depends on how urban tree canopies themselves respond to climate change, and that is not well constrained. Leaf area index (LAI), the one-sided green leaf area per unit reference area, is the standard structural descriptor of a canopy and the variable that most directly governs shading, interception and transpiration (Piao et al. 2020 ). Satellite records show widespread greening of terrestrial vegetation since the 1980s (Zhu et al. 2016 ), but urban trees grow under constraints that natural stands do not share: restricted rooting volume, impervious surfaces, altered hydrology, active management and species assemblages chosen by people rather than assembled by competition; urban trees have in some regions grown faster than their rural counterparts as the climate has warmed (Pretzsch et al. 2017 ), while inventories show canopy extent declining in others Nowak and Greenfield ( 2018 ). Species-level work indicates that a large fraction of the species now planted in low-latitude cities will exceed their thermal safety margins within decades (Esperon-Rodriguez et al. 2022 ), but species-level risk does not translate directly into a spatially continuous statement about canopy structure. The recent release of a global, multi-decadal urban tree LAI product (Dong et al. 2025 ) makes a spatially continuous assessment possible for the first time. It also introduces a complication that shapes everything that follows, and that we state at the outset rather than in the limitations. That product is itself a Random Forest prediction, trained to reproduce reprocessed MODIS LAI from monthly WorldClim temperature, precipitation, radiation and vapour-pressure normals together with a canopy-height map. Any subsequent model that predicts this product from monthly temperature and precipitation is therefore partly re-estimating a relationship that was built into the response variable. High predictive skill in such a model is expected, and it is not evidence that urban tree leaf area responds to climate in the fitted way. That does not make the exercise empty, but it does change what it can claim. Data-driven models are effective at recovering non-linear vegetation–climate relationships from large observational archives (Reichstein et al. 2019 ). What a statistical model of this kind can do is make the embedded climate–leaf-area association explicit, testable and transferable: it can be interrogated for the shape of the response, evaluated where the response is and is not supported by data, and propagated into projected climate with its uncertainty carried along. What it cannot do is demonstrate that a given city’s canopy will change over time in the way that the spatial gradient between cities implies. Substituting space for time is a strong assumption, and one that is testable rather than axiomatic (Blois et al. 2013 ; Damgaard 2019 ; Lovell et al. 2023 ). We test it here, and the answer shapes everything that follows: the relationship recovered across space predicts where leaf area is far better than it predicts how leaf area moves within a place over time. We therefore set out to answer five questions, framed so that each has a determinate answer. We expected, before analysing, that the answers would be more equivocal than the predictive skill of the model suggests: that trend detection would be substantially reduced by proper inference, that the model would be extrapolating over a large part of the high-emission climate space, and that the spatial gradient would overstate temporal response. We report below the extent to which that was borne out. 1. After correcting for serial dependence and multiple testing, what fraction of urban grid cells shows a detectable trend in leaf area over 2000–2022, and in which direction? 2. How well does a climate-only statistical model transfer to unseen continents and to unseen climate zones, and how much of that skill rests on non-climatic spatial gradients? 3. For how much of the projected end-of-century climate is such a model inside its domain of applicability, and how large is the projected signal relative to the uncertainty that can be quantified? 4. How does the projected change decompose into temperature, precipitation and their interaction, and does that decomposition hold across regions? 5. Does the spatial climate–leaf-area gradient reproduce the temporal change actually observed over the satellite record? Materials and methods Urban tree leaf area data We used the Global Urban Tree LAI/SAI dataset (Dong et al. 2025 ), which provides monthly urban tree LAI and stem area index for 2000–2022. The native product is at 500m; we used the \(0.5^{\circ }\) aggregate distributed by the same authors, because that is the resolution of the climate archive described in Section 2.2 and because resampling either product to the other’s grid would introduce an interpolation step whose error we could not quantify. The cost of this choice is that within-city heterogeneity in canopy structure and microclimate is averaged away, and results should not be read as statements about neighbourhoods. Three properties of the product govern the interpretation of every result below, and are documented in ESM 1 , Section 1 . Provenance. The product was generated by a Random Forest, selected by the FLAML automated machine learning library over a LightGBM alternative (Wang et al. 2021 ), trained to predict reprocessed MODIS Version 6.1 LAI (Lin et al. 2023 ) from monthly maximum and minimum air temperature, precipitation, incident shortwave radiation, vapour pressure and vapour pressure deficit (all from the WorldClim v2.1 1970–2000 climatology; Fick and Hijmans 2017), together with the ETH global canopy height map (Lang et al. 2023 ). Training samples came from natural-tree pixels in 5 regions containing urban area, and the fitted model was then applied to the urban pixels. Two of the seven predictors of that product belong to the same variable families we use here. The spatial field we model is therefore not independent of our predictors. Temporal dimension. The product’s climate inputs are a single fixed 1970–2000 climatology. Its year-to-year variation derives from the MODIS target, not from the climate covariates. The trend analysis of Section 2.3 and the hindcast of Section 2.7 therefore exploit a dimension of the product that was not manufactured from the predictors used here. Denominator. The product reports green leaf area per unit tree-covered area within the cell, explicitly excluding buildings and other impervious surfaces. It is a leaf-density measure, not a measure of canopy extent. A city could lose trees without the index changing, and the index could fall while tree cover is constant. We therefore use “urban tree LAI” or “leaf-area density” throughout and never “canopy loss”, “canopy cover” or “canopy extent”. Uncertainty. The product’s authors report \(R = 0.85\) and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 1.03 against 14 field reference sites, and \(R =\) 0.89–0.97 with RMSE \(=\) 0.36–0.64 against its MODIS target. The first of these exceeds the error of the model we fit to the product, a point we return to in Section 4.1 . A cell was retained if it had a valid LAI value in at least 6 months of the year in at least 10 of the 23 years, and valid climate data. This yielded \(N = {21,590}\) cells. In practice the criterion binds weakly: 21,220 cells (98.3%) have a valid annual value in all 23 years, and no month is systematically missing (ESM 1 , Section 3 ). The annual mean LAI of cell i in year t is the mean over the valid months of that year, and the response variable is $$\begin{aligned} \overline{\textrm{LAI}}_i = \frac{1}{T_i}\sum _{t \in \mathcal {T}_i} \textrm{LAI}_{i,t}, \end{aligned}$$ (1) where \(\mathcal {T}_i\) is the set of years with a valid value at cell i and \(T_i = |\mathcal {T}_i| \le 23\) . Using the cell-specific \(T_i\) rather than a fixed \(T = 23\) matters for the 370 cells with incomplete records. Climate data Monthly mean air temperature and monthly total precipitation were taken from the constrained Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) archive of Beck et al. ( 2023 ), downloaded from figshare ( https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.21789074 ) in March 2026, at 0.5 resolution. We used the ensemble mean and the ensemble standard deviation for the historical periods 1901–1930, 1931–1960, 1961–1990 and 1991–2020 and for 2071–2099 under SSP1-1.9, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, SSP4-6.0 and SSP5-8.5 (Eyring et al. 2016 ; O’ Neill et al. 2016 ; Riahi et al. 2017 ). The Köppen–Geiger classifications for the same periods come from the same archive. The archive distributes ensemble summaries rather than individual model fields, so we could not run each general circulation model separately. Section 2.6 describes the Monte Carlo scheme we used instead and states explicitly what it does and does not represent. Each cell was assigned a Köppen–Geiger class using the numeric legend distributed with the archive (1 Af, 2 Am, 3 Aw, 4 BWh, ..., 30 EF). The full code-to-class mapping and the number of urban cells in each class are given in ESM 1 , Section 2 . Unless stated otherwise, climate zones refer to the fixed 1991–2020 classification, so a city described as “continental” is described by its present climate. Historical trend analysis Annual mean LAI series were tested for monotonic trend with the Mann–Kendall statistic and the Theil–Sen slope estimator (Mann 1945 ; Sen 1968 ; Kendall 1975 ), using \(T_i\) observations per cell. Two features of these data make the conventional test inappropriate on its own. First, the series are strongly persistent: the mean lag-1 autocorrelation of the detrended series is \(+0.44\) and 89.2% of cells exceed \(r_1 = 0.2\) , which inflates the number of trends declared significant. Second, 21,590 tests are performed, so a nominal 5% threshold is expected to return roughly 1,080 false positives. We therefore report the test under three specifications: conventional Mann–Kendall, the modified test of Hamed and Rao (1998) that inflates the variance of S by the effective sample size implied by the significant autocorrelations of the detrended ranks, and trend-free pre-whitening (Yue et al. 2002). Each is run with and without Benjamini–Hochberg control of the false discovery rate (FDR) at \(q < 0.05\) (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995 ). As a check on the analytical variance correction we added a fourth specification in which the null variance of S is estimated by resampling rather than assumed. Each cell’s series is detrended with its Theil–Sen slope, the residuals are resampled in contiguous blocks of three years so that short-range persistence is retained, and the observed S is referred to the standard deviation of S across 499 such resamples. This is run for every one of the 21,590 cells, so it is directly comparable with the analytical tests under the same FDR control. We estimate the null variance rather than form a resampling p value because the latter cannot fall below \(1/(B+1) = 0.002\) with 499 resamples, which is coarser than the first Benjamini–Hochberg threshold across 21,590 tests ( \(2.3 \times 10^{-6}\) ); no cell could then be rejected however strong its trend. Theil–Sen slopes are reported with 95% confidence intervals from the Kendall rank method. The headline figures use the modified test with FDR control; ESM 1 , Section 3 reports all specifications, the sensitivity to the completeness criterion, and the bootstrap comparison. Statistical model and validation The predictor matrix comprises 24 columns: the 12 monthly temperature normals and the 12 monthly precipitation totals, the latter transformed as \(\ln (1+P)\) to compress the right tail. No geographic coordinate is included. That choice is deliberate and is tested in Section 3.2 : a coordinate would be held fixed while climate is perturbed in a projection, so any part of the spatial gradient the model attributed to position rather than to weather could not respond to warming, and in cross-validation the coordinates do not earn their place. Two geographic encodings are retained as sensitivity analyses: latitude with the sine and cosine of longitude, and latitude with longitude divided by 180. Performance was estimated by leave-one-continent-out cross-validation (Roberts et al. 2017 ; Ploton et al. 2020 ). Folds are the dissolved Natural Earth admin-0 continent polygons, with North and South America merged and coastal cells assigned to the nearest polygon; every cell belongs to exactly one fold by construction, which is asserted in code. The fold map is shown in Fig. 2 c. All preprocessing (the z -scoring of temperature and the log transform and z -scoring of precipitation) is fitted on the training partition of each fold only. Random Forest hyperparameters were selected by nested spatial cross-validation (nested-CV): within each outer fold, an inner leave-one-continent-out loop over the remaining continents chose among five configurations (ESM 1 , Table S5 ). The configuration selected in the majority of outer folds (300 trees, maximum depth 20, max_features \(= 0.33\) , minimum 5 samples per leaf) was used for the final model, which was refitted on all 21,590 cells and used for every projection reported here. Eight algorithms were compared under identical folds: Random Forest (Breiman 2001 ), XGBoost (extreme gradient boosting; (Chen and Guestrin 2016 ), Gradient Boosting (Friedman 2001 ), k -nearest neighbours, a spline-based additive model with cubic B-splines and a ridge penalty as a flexible but interpretable reference, and Ridge, Lasso and Elastic Net regressions (Hoerl and Kennard 1970 ; Tibshirani 1996 ; Zou and Hastie 2005 ). All metrics carry 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, and each algorithm is compared with Random Forest by a paired bootstrap of squared errors and by a signed-rank test across folds. We also report the effect of ten random seeds, residual diagnostics against prediction, latitude, temperature and continent, and Moran’s I of the residuals. Analyses used scikit-learn 1.6.1 (Pedregosa et al. 2011 ) and XGBoost 3.2.0; complete versions, seeds and hyperparameters are in ESM 1 and ESM 2 . Domain of applicability A Random Forest cannot extrapolate: outside the fitted predictor envelope its predictions are bounded by the training response. Projection into climates with no present-day analogue is a recognised failure mode of statistical transfer functions (Williams and Jackson 2007 ; Fitzpatrick and Hargrove 2009 ), so before interpreting any projection we classified every cell in every scenario (Meyer and Pebesma 2021 ). The multivariate environmental similarity surface (MESS; Elith et al. 2010 ) gives the minimum, across the 24 monthly climate predictors, of a similarity score that is negative when a predictor is outside its training range. ExDet (Mesgaran et al. 2014 ) separates marginal extrapolation ( \(\textrm{NT1} < 0\) , at least one predictor out of range) from combinatorial novelty ( \(\textrm{NT2} > 1\) , every predictor individually in range but the joint combination outside the training Mahalanobis hull), the latter computed in the space of principal components retaining 99% of the training climate variance because the monthly predictors are strongly collinear. As an environmental-block complement to the geographic folds, we also ran leave-one-climate-zone-out cross-validation, holding out each Köppen–Geiger major group in turn. Projections, attribution and uncertainty Future climate features were constructed by the delta method, adding the scenario anomaly to the historical normals and clipping precipitation at zero, and scaled with the scalers fitted to the historical period. The projected change is the difference between the model’s prediction under future and historical climate at the same cell. Projections were computed with Random Forest, XGBoost, Gradient Boosting and k -nearest neighbours, and we report the correlation between them, the fraction of cells at which all four agree on the sign, and their spread. The uncertainty budget has four components, combined in quadrature. Climate spread was obtained from 100 Monte Carlo draws from the CMIP6 ensemble standard deviation, applied coherently across the twelve months (a single standardised deviate per cell and variable, scaled by the monthly \(\sigma \) ), so that the month-to-month correlation of a model bias is preserved; an independent-month variant is reported for contrast and gives a smaller spread. Fitting uncertainty comes from 20 bootstrap refits, seed variability from 10 seeds, and structural uncertainty from the spread across the four algorithms. The four terms are combined in quadrature, which assumes them mutually independent; Section 3.5 reports a nested check in which the two largest are crossed explicitly instead. We use 100 Monte Carlo draws here, where the quantity of interest is a per-cell standard deviation, and 15 draws crossed with the four algorithms for the city ensemble of Section 2.8 , where the quantity of interest is the rank distribution and each draw must be paired with every algorithm. We report the fraction of cells at which the projected signal exceeds the combined \(1\sigma \) , for the full domain and for the interpolative subset. This budget does not include the error of the LAI product itself, which the product’s authors report as RMSE \(= 1.03\) \(m^{2} m^{-2}\) against field data and which is not resolvable per cell; nor the error of matching a city to a cell, which we report separately in Section 2.8 . The response was decomposed by a factorial design at pixel level: $$\begin{aligned} \Delta \textrm{LAI}_{\Delta T}&= f(T^{\text {fut}}, P^{\text {hist}}) - f(T^{\text {hist}}, P^{\text {hist}}), \end{aligned}$$ (2) $$\begin{aligned} \Delta \textrm{LAI}_{\Delta P}&= f(T^{\text {hist}}, P^{\text {fut}}) - f(T^{\text {hist}}, P^{\text {hist}}), \end{aligned}$$ (3) $$\begin{aligned} \Delta \textrm{LAI}_{T\times P}&= \Delta \textrm{LAI}_{\text {total}} - \Delta \textrm{LAI}_{\Delta T} - \Delta \textrm{LAI}_{\Delta P}. \end{aligned}$$ (4) The three terms are an exact additive decomposition of the total. Because percentages formed by dividing by a near-zero total are unstable, we report the absolute terms and, as a share, the mean pixel-level fraction of \(|\Delta T| + |\Delta P| + |\text {interaction}|\) carried by each, together with the fraction of cells at which precipitation is the larger term. Attribution is reported globally and by climate group, continent and hemisphere. Global means were computed under four weightings: unweighted; by cell area; by cell area multiplied by historical LAI, as a sensitivity analysis that gives greater influence to large, high-LAI cells; and by the summed population of the cities matched to each cell. The third of these does not estimate total urban leaf area, because the urban and tree-covered fractions of each cell are unavailable. A weight consistent with this response variable would have to be the same 500m tree-cover mask that defines the product’s own denominator, which is not distributed with the \(0.5^\circ \) aggregate; substituting an independent land-cover product would weight the quantity by a different definition of tree cover than the one it is expressed per unit of. Model behaviour was examined with SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values (Lundberg and Lee 2017 ; Lundberg et al. 2020 ) computed on ten independent stratified subsamples of 800 cells drawn proportionally across the continent \(\times \) climate-group grid, with importance reported for predictor blocks as well as individual months, because collinear monthly predictors are not separately identifiable. Because a calendar month denotes opposite seasons in the two hemispheres, we repeated the analysis on predictors re-indexed relative to each cell’s own climatologically warmest month. The temperature response was characterised by accumulated local effects (ALE; Apley and Zhu 2020 ), which unlike partial dependence (PD) does not average the model over predictor combinations that never occur, alongside PD and individual conditional expectation curves and the density of training support. Temporal transferability Because the model is fitted to a spatial gradient and applied to a temporal change, we tested the substitution on windows that coincide exactly with the LAI record. ERA5 monthly reanalysis (Hersbach et al. 2020 ) supplies temperature and precipitation for the same 276 months, January 2000 to December 2022. For each year the model is driven by the calibration climatology plus that year’s observed anomaly, $$\begin{aligned} T_{i,m}(t) = T_{i,m}^{\text {hist}} + \bigl [T_{i,m}^{\text {ERA5}}(t) - \overline{T_{i,m}^{\text {ERA5}}}\bigr ], \end{aligned}$$ (5) and analogously for precipitation with the result clipped at zero. This anomaly formulation anchors the annual inputs to the calibration climatology and removes the mean offset between ERA5 and the calibration normals: over the urban cells the ERA5 2000–2022 mean temperature correlates with the normals at \(r = 0.999\) , with a mean offset of \(+0.24^\circ \text {C}\) that the anomaly form eliminates. It does not by itself guarantee that every annual value falls inside the fitted predictor range, since a large anomaly in one month can still carry a cell past a training extreme. We therefore checked, applying the same range and MESS diagnostics used for the scenarios: on average 4.0% of cells per year have at least one predictor outside the training envelope, at most 6.8% in any single year, and 24.8% of cells are affected in at least one of the 23 years (ESM 1 , Table S30 ). The aligned test therefore operates much closer to the fitted domain than the end-of-century projections, for which the corresponding figure reaches 10.6%. Four tests follow. First, the Theil–Sen rate of the predicted annual series is compared with that of the observed series, both over 2000–2022. Second, both series are detrended cell by cell and their annual anomalies correlated, which tests covariation without relying on either series having a trend. Third, the model is refitted on the first half of the record alone. The training unit is the urban cell, as everywhere else in this study, and both halves are described by period means: the response is the mean observed annual LAI over 2000–2011, and the 24 predictors are the calibration normals plus the mean ERA5 anomaly of those same twelve years, formed exactly as in Eq. ( 5 ) but with the anomaly averaged over the period rather than taken year by year. The validation matrix is built the same way from the 2012–2022 mean anomaly, and the response is the mean observed LAI of that period. Cells lacking a valid mean in either half are dropped. Hyperparameters are the modal nested-CV configuration used throughout, and the scaler is fitted on the 2000–2011 matrix only. The split is temporal and not spatial: every cell contributes to training, so the level of the held-out decade is partly recoverable from the spatial pattern alone and the informative quantity is the predicted change between the halves, \(\hat{Y}_{2012\text {--}22} - \hat{Y}_{2000\text {--}11}\) , compared with the observed one. Fourth, we report the warming rate that the 2000–2022 window actually samples, so that a weak result cannot be attributed to a weak climate signal. For context we also run the fitted model on the four 30-year climatologies from 1901–1930 onwards, which gives the leaf-area trajectory implied by twentieth-century warming. City-scale summary We form no composite index. Projected leaf-area change is itself the model’s response to warming and to precipitation change, so multiplying it by warming would place exposure on both sides of the same product and count it twice. Warming and projected change are therefore reported as two separate axes, and the summarising quantity is the projected decline alone: $$\begin{aligned} D_i = \max (-\Delta \textrm{LAI}_i, 0), \qquad R_i = D_i / \overline{\textrm{LAI}}_i , \end{aligned}$$ (6) where \(\Delta \textrm{LAI}_i\) is the projected change of cell i (2071–2099 minus 1991–2020), \(D_i\) is the absolute projected decline in \(m^{2} m^{-2}\) , set to zero wherever leaf area is projected to increase, and \(R_i\) is that decline relative to the cell’s present mean leaf area \(\overline{\textrm{LAI}}_i\) . Cities are grouped into five categories rather than ranked, because the ensemble described below shows that ordinal positions are not identifiable. Two thirds of matched cities have \(D = 0\) exactly, so percentiles taken over the whole set would place every cut-point inside that tie. The lowest category is therefore the exact-zero group (989 cities), and the remaining four are percentiles of D among the 507 cities with \(D > 0\) : Low for \(0 < D \le 0.109\) , Moderate for \(0.109< D \le 0.257\) , High for \(0.257< D \le 0.390\) and Very high for \(D > 0.390\) \(m^{2} m^{-2}\) , corresponding to the median, 80th and 95th percentiles of the positive values. Composite alternatives (the product and the mean of normalised warming and decline under five normalisation schemes, and an exposure-independent variant in which decline is divided by local warming before compositing) are computed only to document how far the resulting order depends on an arbitrary choice (ESM 1 , Table S33 ). Cities were taken from the Natural Earth 1:10 m populated places dataset with \(\mathrm {POP\_MAX} \ge {300,000}\) , where POP_MAX is that dataset’s maximum population estimate for a place, the largest of the city-proper and metropolitan figures it carries, and matched to the nearest urban cell within \(0.5^\circ \) ; 1,496 cities are retained, with a median matching distance of 0.203 \(^{\circ }\) . Every city is reported with its own coordinates, its cell’s coordinates, the matching distance, the cell identifier and the number of cities sharing that cell, because 114 cells host more than one city (237 cities in all), and those cities are not independent observations. Spread was quantified with a 60-member ensemble crossing 15 coherent climate draws with the four algorithms. Because that ensemble mixes structural variation with synthetic perturbations and is not a calibrated predictive distribution, member counts are reported throughout as ensemble frequencies and never as probabilities. Software and code Analyses used Python 3.11.9 with numpy 1.26.4, pandas 3.0.2, scipy 1.17.1, scikit-learn 1.6.1, xgboost 3.2.0, shap 0.48.0, geopandas 1.1.0 and cartopy 0.25.0. Random seeds are listed in ESM 1 . The complete code is provided in ESM 2 . Results Historical trends are weaker than an uncorrected test suggests Mean urban tree LAI follows a strong latitudinal gradient, highest in tropical cities and lowest in arid and high-latitude ones (Fig. 1 a). The trend result depends materially on how the test is specified (Table 1 ). A conventional Mann–Kendall test at \(p < 0.05\) returns 30.0% greening and 10.8% browning. Applying the Hamed–Rao correction for serial dependence and controlling the false discovery rate reduces these to 19.8% and 5.3%, a total of 25.2% of cells with a detectable trend (Fig. 1 b). The browning fraction is more than halved; the ratio of greening to browning rises from 30.0/10.8 to 3.7:1. Even that is likely to be generous. Replacing the analytical variance correction with a block bootstrap that estimates the null variance of S by resampling the detrended residuals, and applying the same FDR control to the whole domain, leaves only 18.3% of cells with a detectable trend (ESM 1 , Table S3 ). The reason is visible in the variance itself: the bootstrap null standard deviation of S averages 42.7 against 37.9 under independence, a 13% inflation, whereas the Hamed–Rao correction recovers a mean variance-inflation factor of only 1.16. The analytical correction therefore still leaves some liberality, and the figures we quote should be read as an upper bound on the detectable fraction. The result is insensitive to the completeness criterion, as expected given that 98.3% of cells have complete records (ESM 1 , Table S2 ). Trend-free pre-whitening gives a qualitatively different answer, with greening and browning almost equal. Pre-whitening is known to distort error rates when the record is short and persistence is strong (Serinaldi and Kilsby 2016), which is exactly this case ( \(T = 23\) , \(r_1 \approx 0.45\) ), so we do not adopt it; but the divergence is a genuine indication that the direction of change at individual cells is less firmly established than any single specification implies. Where trends are detected they are not small: the median Theil–Sen slope is \(+11.1\times 10^{-3}\) \(m^{2} m^{-2} yr^{-1}\) at greening cells and \(-21.2\times 10^{-3}\) \(m^{2} m^{-2} yr^{-1}\) at browning cells. The 95% interval on \(R^2\) is \(0.813\) – \(0.873\) when contiguous spatial blocks are resampled, which is the interval we quote throughout, for the reason given below. Three qualifications follow immediately. First, Random Forest is not uniformly best. It wins three of the five folds (Americas, Asia and Europe), while k -nearest neighbours is clearly better in Africa (0.704 against 0.675) and Gradient Boosting in Oceania (0.809 against 0.781); ESM 1 , Table S4 . Pooled over all cells the advantage over the next-best learner is small ( \(\Delta \) RMSE \(= 0.018\) \(m^{2} m^{-2}\) against XGBoost ), a signed-rank test across the five folds never reaches significance for any algorithm (ESM 1 , Table S6 ), and once the confidence intervals are computed on spatial blocks rather than on individual cells (below) the intervals of all four non-linear learners overlap. The claim that Random Forest is the best model is defensible only in the pooled sense, and we make it no more strongly than that. Second, the gap between non-linear and linear models is narrower than a comparison against ordinary ridge regression suggests. A spline-based additive model reaches \(R^2 = 0.766\) against \(0.713\) for Ridge, recovering more than half of the difference between the linear models and the ensembles. The remaining gap is consistent with interactions among predictors rather than with univariate non-linearity alone, so we describe the relationship as involving interactions and curvature rather than as “fundamentally non-linear”. Third, adding geographic coordinates makes the model worse, not better. This comparison holds the hyperparameter configuration fixed at the modal nested-CV choice, so that only the predictor set differs; the resulting climate-only value, \(R^2 = 0.849\) , is therefore slightly above the \(0.846\) quoted above, which uses the configuration selected independently inside each outer fold. Out-of-continent \(R^2\) falls from \(0.849\) to \(0.840\) when latitude and the sine and cosine of longitude are added, and the choice between a circular and a discontinuous longitude encoding changes the result by less than 0.001 (ESM 1 , Table S7 ). Coordinates carry nothing that the monthly climate does not already carry, and they slightly impede transfer to an unseen continent. They also change the projection, and in a direction that matters: because a coordinate is held fixed while climate is perturbed, any part of the spatial gradient attributed to position rather than to weather cannot respond to warming, and the geographic model damps the projected global mean under SSP5-8.5 and the high-latitude gain in particular (ESM 1 , Table S8 ). The two projections nonetheless correlate at \(r = 0.99\) and agree in sign at 96% of cells, so the choice affects magnitude more than pattern. Held-out climate zones are harder than held-out continents: pooled \(R^2\) falls to \(0.808\) when each Köppen–Geiger major group is withheld in turn (ESM 1 , Table S16 ). This is the more relevant benchmark for a projection that moves cells into climates they do not currently occupy. Residual diagnostics show the shrinkage expected of a bagged ensemble evaluated outside its training region, with positive residuals at low observed LAI and negative at high (ESM 1 , Table S13 and Fig. S2 ), and a mean bias of \(+0.047\) \(m^{2} m^{-2}\) . The same shrinkage appears when the residuals are stratified by held-out continent and by latitude band (ESM 1 , Tables S11 and S12 ). Moran’s I of the residuals is 0.717 against 0.938 for the response itself (ESM 1 , Table S14 ), so continental folds reduce but do not remove spatial dependence between training and test data (Wadoux et al. 2021). Ten random seeds change \(R^2\) by less than \(\pm 0.0007\) (ESM 1 , Table S10 ). That residual dependence has a direct consequence for how precisely the metrics can be quoted. Resampling individual cells assumes an independence the residuals contradict, and the resulting intervals are correspondingly optimistic. Resampling contiguous spatial blocks instead widens the 95% interval on \(R^2\) from \(\pm 0.0049\) to \(\pm 0.0301\) at a \(10^\circ \) block size, a factor of 6.2, and the width keeps growing with block size (ESM 1 , Table S9 ). We quote the block-resampled intervals in Table 2 for that reason. The practical effect is on comparisons rather than on the point estimates: under block resampling the intervals of all four non-linear learners overlap, so the ordering among them is not resolved by these data even though the gap to the linear models remains clear. The temperature response (Fig. 5 a) rises steeply through the cold part of the range and flattens as temperature increases. PD and ALE agree closely up to about 15 \(^{\circ }\text {C}\) ( \(+0.050\) against \(+0.047\) \(m^{2} m^{-2}\) \(^{\circ }\) C \(^{-1}\) over \(-15\) to \(5^{\circ }\text {C}\) ), but they part company at the warm end: over 25–32 \(^{\circ }\text {C}\) the PD slope is \(+0.005\) while the ALE slope is \(-0.045\) (ESM 1 , Table S26 ). ALE is the estimator to prefer here, because unlike PD it does not average the model over temperature–precipitation combinations that do not occur, and hot–wet combinations are exactly what the global predictor space lacks. Read through ALE, the fitted surface turns over rather than merely saturating above roughly 25 \(^{\circ }\text {C}\) . Nearly a fifth of urban cells (19.5%) currently sit in that range. Two cautions attach to this. The individual conditional expectation curves spread very widely at the warm end: at 25 \(^{\circ }\text {C}\) the 10th and 90th percentiles are 2.1 and 4.4 \(m^{2} m^{-2}\) , so the mean curve conceals most of the variation between cells. And a turning point in a statistical response surface fitted across space is not a physiological limit. It is consistent with one, and with the reported decline in tropical tree longevity above about 25 \(^{\circ }\text {C}\) (Locosselli et al. 2020), but this analysis contains no species and no physiology, so we identify no thermal optimum and no tolerance threshold. The aligned window is not short of climate signal: it samples a warming rate of +0.31 \(^{\circ }\text {C}\) per decade, in fact larger than the +0.23 \(^{\circ }\text {C}\) per decade separating the twentieth-century climatologies used in earlier space-for-time designs (ESM 1 , Table S31 ). What remains is that the observed record contains drivers the model omits (CO 2 fertilisation, planting and removal, land-cover change, management), and that the product’s own error, an RMSE of 1.03 \(m^{2} m^{-2}\) against field data, is large relative to the interannual signal being tested. Whatever the cause, the consequence stands and it is the central caveat of this study. A relationship fitted across space reproduces neither the magnitude nor the pattern of change within places over the satellite era. These projections should be read as the leaf-area distribution consistent with a given climatology, not as a forecast of what any particular city will experience, and they should not be used to prioritise municipal investment. A second Random Forest that predicts it from monthly temperature and precipitation is therefore partly recovering a function that was built in. The cross-validated \(R^2\) of 0.846 should be read in that light: it measures how faithfully our model reproduces a surface that another model generated, not how faithfully either reproduces urban canopies. The comparison that makes this concrete is that our RMSE against the product ( \(0.377\) \(m^{2} m^{-2}\) ) is smaller than the product’s own reported RMSE against field measurements (1.03 \(m^{2} m^{-2}\) ). Precision with respect to the product is not accuracy with respect to reality. Three things nevertheless remain informative, and they are what we claim. The historical trends use the product’s temporal dimension, which its climate inputs, a fixed 1970–2000 climatology, did not generate. The domain analysis is a statement about the model and the projected climate, independent of how the response was made. And the aligned transferability test of Section 2.7 sets the fitted spatial gradient against observed temporal change over identical windows, which the circularity does not compromise for the same reason: the product’s year-to-year variation was not manufactured from the climate covariates used here. It is the only one of the three that directly challenges the projection, and it fails plainly rather than marginally. Breaking the circularity properly requires a response variable that is not a climate-driven product: city-scale light detection and ranging (LiDAR) or very-high-resolution canopy inventories, or field LAI campaigns in urban settings. Until such data exist at global coverage, any global statistical projection of urban canopy structure will inherit this problem, and should say so. Divergence between climate zones is the robust result What survives the caveats is the zonal contrast, and even that is a statement about internal reproducibility across the evaluated algorithms rather than about external validity. The fitted model projects declines in tropical humid cities and gains at high latitudes and in cool-temperate zones, and the divergence widens with forcing. At the level of the individual grid cell the result is much weaker: the four algorithms agree on the sign at only 68.1% of cells under SSP5-8.5, and 5 of 24 sub-types change sign between scenarios. The two ends of the gradient also differ in how firmly they are established. Algorithm agreement reaches 96% in boreal cold-summer cells but only 65% in tropical rainforest cells, and it is the tropical cells that also carry the highest rates of climate novelty. The projected boreal gain is thus the better-supported half of the divergence, and the tropical loss, the half with the greater practical consequence, is the less certain. Any consistency claim should be confined to the broad contrast between warm-humid and cold climates, and stated with that asymmetry attached. The direction is physiologically plausible. Warming relieves a cold limitation where growing seasons are short and adds heat and evaporative stress where temperatures are already near the upper part of the observed range (Teskey et al. 2015 ; Grossiord et al. 2020 ), and tropical tree longevity declines above roughly 25 \(^{\circ }\text {C}\) mean annual temperature (Locosselli et al. 2020 ). Plausibility is not evidence, however: our analysis contains no physiology, and the flattening of the fitted response at high temperature is a property of a statistical surface fitted to a spatial gradient. It is consistent with a thermal constraint; it does not demonstrate one, and it certainly does not locate a threshold. If the pattern holds, its significance is distributional. Losses fall disproportionately on cities that currently have the most leaf area to lose and the greatest need for it, and gains accrue where cooling demand is lowest. We stop short of describing this as a redistribution of ecosystem services, because leaf-area density is only one input to service delivery and we have not modelled the services themselves. Extrapolation, uncertainty and what should not be mapped The applicability analysis changes how the maps should be used. Under SSP5-8.5 roughly 10.6% of cells present climates the model has never seen, most often through monthly temperatures above the training maximum, and tree ensembles cannot extrapolate through such conditions: they saturate at the boundary of the fitted response, which biases projected losses toward zero in exactly the hottest cities of greatest concern. That novelty is concentrated in arid (23.8%) and tropical (20.9%) cells rather than spread evenly. Combined with a total \(1\sigma \) that exceeds the signal at about half of all cells under SSP5-8.5 and at three quarters under SSP1-1.9, this means that scenario-mean maps convey more confidence than the analysis supports, and we have marked both conditions on every map rather than relying on a single stippling convention. What the budget covers should be stated as carefully as what it contains. It is a four-component model-and-climate budget: climate-ensemble spread, fitting, seed and algorithm choice. It excludes the error of the LAI product itself, the covariance structure between individual CMIP6 members, spatial covariance between cells, and the error of matching a city to a cell. The archive we used distributes ensemble summaries rather than individual model fields, so the climate term is a Gaussian emulation of ensemble spread rather than a true multi-model ensemble, and it yields no fraction-of-models-agreeing statistic. Nor are the four components guaranteed independent, which is what combining them in quadrature assumes; crossing the two largest explicitly gives a spread within 3% of the quadrature value, so the assumption is adequate for them, but we have not tested it for the smaller terms. The figures we report are a lower bound. Relatedly, “signal exceeds the combined \(1\sigma \) ” is a signal-to-noise statement, not a significance test, and should not be read as a 95% statement; it is conditional on the LAI product and on this modelling framework rather than a calibrated measure of predictive uncertainty. Why no vulnerability index We report projected decline and warming as separate quantities and form no composite. The reason is not presentational. Projected leaf-area change is itself the model’s response to warming and precipitation change, so any product of warming and that response places exposure on both sides and counts it twice; a composite built that way measures exposure more than it measures anything new. The alternative of dividing decline by local warming before compositing removes the double counting but becomes undefined wherever warming is small, which is much of the low-forcing domain. The sensitivity table shows how far the resulting order moves between specifications, which is the practical argument against reporting any of them as a result. A vulnerability index in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) sense (IPCC 2022) would in any case require an adaptive-capacity term that is independent of exposure, and this analysis contains none: municipal budgets for tree care, irrigation infrastructure, species composition and its replaceability, governance capacity, and the distribution of exposure within cities, all of them practical constraints that determine whether tree planting delivers its intended benefits at all (Pataki et al. 2021). Those data exist for some cities and not for most, which is itself a finding relevant to global assessment. Until they are assembled, the defensible city-scale statement is where leaf-area decline is projected to be largest, read at the level of broad categories rather than as an ordering. Limitations Beyond the circularity, the domain restriction and the failed temporal transfer already discussed, six limitations bound these results. The \(0.5^{\circ }\) resolution averages over whole metropolitan areas and cannot address within-city variation, which is where most urban forestry decisions are made. The model assumes the fitted relationship is stationary, an assumption that novel climates make less safe rather than more. CO 2 fertilisation, which could partly offset temperature-driven declines (Zhu et al. 2016 ), is absent, as are land-use change, urban expansion and management. The delta method assumes climate anomalies add to the historical climatology and ignores changes in interannual variability and extremes, which may matter more for canopies than changes in the mean. City-to-cell matching, though tightened and fully documented, still links a point to a \(0.5^{\circ }\) cell, and 237 cities share a cell with at least one other. Finally, the historical trends, though corrected, remain descriptive: we have not attributed them to afforestation, urbanisation or any other cause, and the associations between browning and rapidly urbanising regions that we observe are hypotheses for future work rather than findings. Conclusions We modelled the association between monthly climate normals and a satellite-informed machine-learning urban tree leaf area product across 21,590 urban grid cells and projected it into end-of-century climate under six scenarios. The most consequential finding is a contrast: the fitted relationship predicts where leaf area is, across continents and climate zones, far better than it predicts how leaf area moves through time within a place. Four conclusions follow, stated at the confidence the evidence supports. The most useful next step is not a better statistical model but a better response variable. A global urban canopy record that is not itself derived from climate, built from high-resolution imagery, LiDAR or municipal inventories, would make it possible to test, rather than assume, the relationship that every projection of this kind depends on. 1. Detected historical change is substantially smaller than an uncorrected analysis implies. With serial dependence and multiple testing handled, 19.8% of urban cells greened and 5.3% browned over 2000–2022; a block bootstrap that estimates the null variance by resampling rather than assuming a form for it gives 18.3% of cells with a detectable trend, against 25.2% for the analytical correction. 2. A model using monthly climate alone transfers across continents ( \(R^2 = 0.846\) ) and is degraded, not helped, by geographic coordinates, but it transfers less well across climate zones ( \(R^2 = 0.808\) ), which is the more relevant test for a projection. Random Forest is best in the pooled sense; two of five folds are won by other algorithms. 3. Projected changes diverge by climate zone, with losses in tropical humid cities and gains at high latitudes, and that contrast is reproduced by four different learning algorithms, though far more securely at the cold end (96% agreement) than the warm end (65%). Individual cells are far less settled: the four algorithms agree on the sign at only 68.1% of cells under SSP5-8.5, the quantified four-component uncertainty exceeds the projected signal at half of all cells under SSP5-8.5 and at three quarters under SSP1-1.9, and 10.6% of cells (rising to 23.8% in arid zones) lie outside the model’s domain of applicability under high forcing. 4. Tested on windows that coincide exactly with the observational record, the relationship fitted across space reproduces neither the magnitude nor the pattern of change within places, and it fails on three independent criteria: predicted and observed rates of change disagree in sign in the mean and correlate at only \(\rho = +0.087\) ; detrended annual anomalies have a median cell-wise correlation of -0.007, which is what independence would give; and a model calibrated on 2000–2011 recovers the change to 2012–2022 with \(R^2 = -1.115\) , worse than its own mean. The window is not short of climate signal, sampling +0.31 \(^{\circ }\text {C}\) of warming per decade. These projections therefore describe the leaf-area distribution consistent with a given climatology and are best read as the change implied by transferring a fitted spatial relationship into a future climate, not as forecasts with demonstrated temporal validity; they are not forecasts for individual cities, and should not be used to prioritise municipal investment without independent local evidence.
