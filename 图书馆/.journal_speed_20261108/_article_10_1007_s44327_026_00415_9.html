Abstract

Rapid peri-urban growth in Global South secondary cities consistently outpaces municipal public service delivery, generating severe spatial inequalities. This paper examines the spatial and institutional production of primary school infrastructure deficits in Adama City, Ethiopia. Deploying a mixed-method approach, this study integrates a GIS-based Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) site suitability model configured at a precise 30 m \(\:\times\:\) 30 m grid resolution, a 384-household socioeconomic survey, 12 key informant interviews with planning and education officials, and four focus group discussions with community leaders, parents, and teachers. The spatial model indicates that while 16.14% of municipal land is highly suitable for educational infrastructure, existing school infrastructure remains concentrated in saturated central cores rather than in these newly identified peripheral priority zones. This spatial mismatch coincides with severe overcrowding: the 12 schools identified as operating above capacity average 58 students per classroom. Analytically, spatial and field validation indicated that 73% of the area classified as underserved (by household, administrative, and stakeholder indicators) fell within model-identified high-suitability zones; because the validation indicators and the model criteria draw on related conditions, this is evidence of convergence rather than fully independent validation. Qualitative findings suggest that accessibility deficits are shaped not merely by geographic friction, but also by institutional land-reservation failures, unauthorized land-use conversions, and un-synchronized capital investment cycles. This study contributes by indicating how spatial decision-support tools may need to be coupled with institutional governance reforms to enforce land-use zoning and mitigate infrastructure lag in rapidly expanding secondary cities. For Adama and comparable Global South secondary cities, the findings suggest that GIS-AHP suitability outputs should be embedded within binding land-reservation and inter-agency coordination mechanisms before, rather than after, peripheral settlement occurs.

1 Introduction

Rapid urbanization has intensified pressure on public school infrastructure across many secondary cities in Sub-Saharan Africa, where population growth frequently exceeds the pace of formal urban service development [1, 2]. Among the most affected sectors is primary education: expanding residential neighborhoods often emerge without corresponding investments in school infrastructure, producing overcrowded classrooms, extended travel distances, and deepening inequalities in educational opportunity. Ensuring equitable access to primary education has consequently become both a planning and social development challenge in rapidly urbanizing cities [3, 4].

Recent urban planning scholarship increasingly frames unequal access to public services as a question of spatial justice rather than solely a technical problem of geographic distribution. Spatial justice perspectives emphasize that access to infrastructure is shaped by political, institutional, and economic processes that influence where resources are allocated and which populations benefit from urban development [5,6,7]. In rapidly expanding secondary cities, unequal access to primary education therefore reflects broader processes of peripheralization, delayed school investment, and uneven territorial investment rather than simply deficiencies in service distribution [8, 9]. Educational accessibility is increasingly understood as a multidimensional concept shaped by interactions among land use, transport systems, mobility opportunities, and institutional decision-making. Accessibility outcomes depend not only on physical proximity to services but also on the organization of urban space and the capacity of infrastructure systems to respond to demographic change [10]. In peripheral urban environments, fragmented road networks, weak pedestrian infrastructure, and limited transport services frequently constrain school access even where straight-line distances appear relatively short. Consequently, educational service disparities often emerge through the interaction between urban growth patterns and uneven school investment rather than through distance alone [10,11,12]. Recent GIS-based accessibility studies similarly demonstrate that educational inequalities emerge through interactions among transport systems, settlement morphology, and service distribution patterns rather than geographic proximity alone [7, 11].

Research from rapidly urbanizing contexts demonstrates that infrastructure investment rarely expands at the same pace as residential growth. Peri-urban settlements commonly experience delayed service provision because planning systems struggle to anticipate both the speed and spatial direction of urban expansion [13]. These dynamics are particularly evident in many Sub-Saharan African cities, where fragmented administrative systems, limited institutional coordination, and delayed capital investment frequently produce persistent accessibility disparities between established urban cores and expanding peripheral neighborhoods [14, 15]. Recent studies of peri-urban expansion further show that infrastructure deficits intensify where institutional planning systems fail to synchronize service provision with outward urban growth, particularly in rapidly urbanizing African city-regions [1, 16]. As urban development extends outward, educational facilities, transport networks, and other social services often remain concentrated in older districts, generating cumulative disadvantages for newly urbanizing communities [17]. Equity-oriented planning frameworks therefore argue that infrastructure allocation should be evaluated not only by efficiency but also by fairness and inclusion [8, 11]. From this perspective, unequal school accessibility represents more than a service delivery problem; it reflects how planning systems prioritize, sequence, and distribute public investment across urban space [8]. Educational infrastructure planning consequently becomes both a spatial and governance challenge, particularly in rapidly growing cities where institutional responses frequently lag behind settlement expansion [9, 15].

Existing geospatial literature heavily prioritizes technical optimizations of facility siting, frequently treating urban space as an open, friction-free plane [14, 18]. This approach creates a critical gap: standard suitability models overlook the political economy of land allocation and the institutional mechanisms that cause master plans to deviate from reality. In secondary cities of the Global South, infrastructure lag is treated as an inevitable mathematical consequence of demographic expansion, rather than a symptom of fragmented municipal governance and weak land-tenure enforcement.

Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) have become widely used tools for infrastructure planning because they enable planners to integrate demographic, environmental, accessibility, and land-use variables within unified analytical frameworks. Among MCDA approaches, the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) is frequently applied because it provides transparent procedures for weighting planning criteria and supporting structured spatial decision-making [19, 20]. GIS-based suitability analysis has consequently been applied across diverse planning domains, including educational infrastructure, healthcare accessibility, transport planning, and land allocation [7, 21, 22]. Recent studies increasingly integrate GIS-based accessibility modeling with equity-oriented planning objectives to identify educational service disparities and support more socially responsive infrastructure allocation [7, 11]. Although GIS-based suitability approaches are well established, existing applications remain concentrated within metropolitan regions and frequently prioritize technical optimization over institutional and governance dimensions of infrastructure allocation. Comparatively less attention has been directed toward rapidly expanding secondary cities in Sub-Saharan Africa, where fragmented governance systems, informal expansion, and delayed school investment interact to produce persistent uneven access to schooling [14, 15]. These conditions create planning environments in which technical suitability alone may not adequately capture where service deficits are most severe. Recent educational accessibility studies increasingly emphasize combining spatial optimization with equity-oriented planning objectives, arguing that infrastructure prioritization should account for both technical suitability and socially uneven service outcomes [7, 11]. Recent investigation on Ethiopian urban political economy further situates these dynamics within national land-tenure and administrative-response frameworks, showing how informal settlement and land-tenure insecurity are shaped by asymmetric relations among land brokers, residents, and municipal agents in newly established Ethiopian cities [23].

Adama City provides an appropriate case for examining these dynamics. As one of Ethiopia’s rapidly growing secondary cities, Adama City has experienced sustained residential expansion, particularly within peripheral and peri-urban neighborhoods [18]. However, educational infrastructure development has not expanded at comparable rates, contributing to substantial variation in accessibility across neighborhoods. Similar patterns have been documented across rapidly growing African cities, where school investment frequently lags behind population growth, resulting in overcrowded schools, longer travel distances, and uneven access to educational opportunities [14, 15]. Examining these dynamics in Adama City therefore provides an opportunity to understand how spatial planning tools can support more equitable infrastructure allocation under conditions of settlement growth and institutional constraint.

Adama City growth since the early 2000s has been driven less by organic demographic increase than by its position on the Addis Ababa-Djibouti trade corridor, which has attracted manufacturing investment, logistics operations, and migrant labor from across Oromia. This economic role accelerated outward residential expansion into peripheral gandas, where housing construction has consistently outpaced the extension of public services. Gandas (or small urban jurisdictions) such as Boku, Ganda Gara, and Dhekaa Adii Wereda have absorbed the fastest growth and roughly when expansion accelerated. In several rapidly expanding eastern and southeastern neighborhoods, residential subdivision, much of it informal or only partially regularized, has occurred several years before any corresponding school was built, leaving newly settled households dependent on distant, overcrowded facilities in the urban core (Fig. 1). Key informant interviews with municipal planners indicate that this lag is not incidental: land parcels originally designated for social-service use within approved structure plans have in multiple cases been converted to residential or commercial development as expansion pressure increased, a pattern consistent with broader documentation of weak structure-plan enforcement across Oromia secondary cities [18]. The pattern of agricultural land conversion and informal subdivision preceding formal infrastructure. Understanding Adama City accessibility deficits therefore requires attention not only to where population has grown, but to how the city’s land-administration and planning institutions have or have not protected space for the services that growth requires. Consequently, these structural conditions dictate, this study addresses the following research questions:

  1. 1.

    How does the current spatial distribution of primary schools correspond with patterns of residential expansion and population growth in Adama City?

  2. 2.

    Which spatial conditions contribute most to unequal access to primary education across rapidly urbanizing neighborhoods?

  3. 3.

    How can GIS-based multi-criteria decision analysis support more equitable and analytically informed primary school planning in Adama City?

The contribution of this study is primarily methodological and empirical rather than resting on the novelty of GIS-AHP as an analytical technique, which is well established elsewhere. Methodologically, the contribution lies in integrating GIS-AHP suitability modeling within a single mixed-method framework alongside household accessibility experiences, key-informant and stakeholder validation, and spatial-justice framing, paired with sensitivity and field-based validation to strengthen interpretation under conditions of planning-data scarcity. Empirically, the study provides evidence, for a rapidly urbanizing Ethiopian secondary city, that accessibility deficits appear to be shaped not only by spatial mismatch between population growth and school siting, but also by land-reservation failure and delayed institutional response — evidence generated by triangulating spatial suitability outputs against household and stakeholder accounts. The study thereby offers a transferable decision-support framework for infrastructure prioritization in rapidly urbanizing, data-scarce secondary cities.

2 Materials and methods

2.1 Study area

Adama City is a rapidly growing secondary city in the Oromia Region of Ethiopia, situated at approximately 8.54°N latitude and 39.27°E longitude at an elevation of approximately 1,712 m above sea level [24]. Located approximately 99 km southeast of Addis Ababa and connected by the Addis Ababa-Adama corridor, the city functions as a strategic node for transportation, commerce, and regional development, as shown in Fig. 2. Its geographical setting, at the base of a slope to the west and the Great Rift Valley to the east, is characterized by relatively flat terrain transitioning to Rift Valley features [25]. Sustained industrialization, strategic positioning along the Addis Ababa-Djibouti trade corridor, and migration-driven population growth have made Adama one of Ethiopia’s fastest-growing secondary cities. Population for spatial analysis was drawn from the 2024 WorldPop gridded dataset (347,833 residents), selected over general 2025 projections because it provides high-resolution spatial density surfaces required for precise suitability mapping. Because age-disaggregated population surfaces at comparable spatial resolution were not available for Adama City at the time of analysis, total resident population was used as a proxy for school-age demand. This is a limitation: if the age structure of peripheral and central neighborhoods differs systematically, total-population weighting could over- or under-state demand-driven suitability in specific zones. This possible spatial bias is revisited in the Limitations section alongside a recommendation to substitute school-age-specific population surfaces as these become available. Adama City’s estimated 2024 population of 521,680 reflects sustained industrialization, transport-linked investment, and rural-to-urban migration [26, 27]. Therefore, this WorldPop-derived estimate should be interpreted cautiously: Ethiopia has not conducted a national census since 2007, and independent estimates of Adama City Administration, the city’s population range up to and beyond 500,000. The figure used here is a modeled approximation rather than an enumerated count, which is itself a limitation of the demand-weighting approach.

2.2 Data used

The study integrated both spatial and socioeconomic datasets. Spatial datasets included road networks, land-use maps, digital elevation models (DEM), administrative boundaries, and existing primary school locations, obtained from municipal spatial databases and remote sensing sources processed in ArcGIS 10.8, as shown in Table 1. Socioeconomic data included population census records, municipal population projections, and residential density information. Population density surfaces were generated using WorldPop gridded datasets (WorldPop, 2024). All spatial datasets were standardized, geo-referenced, and converted to raster format to support multi-criteria suitability analysis. Processed datasets were subsequently reclassified based on suitability criteria and integrated into the weighted overlay model.

2.3 Methods of data collection

Selection criteria were derived from the established literature on educational facility planning, urban accessibility, and GIS-based land suitability analysis, as shown in Fig. 3. Five criteria were selected to represent three interrelated planning dimensions: demographic demand, spatial accessibility, and physical feasibility. Although additional variables such as school capacity, public transport accessibility, land ownership status, environmental hazards, and school-age population structure can also influence access to primary education, these datasets were unavailable and lacked sufficient spatial consistency across the study area. The selected criteria therefore reflect a balance between conceptual relevance, data availability, and methodological feasibility appropriate to a data-constrained urban planning environment. Road networks represent accessibility because a school is only useful if children can reach it safely. Population density captures demand, a factor consistently emphasized in Ethiopian urban studies as a key driver of public facility needs [28]. Land-use data were used to exclude incompatible development zones, while slope and terrain information were used to identify physically unsuitable sites from the outset [16]. Previous educational accessibility studies have also emphasized the importance of minimizing service overlap while improving accessibility in underserved neighborhoods [11].

A mixed-methods survey design was employed, incorporating questionnaires, direct observations, key informant interviews (KIIs), and focus group discussions (FGDs). Qualitative participants were purposively recruited based on their direct knowledge or experience of primary-school accessibility, educational service provision, urban planning, or GIS-based planning. KII participants were urban planners and land-administration officials (Bureau of Urban Planning, Land Development and Management Bureau, Cadaster and Cartography Office, and sub-city planning offices), Education Bureau officials, and school administrators; FGD participants were community leaders, parents, and teachers. The recruitment process sought to capture perspectives from both central and peripheral neighborhoods and from participants with different institutional and experiential backgrounds. In total, 12 KIIs and 4 FGDs (6–10 participants per session) were conducted; these final totals are used consistently in the Abstract, Table 2, and throughout the manuscript. The six experts involved in the AHP pairwise-comparison exercises had between 6 and 18 years of professional experience in urban planning, educational infrastructure planning, cadastral administration, and GIS analysis. Their selection emphasized institutional and disciplinary diversity to reduce potential bias in the weighting process.

The household survey was conducted between January and March 2025. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. For the qualitative component, KIIs were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide covering school accessibility, overcrowding, spatial disparities in service provision, barriers to accessing schools, and perceptions of underserved areas. FGDs followed a semi-structured discussion guide addressing similar issues while allowing participants to introduce additional experiences and concerns. Representing formal and informal households across central-commercial and peri-urban neighborhoods. KIIs lasted 45–60 min and FGDs 60–90 min, conducted in Amharic or Afaan Oromo and audio-recorded with consent and translated into English for analysis; where recording was not permitted, detailed field notes were taken. Transcripts were checked against the recordings and field notes to improve accuracy and preserve the meaning of participants’ responses.

Qualitative data were analyzed thematically. The analysis began with repeated reading of transcripts and field notes to establish familiarity with the data, followed by identification of meaningful text segments and assignment of initial codes. Codes were developed both deductively from the study objectives and interview/FGD guides and inductively from issues that emerged directly from participants’ accounts. Related codes were subsequently compared, grouped, and refined into broader categories, from which the main themes and subthemes were developed. Particular attention was given to recurring patterns concerning school accessibility, overcrowding, spatial inequity, travel barriers, and gaps in educational service provision. Coding was conducted manually by three coders; the procedure corresponds to the phases of thematic analysis described by Braun and Clarke [29]. Independently coded transcripts were compared and disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus; agreement was therefore established by consensus rather than by a formal inter-coder statistic.

The coding unit was a meaningful text segment (from a phrase to a short passage) expressing a single idea. A preliminary codebook was drafted deductively from the study objectives and the interview and FGD guides, organized around six code families (school accessibility and walking distance; overcrowding and capacity; land reservation and land-use conversion; capital budgeting and inter-agency coordination; peripheral settlement and informality; and travel barriers and safety), and was extended inductively as new codes emerged from participants’ accounts. Each code was recorded with a label and a short description, and additions, merges, and deletions were logged in the audit trail. Related codes were grouped into categories and then into five themes: (1) peripheral access barriers and spatial inequity; (2) school overcrowding and capacity pressure; (3) land-reservation failure; (4) pressure-driven conversion of social-service land; and (5) unsynchronized capital investment and weak inter-agency coordination. The coding frame, with illustrative codes and the principal data source for each theme, is given in Appendix A (Table A1).

Data collection and analysis continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which additional interviews or focus-group discussions produced no substantially new codes, concepts, or themes. Saturation was assessed by comparing emerging codes and themes across successive interviews and FGDs. The credibility of the qualitative findings was enhanced through triangulation of KIIs, FGDs, household survey responses, and field observations, comparison of perspectives across different participant groups and geographic areas, repeated review of transcripts and codes, and member checking. Maintaining an audit trail of coding decisions and revisions also helped ensure transparency and consistency throughout the analysis.

The study followed established ethical principles for social science research, including voluntary participation, informed consent, confidentiality, and anonymity. Participants were informed about the objectives of the research and their right to decline participation or withdraw from the study at any stage. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all responses were used solely for academic research purposes. Ethical approval details (approving committee and applicable guidelines) are provided in the Declarations section. The household questionnaire addressed walking distance to schools, travel conditions, perceived accessibility challenges, school overcrowding, and neighborhood-level service adequacy. Respondents were drawn from both central and peripheral gandas to ensure broader spatial representation.

2.4 Accessibility analysis and distance modeling

To evaluate road accessibility across peripheral expansion zones, straight-line Euclidean distance surfaces were computed. While network analysis offers superior precision in highly structured urban cores [3, 10], incomplete or un-cadastered road networks in Adama City’s rapidly emerging peripheral settlements render comprehensive graph-network modeling mathematically unviable. The Euclidean distance layout serves as an operational proxy, establishing a conservative baseline of accessibility metrics. It is important to distinguish three related but non-equivalent measures used in this study: straight-line distance to the nearest road segment, straight-line distance to the nearest existing school, and actual network or travel accessibility (the real route a resident would walk or travel). Only the first two are computed directly from the GIS layers; the third is not measured here and is instead approximated by the Euclidean surfaces. Euclidean distance simplifies actual travel conditions in peri-urban environments where mobility is shaped by informal roads, pedestrian safety, and seasonal accessibility constraints [30]. Two factors, however, make this choice methodologically defensible in the Adama City context.

First, many expansion zones in the city lack a formalized or fully digitized street hierarchy. A network-based approach would therefore be technically unfeasible or prone to significant data gaps in precisely the peripheral areas the study seeks to analyze. Second, Euclidean distance serves as a measure of theoretical proximity which is the minimum possible distance between two locations. If accessibility appears poor under this conservative, best-case-scenario metric, the actual situation is, in most cases, likely to be at least as poor once fragmented connectivity, physical barriers, and informal circulation networks are accounted for. However, the surfaces should not be read as a strict universal lower bound, since informal shortcuts or parallel access points could occasionally shorten effective travel relative to the straight-line calculation. The Euclidean surfaces generated here should therefore be interpreted as conservative lower bounds on accessibility deprivation rather than precise travel-time estimations. A cost-distance or friction-surface model incorporating slope and land cover as impedance factors would represent a stronger alternative to isotropic Euclidean buffering, since it can partially account for barriers such as drainage channels, steep terrain, and rail lines even without a fully attributed road network. This was not implemented here because reliable slope- and land-cover-based friction coefficients specific to Adama City’s informal periphery were not available within the study timeframe, and it is flagged as the priority direction for methodological refinement. This limitation is revisited in Sect.  4.3.

2.5 Sampling and household survey design

A mixed-methods sampling strategy was employed to complement the spatial analysis with ground-level socio-spatial validation. The household survey captured school accessibility experiences, perceived distance to primary schools, travel time, mode of transport, and perceived adequacy of existing school distribution. Respondents also provided information on neighborhood-level access constraints, including road conditions, safety concerns, and enrollment pressure in nearby schools.

The target population comprised residents in both central and peri-urban gandas of Adama City. A total sample of 384 households was determined using the Cochran [31] formula for large populations (95% confidence level, 5% margin of error). The final sample was proportionally distributed across selected neighborhoods based on population density and spatial coverage. Systematic random sampling was applied within each ganda to ensure spatial representativeness and reduce selection bias. The sampling frame was constructed from the most recent Adama City Administration household/ganda register. Of Adama City’s 14 gandas, 9 were purposively selected to represent a mix of central/peripheral and formally planned/informally expanded settlement types. Within each selected ganda, respondents were allocated proportional to its estimated household count, and a systematic sampling interval (k) was calculated as \(\:k\:=\:N/n\), where N is the ganda’s sampling-frame household count and n is its allocated sample size. The first respondent was selected using a random start between 1 and k, with every kth household thereafter selected along a fixed walking direction. Where a selected household was unavailable after 3 revisit attempts, or a resident declined, the interviewer proceeded to the next household in sequence. The questionnaire was developed from the literature cited in the Introduction, translated into Afaan Oromo and Amharic, and pretested with 65 households before full deployment. Enumerators were trained ASTU research assistants who received 5 days of structured training on the questionnaire, consent procedures, and standardized administration. KIIs (n = 12) and FGDs (4 sessions), described in Sect.  2.3, were used to triangulate household-level responses. Qualitative data collection and coding followed the protocol described in Sect.  2.3. KIIs and FGDs were arranged through the Bureau of Urban Planning and the Cadaster and Cartography Office and were conducted in Afaan Oromo and Amharic by trained research assistants, audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English. Thematic saturation was judged to have been reached when no new themes emerged across the final two to three sessions. Credibility was supported by investigator triangulation across the three coders, member-checking of preliminary themes with a subset of KII participants, and triangulation of the qualitative findings against the spatial suitability outputs and household survey results.

Survey data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) to summarize accessibility conditions and perceived service gaps. Table 2 presents the results of the thematic analysis of qualitative data from KIIs and FGDs, highlighting recurring patterns related to school accessibility, overcrowding, and spatial inequity.

The household and stakeholder data served two key functions in relation to the GIS model. These are validation of spatial outputs by comparing perceived accessibility with GIS-derived accessibility zones, and contextual interpretation of model results particularly in identifying underserved neighborhoods and confirming areas of high service pressure. This integration ensured that the suitability model was grounded in lived accessibility experiences and institutional planning realities.

Importantly, institutional governance dynamics were not directly rasterized into the GIS weighted overlay model. Rather, the spatial model was deployed to isolate strictly objective physical, environmental, and demographic suitability. Institutional barriers, policy failures, and land-use conversions were interpreted through triangulated qualitative evidence from KIIs, FGDs, and document reviews, explaining why the optimal configurations identified by the spatial model have failed to materialize in practice.

2.6 Data analysis tools and AHP weight derivation

Spatial data processing and analysis were conducted using ArcGIS 10.8 (ESRI), IDRISI Selva, and Microsoft Office applications. ArcGIS 10.8 supported spatial preprocessing, raster conversion, proximity analysis, weighted overlay operations, and map production, while the polygon-based spatial overlap validation reported later was conducted separately in ArcGIS Pro 3.2, reflecting a later stage of the analysis workflow. IDRISI Selva was used to generate AHP pairwise comparison matrices and calculate criterion weights. Vector layers were explicitly projected into the Adindan/UTM Zone 37 N coordinate system prior to raster conversion and spatial overlay execution to ensure spatial consistency. Vector datasets were converted to raster format at a common 30 m \(\:\times\:\) 30 m resolution to maintain compatibility during overlay analysis [32]. Datasets originating at coarser native resolutions (for example, the WorldPop population surface) were resampled to the common 30 m \(\:\times\:\) 30 m grid using bilinear interpolation for continuous surfaces and nearest-neighbor for categorical layers. Resampling aligns all criteria within a shared raster extent and cell size but does not itself improve the underlying precision of coarser-resolution inputs. All rasters were clipped and snapped to a common extent and origin corresponding to the Adama City administrative boundary; cells outside available data coverage were coded as NoData and excluded from the overlay calculation. All criteria layers were then reclassified into standardized suitability categories on a 1–9 scale.

Criterion weights were derived through the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) using pairwise comparisons completed by six domain experts drawn from urban planning, municipal land administration, and educational infrastructure institutions: two municipal urban planners, one education bureau infrastructure officer, one cadastral specialist, and two GIS professionals. Individual pairwise comparison matrices were aggregated using the geometric mean method to reduce the influence of individual bias. Each expert’s individual pairwise comparison matrix was first checked for internal consistency (individual CR < 0.10) before aggregation; all six individually satisfied CR < 0.10 and only matrices meeting this threshold were included in the geometric-mean aggregation. The relative importance of each criterion was evaluated using Saaty [19] pairwise comparison scale, and the resulting weights were normalized to produce the final criterion hierarchy. Pairwise matrices were aggregated using the geometric mean because it preserves reciprocal properties required in group decision-making frameworks and reduces dominance effects from individual respondents.

Each expert completed all 10 pairwise comparisons among the five criteria (n(n − 1)/2 with n = 5) on Saaty’s 1–9 scale, and reciprocal values were assigned to the lower triangle. For every criterion pair, the six individual judgments were combined by the element-wise geometric mean: the aggregated judgment aijG is the sixth root of the product of the six experts’ judgments aij(k), k = 1, …, 6. This preserves the reciprocal property ajiG = 1/aijG [33]. The aggregated 5 × 5 matrix is reported in Appendix B (Table B1). Criterion weights were obtained as the normalized principal eigenvector of the aggregated matrix (IDRISI Selva Weight module), so that the weights sum to 1. The maximum eigenvalue (λmax), the consistency index CI = (λmax − n)/(n − 1), and the consistency ratio CR = CI/RI were computed from the same matrix, using the random index RI = 1.12 for n = 5 [32]. A CR of 0.04 therefore corresponds to CI ≈ 0.045 and λmax ≈ 5.18. The reported CR = 0.04 refers to the aggregated matrix; the CR < 0.10 screening was applied separately to each expert’s matrix before aggregation, and the six individual values are given in Table B2 (Appendix B).

The final AHP-derived weights were: population density (32.4%), road accessibility (26.3%), slope/physical conditions (21.0%), land-use compatibility (10.4%), and proximity to existing schools (9.9%). The consistency ratio of the aggregated matrix (CR = 0.04; individual expert values in Table B2) satisfies the accepted threshold of CR < 0.10 [34], indicating an acceptable degree of internal logical consistency among the six experts’ pairwise judgments. This consistency measure does not eliminate subjectivity or bias in the underlying judgments; it indicates only that each expert’s own comparisons were not internally contradictory, not that the resulting shared weights are free of bias. The high weighting assigned to population density reflects the central role of demographic concentration in shaping educational demand within rapidly urbanizing neighborhoods. This should not be read as implying that accessibility, land-use compatibility, or physical suitability are unimportant; the weighting hierarchy instead reflects the immediate planning pressures associated with rapid peri-urban population growth and school overcrowding observed during field investigations.

The weighted overlay was computed cell by cell as Sj = Cj × (w1 × 1j + w2 × 2j + … + w5 × 5j), where xij is the 1–9 score of criterion i in cell j, wi is its AHP weight (Table 13), and Cj is a binary constraint mask equal to 0 for protected/conservation areas, active industrial zones, and floodplain/hazard buffers and 1 elsewhere. The continuous score (range 1–9) was rounded to the nearest integer to give the nine-class scale of Table 7; cells scoring 7.5 or above (classes 8–9) define the Highly Suitable areas, and constrained cells (Cj = 0) were assigned to the Restricted class.

Sensitivity analysis showed that the model remained relatively stable under ± 10% changes in criterion weights. In each scenario, the weight of the tested criterion was multiplied by 1.10 or 0.90 and the other four weights were rescaled proportionally so that the weights still summed to 1 (Table 3). Increasing or decreasing the population-density weight by 10% changed the priority-area proportion from 16.14% in the base model to 16.39% and 15.89%, respectively, representing a maximum change of ± 0.25% points. In contrast, equivalent adjustments to the road-accessibility weight resulted in priority areas of 16.19% and 16.09%, corresponding to changes of only ± 0.05% points. The slope, land-use compatibility, and proximity-to-existing-schools’ criteria were subjected to the same ± 10% sensitivity procedure and showed comparatively minor spatial variation; consequently, their results are summarized narratively rather than presented as separate scenarios in Table 3. Overall, the sensitivity analysis indicates that the model is relatively robust, although population density exerts greater influence on the spatial distribution of priority areas than the other tested criteria.

2.7 GIS model validation

The suitability model was validated through three complementary approaches: sensitivity testing, spatial overlap analysis, and field-based verification. Sensitivity testing involved adjusting each of the five criteria weights by ± 10% and observing changes in the spatial distribution of priority areas, as presented in Fig. 4. The spatial distribution reveals that highly suitable zones are heavily concentrated along peripheral expansion corridors, contrasting with saturated central cores (Fig. 5; Table 14). The results confirm that the model is relatively stable under moderate weighting assumptions.

Spatial validation used overlay intersection analysis between GIS-generated priority areas and underserved neighborhoods identified through household surveys, municipal education records, KIIs, FGDs, and field observations. Underserved areas were defined by three indicators: overcrowded schools, excessive walking distance to the nearest school, and stakeholder-confirmed service shortages. Polygon intersection analysis in ArcGIS revealed a 73% spatial overlap between identified priority areas and documented underserved regions (Tables 4 and 5). The 73% spatial overlap between technical suitability outputs and community-reported deficits was calculated by intersecting raster zones classified as Highly Suitable (scores 8–9) with vector polygons mapping the spatial perimeters of underserved gandas derived from the stratified household survey.

Underserved polygons were delineated by aggregating household survey responses to the ganda level and flagging any ganda in which (i) reported average walking time to the nearest primary school exceeded 30 min, (ii) municipal records classified at least one serving school as overcrowded, or (iii) FGD participants explicitly identified the area as underserved; gandas meeting at least two of these three criteria were classified as underserved and dissolved into a single polygon layer. Overlap (%) was then computed as (Area of Intersection between Highly Suitable zones and Underserved polygons \(\:\div\) Total Area of Underserved polygons) \(\:\times\:\) 100. To assess the robustness of this classification, ganda-level results were additionally cross-tabulated across the three underserved criteria, allowing the degree of agreement and disagreement among household, administrative, and community-based indicators to be reported. This statistic is directional: it measures the share of the underserved area covered by Highly Suitable cells and does not penalize Highly Suitable cells lying outside underserved gandas; the Jaccard index below provides a symmetric measure.

To further quantify agreement between GIS-generated priority areas and independently identified underserved neighborhoods, intersection-based similarity and classification agreement metrics were computed. Spatial overlap analysis was first conducted in ArcGIS Pro 3.2 using polygon intersection and union operations. Model validation was conducted using sensitivity analysis, spatial overlap assessment, the Jaccard similarity coefficient [35], and Cohen’s Kappa statistic [36]. The Jaccard similarity coefficient was calculated as \(\:\text{J}\:=\:|\text{A}\:\cap\:\:\text{B}|\:/\:|\text{A}\:\cup\:\:\text{B}|\), where A is the set of gandas classified high-priority by the GIS-AHP model and B is the set independently classified as underserved from household/institutional evidence. Cohen’s Kappa was calculated from the corresponding 2\(\:\:\times\:\:\)2 agreement table, correcting observed agreement for chance. Because both the GIS suitability criteria (particularly population density and road accessibility) and the independent validation indicators (walking distance, overcrowding) draw on related underlying conditions, some conceptual overlap between predictor and validation measures is expected. The validation exercise is therefore best understood as a test of convergence between an ex-ante spatial model and ex-post household/institutional reporting rather than as fully independent external validation ‒ a distinction now made explicit as a limitation. Agreement between modeled priority areas and the separately compiled underserved neighborhoods was moderate to substantial (Jaccard = 0.61; Cohen’s Kappa = 0.63). The Kappa value falls in the 0.61–0.80 band labelled “substantial” by Landis and Koch [37], although such bands are conventions rather than tests, and no comparable benchmark exists for the Jaccard index. The results therefore suggest that the model captures much, but not all, of the observed pattern of educational accessibility deprivation (Table 4). The 2 × 2 agreement table underlying both statistics, including the unit of analysis, is given in Appendix B (Table B3).

This overlap is consistent with the suitability criteria capturing part of the geography of service gaps, but the design cannot show which criteria drive the agreement: population density carries the largest weight, and the underserved indicators (walking distance, overcrowding) are related to the same demand and accessibility conditions. While no universal benchmark exists for spatial overlap validation in educational accessibility studies, the observed correspondence indicates moderate-to-substantial agreement between model outputs and the separately compiled service-deficit locations. The remaining divergence may reflect temporal mismatches between rapidly changing settlement patterns and available planning datasets.

The 27% of the underserved area not covered by Highly Suitable cells may partly reflect neighborhoods where informal expansion occurred after the most recent land-use data were compiled. This explanation was not tested directly, and other contributors, such as criteria not included in the model (school capacity, pedestrian safety) or the ganda-level aggregation of underserved polygons, cannot be excluded. The overlap is therefore consistent with, but not proof of, the planning lag central to this study’s argument. Field verification confirmed that many high-suitability zones were located in rapidly expanding peripheral neighborhoods characterized by limited school access, overcrowded classrooms, and inadequate educational infrastructure coverage. In Boku, Ganda Gara, and other rapidly expanding eastern neighborhoods, residential construction advanced considerably faster than public educational investment. Interviews with municipal planners indicated that land originally reserved for social infrastructure was frequently converted to residential use under development pressure, leaving newly settled households dependent on overcrowded schools located several kilometers away. Municipal education office records identified 12 schools operating above capacity, with an average student-classroom ratio of 58:1, as shown in Table 5. Household surveys indicated that average walking distance to the nearest primary school was 2.4 km in peripheral neighborhoods compared to 0.9 km in central urban areas.

Key informant interviews with municipal urban planners provided direct institutional context for these patterns. Planners interviewed for this study indicated that land originally designated for social-service use in approved structure plans had in several cases been reassigned to residential development as expansion pressure increased, and that the Education Bureau’s facility budget was not adjusted to match the pace of peripheral settlement.

3 Results

3.1 Spatial distribution of primary schools relative to residential expansion

The spatial distribution of land use in Adama City reflects a pronounced contrast between concentrated commercial activity within the urban core and dispersed residential expansion toward peri-urban areas, as shown in Fig. 1. Outward residential growth has not been matched by proportional expansion of educational infrastructure, producing uneven accessibility conditions across the city. In rapidly expanding eastern and southeastern gandas including areas such as Dhekaa Adii Wereda referenced later in Fig. 6 residential construction has advanced well ahead of school provision. FGD participants in these neighborhoods specifically described having to send children to overcrowded schools several kilometers away. Peripheral neighborhoods more broadly consistently experience longer walking distances, weaker transport connectivity, and limited educational service coverage.

(Fig. 7) social services currently account for only 2.11% (6.24 km²) of total urban land use in Adama City is far below the 10–20% threshold mandated by the Ministry of Urban Development and Construction in 2012 for sustainable urban service delivery [18].

Analysis of the 67 primary schools identified in the study area reveals that most are concentrated within the established urban core, leaving peripheral and peri-urban residential areas poorly served relative to their growing populations, as shown in Fig. 8. Key informant interviews with urban planners and focus group discussions with teachers, parents, and community leaders indicated that peripheral residential growth, often into informal zones, tends to outpace formal facility construction; participants linked this to the long walking distances reported by households.

3.2 Primary school enrollment growth and spatial service pressure

Cumulative primary school enrollment in Adama City established an increase of approximately 44% in five years (Table 6). New Grade 1 intake grew correspondingly over the same period, reflecting sustained population growth, migration-driven urban expansion, and rising demand for educational services. These figures are not merely aggregate statistics; they translate directly into spatial service pressure. Rapid growth in the student population has contributed to overcrowding, increased classroom pressure, and uneven service accessibility, particularly in newly expanding peripheral settlements. The enrollment series in Table 6 is reported at city level and cannot be spatially disaggregated here, so the correspondence between enrollment growth and the high-suitability zones is inferential; it is, however, consistent with the school-level overcrowding records reported in Table 5.

3.3 Spatial, demographic, and physical factors influencing primary school site suitability

Primary school siting requires the integration of demographic demand, accessibility, land-use compatibility, and physical suitability [10, 21]. In practice, failing to consider any one of these domains often leads to underutilized schools or unsafe locations. Accessibility must be treated not merely as a physical distance to be minimized, but as a socio-spatial requirement that aligns school locations with the actual daily mobility and residential patterns of urban residents ‒ particularly the urban poor.

3.3.1 Criteria suitability assessment

A uniform 1–9 suitability scale was applied across all criteria to ensure consistency in the weighted overlay analysis; higher values indicate greater suitability. Table 7 presents the scale. The five criteria and their suitability classifications are summarized in Tables 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12; key findings for each are noted below. For each criterion, class boundaries and their 1–9 scores were derived from a combination of sources rather than a single uniform method. Population density classes were based on natural breaks (Jenks) informed by expert judgment. Road-distance classes followed planning-standard walking-distance thresholds, supplemented by expert judgment. Slope classes were based on established construction-suitability thresholds. Land-use compatibility scores followed regulatory zoning categories. Distance-to-existing-school classes were based on a minimum service-radius standard. For clarity, suitability increases with population density; decreases with distance from roads and existing schools (though very close proximity to major roads is penalized, see below); decreases with steeper slope; and increases where land use is residential/mixed-use, decreasing or excluded for industrial or protected zones.

A. population demand (Residential Density)

High-density residential areas were concentrated primarily within rapidly expanding peripheral neighborhoods, where population growth has increased demand for educational services. These locations consistently exhibited a mismatch between demographic pressure and existing school provision, indicating that educational infrastructure has not expanded at the same pace as residential development. The spatial pattern suggests that population growth is increasingly occurring in areas where educational accessibility remains comparatively limited (Table 8; Fig. 9).

B. Accessibility (Distance to Roads)

The accessibility analysis revealed notable differences between established urban districts and newly developing peripheral settlements. Areas with weaker road connectivity frequently coincided with neighborhoods already experiencing educational service deficits, suggesting that physical accessibility remains an important dimension of educational inequality (Table 9; Fig. 10). The pattern indicates that improving educational access requires attention not only to school location but also to the broader transport and mobility conditions that shape daily access to services. It should be noted that being within 0–100 m of a road does not itself indicate a uniform accessibility or safety problem: the noise, air-quality, and pedestrian-safety concerns reflected in this innermost band’s low suitability score apply most strongly to major highways and arterial roads, and may not hold for low-traffic residential streets falling within the same buffer distance. The current road-classification data does not differentiate road hierarchy, and this is noted as a limitation.

C. Land Use Compatibility

Suitable land for future educational development was concentrated primarily within residential and mixed-use areas where proximity to population centers would maximize service accessibility. In contrast, industrial and infrastructure-dominated zones offered limited opportunities for educational expansion. This distribution highlights the importance of integrating school planning with broader land-use management strategies to ensure that future urban growth remains adequately served by public facilities (Table 10; Fig. 11). The suitability analysis showed that land-use constraints substantially influenced the spatial distribution of suitable areas. Protected and conservation areas, active industrial zones, and floodplain/hazard-buffer areas were completely excluded from the suitability analysis, while low-density agricultural land at the urban fringe remained eligible but received low, non-zero suitability scores. These excluded land-use categories were treated as hard constraints and masked out entirely during the weighted overlay. Consequently, high suitability scores on other criteria, such as population density, could not compensate for an exclusion, and areas excluded on land-use grounds were not classified as suitable.

D. Physical Conditions (Slope)

Physical suitability was generally favorable across much of Adama City, reflecting the city’s relatively flat terrain. While topographic constraints were evident in selected peripheral locations, they affected only a limited proportion of the urban area. Consequently, the primary barriers to educational accessibility appear to be related more to patterns of urban growth and facility construction than to physical development constraints (Table 11; Fig. 12).

E. Distance from Existing Schools

Large areas of the urban periphery remain relatively distant from existing schools, reflecting the concentration of educational infrastructure within older urban districts (Table 12; Fig. 13). These underserved zones correspond closely with areas experiencing rapid residential expansion, indicating that school provision has not kept pace with changing settlement patterns. The resulting distribution highlights where future investments could most effectively improve educational accessibility and spatial service coverage.

Taken together, the individual suitability criteria reveal a consistent spatial pattern across the study area. High-demand residential growth, limited school coverage, and accessibility challenges are increasingly concentrated within the city’s expanding peripheral neighborhoods. Although each criterion captures a different dimension of educational accessibility, their combined distribution suggests that service deficits are emerging through the interaction of demographic expansion and uneven facility construction. This convergence provides an important foundation for the integrated suitability assessment presented in the following section.

3.4 Integration of AHP weights and weighted overlay analysis

The suitability analysis reveals a clear spatial mismatch between the geography of educational demand and the current distribution of school infrastructure in Adama City (Table 13; Fig. 5). Areas identified as priorities for future school development are concentrated in the city’s rapidly expanding peripheral neighborhoods, where population growth has accelerated but educational provision has remained limited. In contrast, the urban core already benefits from a relatively dense concentration of schools, reducing the immediate need for additional facilities.

The integrated assessment further highlights the cumulative nature of educational accessibility disadvantage. High-priority locations are not defined by a single variable but by the convergence of multiple conditions, including rapid population growth, limited school coverage, and favorable land-development characteristics. These findings suggest that educational inequalities emerge through the interaction of demographic expansion and delayed facility construction rather than from population growth alone.

The concentration of suitable locations in peripheral areas reflects broader patterns of urban expansion in the city. As residential development has extended outward, educational infrastructure has remained largely concentrated within older urban districts, creating areas where demand for educational services increasingly exceeds existing capacity. This spatial imbalance indicates that facility construction has generally followed urban growth rather than anticipated it. Identifying these emerging service-deficit areas before accessibility challenges become more severe provides an opportunity for more proactive and equitable infrastructure planning, particularly in neighborhoods experiencing sustained demographic growth.

The aggregated pairwise comparison matrix (Appendix B, Table B1) yielded a calculated Consistency Ratio (CR) of 0.04. Because this value falls substantially below Saaty’s [34] established operational threshold of 0.10, the pairwise judgments are internally consistent. This indicates coherent, non-contradictory reasoning among the six experts rather than the absence of subjectivity or bias in the resulting weight structure. The Global Moran’s I test for the final primary school suitability surface yielded a positive spatial autocorrelation index of I = 0.402 (z = 20.10, p < 0.001). This statistically significant positive value confirms that primary school suitability levels in Adama City are spatially clustered rather than randomly distributed, with high-suitability zones concentrating along primary expansion corridors.

3.5 Suitability classification as a decision-support framework

The suitability classification demonstrates that opportunities for future school development are more limited than might be expected in a rapidly expanding city. Only a relatively small proportion of urban land meets the combined demographic, accessibility, and physical requirements for immediate educational development, as shown in Table 14. This finding has important planning implications because it indicates that delays in reserving land for public facilities may further constrain future facility construction. For interpretive clarity, the nine-class suitability scores shown in Fig. 5 were grouped into three broader categories in Table 14: High Suitability (scores 8–9), Moderate Suitability (scores 5–7), and Low Suitability (scores 1–4, plus NoData/restricted areas), following the class groupings in Table 7.

The spatial clustering of highly suitable areas in growth corridors suggests that educational planning should be integrated with urban expansion strategies rather than implemented as a subsequent response to emerging service shortages. Without early intervention, current uneven access to schooling is likely to become increasingly entrenched as residential development continues to outpace infrastructure investment, as presented in Fig. 6.

4 Discussion

Consistent with findings from other rapidly growing African secondary cities, where school investment has been shown to lag behind residential expansion [3, 13], schools in Adama City have generally been built after neighborhoods were established rather than alongside them. This pattern is consistent with evidence from other rapidly growing Ethiopian cities, where substantial gaps have been identified between formal planning standards and their implementation in Bahir Dar and Hawassa [38]. Similarly, the Ethiopia Urban Expansion Initiative documented rapid growth and the need for forward-looking planning in Adama, Hawassa, Bahir Dar, and Mekele, emphasizing the importance of anticipating future urban expansion and securing land for infrastructure and other public goods [39]. More recent evidence from Addis Ababa also identifies policy, institutional, and implementation gaps in the planning and provision of preschool infrastructure under conditions of rapid urbanization [37, 38]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the difficulty of coordinating education infrastructure with rapidly expanding residential areas reflects a broader challenge of aligning urban growth with timely social-infrastructure provision in Ethiopian cities. The areas the model flagged as highest priority for new school development largely coincide with the areas where households reported the longest walking distances and where administrative records show the most overcrowded classrooms. The spatial correspondence among these three independent evidence sources such as GIS-modeled priority zones, household-reported walking distances, and administrative overcrowding records constitutes convergent evidence rather than a demonstration of causality. The consistency across sources strengthens confidence that the identified areas are genuinely underserved, even though it does not by itself establish that delayed institutional response is the sole cause of the observed pattern. The pattern points to a specific institutional sequence: residential construction proceeds, population accumulates, and only afterward does school provision attempt to catch up, typically several years later and at higher cost than if land had been reserved in advance.

The importance of the findings goes beyond determining appropriate locations for future schools. This study illustrates how outward growth creates unequal access to important services because of poor integration of infrastructure planning into urban development processes. It is apparent that the problem of accessibility to education cannot be attributed only to the growth in population size or geographical distance. Instead, it is about the speed at which infrastructure development meets changing settlements over time. For example, accessibility problems seen within peripheral settlements of Adama City are an indicator of differential urbanization and infrastructure development over time. Peripheral neighborhoods more broadly consistently experience longer walking distances, weaker transport connectivity, and limited educational service coverage patterns consistent with what urbanization scholars have documented in other fast-growing secondary cities, where school investment in rapidly growing secondary cities lags behind residential expansion [3, 13]. The findings can also be interpreted through the lens of spatial justice. Access to educational infrastructure is not distributed randomly across urban space but is shaped by planning decisions regarding land allocation, infrastructure investment, and development priorities. The concentration of accessibility deficits in rapidly urbanizing peripheral neighborhoods suggests that educational opportunities are becoming unevenly distributed across the city. In this sense, uneven access to schooling reflects not only physical distance but also broader processes through which urban development advantages some locations while leaving others comparatively underserved. This study is in agreement with research on other rapidly developing urban centers with unequal access to important public services. Other research has found that the problem of uneven access to services is common in fast-growing urban centers, where public service delivery systems fail to keep up with growing urban peripheries.

Comparable patterns have been documented in much larger metropolitan contexts such as Nairobi, Lagos, and Greater Mumbai [11], where service backlogs are typically attributed to scale and administrative fragmentation. Adama City deficits emerge under different conditions: a secondary city with a far smaller fiscal base, a structure-plan system that nominally reserves land for schools but lacks enforcement capacity to protect that land from conversion, and a single dominant growth corridor rather than multidirectional metropolitan sprawl. This study therefore contributes to the growing literature on facility construction in secondary cities, a context that remains underrepresented compared with research focused on large metropolitan areas, and shows that similar accessibility outcomes can arise from a distinct institutional pathway.

While previous studies have primarily documented access gaps in large metropolitan regions, the Adama City case suggests that similar patterns may emerge in secondary cities with substantially fewer institutional and financial resources. Unlike major metropolitan areas, where infrastructure deficits often result from scale and governance complexity, accessibility disparities in Adama City appear closely linked to the speed of peri-urban expansion and the limited capacity of local institutions to anticipate future service demand. This suggests that educational accessibility challenges are not exclusively metropolitan phenomena but are increasingly characteristic of rapidly growing secondary cities across the Global South. At the same time, the findings extend existing scholarship by demonstrating how spatial suitability analysis can be used not only to identify locations for future facilities but also to reveal emerging patterns of service inequality before they become more severe. The correspondence between modeled suitability outputs and stakeholder-identified underserved areas suggests that geospatial decision-support tools can provide practical planning intelligence in data-constrained environments. Rather than functioning solely as technical site-selection instruments, such approaches can assist planners in recognizing where infrastructure deficits are likely to emerge under continued urban growth.

In terms of planning practices, this study shows that the problem of access to educational services could be overcome by incorporating educational infrastructure development within comprehensive urban development. Typically, infrastructure provision occurs reactively, trailing residential settlement patterns which results in chronic accessibility issues and ever-increasing costs to solve them. An alternative approach would be to anticipate such problems through strategic planning. It would entail reserving adequate lands for building schools at an earlier stage of urban development when there is still time to influence the development of new neighborhoods, especially in the case of fast-growing suburbs.

4.1 Why these deficits emerged in Adama City: institutional and land-management drivers

The spatial mismatch documented above is not simply a byproduct of population growth outrunning a fixed stock of schools; it reflects specific decisions within Adama City planning and land-management system. Qualitative field evidence suggests a systemic disconnect between macro-level land reservation and local-level enforcement. Interviews with planning officials suggest that parcels designated for social infrastructure are highly vulnerable to informal conversion or re-allocation during delayed fiscal cycles. The findings are consistent with an institutional pattern wherein infrastructure investments reactively chase spatial expansion rather than strategically guiding it. Parcels designated for social-service use in approved structure plans were reassigned to residential or commercial development once expansion pressure on those areas increased, consistent with documented patterns of structure-plan non-implementation across Oromia secondary cities [18]. According to interviewed officials, the Education Bureau capital budget cycle operates independently of, and typically lags, the municipality land-allocation decisions so that by the time a new neighborhood is formally recognized as needing a school, the most accessible parcels are no longer available. Coordination between the Bureau of Urban Planning, the Cadaster and Cartography Office, and the Education Bureau remains largely informal, with no shared monitoring system tracking whether reserved educational land has been protected from conversion. Together, these dynamics suggest that governance and institutional coordination failures are an important, and likely under-recognized, contributor to Adama City’s accessibility deficits alongside demographic growth ‒ though this study’s mixed-method design cannot establish the relative quantitative weight of governance versus demographic drivers, and closing these deficits will plausibly require procedural fixes as well as continued demand-side investment. These fixes may include land-reservation enforcement, cross-bureau coordination, budget synchronization rather than spatial analysis alone. Overall, the study suggests how crucial an anticipatory approach to infrastructure planning could be in secondary cities experiencing rapid urbanization. In view of future urban expansions, the decisions made today as for how the available space would be allocated, what investments would be needed, and which services would be provided might greatly affect accessibility outcomes. Therefore, applying spatial analysis methods in municipal planning could facilitate the development of a balanced education landscape while avoiding future infrastructure deficits.

4.2 Implications for sustainable urban development

The conclusions drawn have wider implications for sustainable urban development, especially in rapidly growing secondary cities. While education infrastructure is mostly seen in terms of learning achievements, the effects of its distribution go far beyond the domain of education. In particular, the distribution of schools affects mobility and opportunity structures, processes of social exclusion and inclusion, development of specific neighborhoods, as well as the quality of urban life more generally. Therefore, one should think about educational accessibility not just as an issue of delivering services but as an essential element of sustainable urban development.

The conclusions indicate that educational service provision inequalities in Adama City are closely connected with the geography of urban growth. Places with fast growing population had worse levels of educational services accessibility. This indicates the gap between demographic trends and the construction of urban infrastructure, and leads to the creation of infrastructure deficits in new urbanizing neighborhoods. If such imbalances continue to exist, they will create additional barriers to accessing educational services for peripheral parts of the urban population.

The lack of schools in newly developed neighborhoods is a problem in terms of sustainability as it brings about social, economic, and even environmental issues for those living there. More travel expenses will be required by households because of this situation; children may need more time on travel to schools, which in turn might result in an increased reliance on transportation. It is possible that people may be dissuaded from attending classes due to the distance involved and the difficulty faced as well. In the long run, this may result in the creation of social inequalities within the city and thus thwarting attempts to achieve social equity and inclusion within the urban environment.

The results of this study point to another very important aspect regarding educational institutions, which includes planning them in relation to urban growth and land use strategies. Schools and other educational facilities are usually considered only after the population growth and new development has been taken into account. The situation is such that when land prices rise and residential construction occurs; it would become increasingly difficult to find a proper location for schools and other facilities. Thus, planning is necessary.

More broadly, the study underscores the strategic importance of secondary cities in achieving sustainable development objectives. Across many countries in the Global South, secondary cities are absorbing an increasing share of urban population growth yet often possess fewer institutional, financial, and technical resources than major metropolitan areas. As a result, infrastructure provision frequently struggles to keep pace with urban expansion. The challenges observed in Adama City are therefore not unique but are likely representative of wider development pressures facing rapidly growing secondary cities. Addressing educational accessibility within these contexts is essential for advancing Sustainable Development Goal 4 (Quality Education), Sustainable Development Goal 10 (Reduced Inequalities), and Sustainable Development Goal 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities).

Ultimately, the study suggests that educational infrastructure should be viewed as a critical element of urban sustainability rather than as a sectoral concern addressed independently of broader planning processes. Integrating educational service provision into long-term urban development strategies can help cities anticipate future demand, reduce spatial inequalities, improve accessibility, and support more inclusive, resilient, and sustainable urbanization pathways. The concentration of high-suitability zones in outer peripheries (such as the expansion corridors near Atena Tera and Denbel) underscores a temporal planning mismatch. Relatively low land values and available open space make these peripheries technically attractive for institutional development; however, interview evidence suggests that, without synchronized budgeting between the Education Bureau and the Land Management Bureau, reserved sites risk being absorbed by residential encroachment before construction capital is deployed.

4.3 Limitations and future research

Several limitations affect the precision and generalizability of these findings. First, the absence of recent municipal census data and limited demographic representation of informal settlements introduced uncertainty into population estimation. WorldPop (2024) gridded data improved spatial representation but cannot fully capture rapid, informal settlement dynamics in Adama City’s expanding periphery. Gridded population estimates carry inherent uncertainty in areas of informal and recent growth; future research should incorporate updated household-level demographic surveys to improve local demand estimation.

Second, the use of Euclidean distance measures, while methodologically defensible as a conservative lower bound (see Sect.  2.4), is likely, in most cases, to understate actual accessibility deprivation in peri-urban environments where fragmented road networks, pedestrian safety constraints, and seasonal mobility barriers substantially increase effective travel distances beyond straight-line measures (although informal shortcuts could occasionally have the opposite effect). The accessibility surfaces produced here should therefore be interpreted as conservative floor estimates rather than accurate travel-time representations. Future research should strengthen accessibility estimation through network-based travel analysis and community-mapped mobility data.

Third, the presence of significant spatial autocorrelation in the suitability surface (Moran’s I = 0.402) deserves methodological attention. While the Weighted Linear Combination approach does not formally require input independence, the clustering observed in both population density and road accessibility layers were driven by the concentric structure of Adama City urban expansion. That means that these two highest-weighted criteria may reinforce each other spatially in ways that amplify the concentration of high-suitability scores in peripheral zones. The sensitivity analysis results (Table 3) suggest the overall priority area classification is robust, but local-level suitability scores in highly autocorrelated zones should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The positive spatial autocorrelation of the primary school suitability surface (Moran’s I = 0.402) indicates that areas with similar suitability levels tend to occur in spatial clusters. This pattern can be interpreted alongside the AHP results, in which population density, road accessibility, and slope received the highest weights (32.4%, 26.3%, and 21.0%, respectively). The spatial concentration of these influential criteria may therefore contribute to the observed clustering of suitability values. Nevertheless, the AHP weights describe the relative contribution of the criteria to the suitability model and do not themselves establish the statistical significance of the spatial clustering. The final suitability map indicates a spatially clustered distribution of suitability levels, with higher suitability concentrated in and around the central urban area and lower suitability occurring more extensively toward peripheral areas. This visual pattern is consistent with the positive Moran’s I value (I = 0.402), and the significance test is reported in Sect.  3 (z = 20.10, p < 0.001).

Fourth, the AHP involved expert judgment from six professionals, introducing subjectivity into the weighting structure. This limitation was mitigated through structured pairwise comparison, geometric mean aggregation, consistency ratio evaluation (CR \(\:=\) 0.04), and sensitivity testing, but future studies could compare the current approach with Fuzzy AHP, TOPSIS, machine learning-based suitability models, or location-allocation optimization techniques. The panel was small, and a leave-one-expert-out test of weight stability is not reported here; such a test, together with a larger and more diverse panel, would strengthen confidence in the weight structure.

Fifth, several potentially important variables were excluded: pedestrian safety, school capacity, land ownership conditions, public transport accessibility, and school-age population distribution. These exclusions may influence the precision of accessibility estimation and infrastructure prioritization. The study adopts a site-suitability rather than demand-optimization framing by identifying where schools could be built equitably rather than how many students each site would serve; which limits but does not eliminate the relevance of capacity data. Future research integrating school-by-school enrollment registers and land ownership records would substantially improve model completeness.

Sixth, uncertainty propagation across multiple raster layers may influence local suitability scores, particularly in rapidly changing peri-urban environments where source datasets differ in temporal resolution and update frequency.

Seventh, the qualitative evidence rests on purposive samples of 12 key informants and four focus groups drawn largely from municipal institutions and selected gandas. Institutional accounts are inherently perspectival: planning officials may under- or over-state the role of their own institutions, and the study does not quantify the extent of conversion of reserved parcels citywide. Findings on land-reservation failure and budget misalignment should therefore be read as triangulated indications rather than measured magnitudes. Coding was consensus-based, without a formal inter-coder agreement statistic.

Eighth, the validation is convergent rather than independent, because the model criteria and the underserved indicators draw on related conditions. The 73% overlap is directional, depends on defining Highly Suitable areas as scores 8–9 and underserved gandas by a two-of-three rule, and is not reported here under alternative thresholds. Jaccard and Kappa values computed on a small number of spatial units are sensitive to how units are defined. The mixed-method design supports triangulation, not causal attribution: it cannot establish the relative contribution of governance failures versus demographic growth to the observed deficits.

Ninth, this is a single-city case. The AHP weights and scoring thresholds partly reflect six local experts’ judgments and local planning standards, so transferability to other cities requires re-deriving weights and thresholds with local data. The sensitivity analysis varied one weight at a time by ± 10% and did not examine joint weight perturbations or alternative class boundaries; global approaches such as Monte Carlo simulation of weights are a priority for future work.

Finally, limited coordination among municipal institutions constrained access to standardized planning datasets and updated infrastructure records. Future research should explore integrated urban data systems, real-time spatial monitoring, and stronger integration between geospatial planning tools and municipal investment decision-making processes. Despite these limitations, the study provides a replicable GIS-based approach for supporting educational infrastructure planning in rapidly urbanizing environments ‒ one that explicitly acknowledges data constraints and embeds them in the interpretive framework rather than treating them as reasons to dismiss the findings.

5 Conclusion

The central challenge in Adama City is not the absence of suitable land for schools but the delayed integration of educational infrastructure into urban expansion processes. As peri-urban neighborhoods continue to absorb population growth, the failure to reserve and service educational land at early stages of development risks locking newly settled communities into persistent access gaps. This study highlights three critical dimensions at the intersection of urban management and spatial infrastructure planning:

  1. 1.

    Empirical Deficit Dynamics: Rapidly growing secondary cities like Adama City are associated with severe peripheral service deficits: the 12 schools operating above capacity average 58 students per classroom, a pattern that the qualitative evidence links to reactive, lag-driven planning.

  2. 2.

    Institutional Vulnerability: Spatial site suitability may be constrained by governance capacity. Even when spatial models identify suitable locations, interview evidence indicates that weak enforcement mechanisms can allow reserved public land to be diverted to competing uses.

  3. 3.

    Methodological Integration: Combining GIS-AHP models with grassroots household tracking and key institutional interviews helps keep desktop modeling anchored in local realities.

For municipal managers in the Global South, the findings suggest that infrastructure equity is unlikely to be achieved by spatial modeling alone and would plausibly require early, legally binding land reservation and the strict alignment of capital investment schedules with spatial expansion corridors. The findings reveal a pronounced spatial mismatch between the geography of educational demand and the distribution of existing school infrastructure. Areas experiencing the most rapid residential expansion tended to exhibit lower levels of educational service provision, longer walking distances to schools, and greater evidence of overcrowding. The suitability analysis identified many of these same peripheral neighborhoods as priority areas for future educational investment. Rather than being driven by a single factor, access gaps appear to emerge through the combined effects of demographic growth, uneven school distribution, and delayed infrastructure provision. The results indicate that educational infrastructure development has generally followed urban expansion rather than anticipated it, creating localized service deficits in newly urbanizing areas.

These findings are important because they suggest that educational accessibility challenges are closely linked to broader urban development processes. In rapidly growing secondary cities, infrastructure deficits are often not the result of population growth alone but of the inability of planning systems to align public service provision with changing settlement patterns. The study contributes to the growing body of research on urban service accessibility by providing evidence from a secondary-city context that remains underrepresented in the literature. It also illustrates how geospatial decision-support tools can move beyond technical site selection to reveal emerging patterns of spatial inequality and support more informed planning decisions in data-constrained environments. From a policy perspective, the findings highlight the need to integrate educational infrastructure planning into long-term urban growth management strategies. Reserving land for schools during the early stages of urban expansion, particularly in rapidly developing peripheral neighborhoods, can help prevent future accessibility deficits and reduce the costs associated with retroactive infrastructure provision. More broadly, the study underscores the value of anticipatory planning approaches that align educational investment with projected demographic change. As secondary cities continue to absorb a growing share of urban population growth across Ethiopia and the wider Global South, proactive and spatially informed planning will be essential for promoting equitable access to educational opportunities and supporting more sustainable urban development outcomes.
