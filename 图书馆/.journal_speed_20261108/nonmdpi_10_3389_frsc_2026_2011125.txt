Centre for Advanced Spatial Analysis, University College London, London, United Kingdom Article metrics View details Abstract Urban digital twins are increasingly used to support planning, policy, and urban governance, yet cities cannot be fully captured as stable digital representations. Building on existing understandings of digital twinning, this article argues for conceptualizing urban digital twinning as a continuing institutional planning practice. We distinguish the digital twin as a configured representational and analytical system from digital twinning as the broader practice connecting digital representation with interpretation, deliberation, governance, planning decisions, urban interventions, monitoring, institutional learning, and model revision across successive planning cycles. This perspective emphasizes the role of human judgment and institutional knowledge in responding to evolving urban conditions and planning uncertainty, offering a basis for more adaptive, transparent, accountable, and context-sensitive urban planning and governance. 1 Cities cannot be treated as stable objects Urban digital twins are rapidly becoming part of planning, infrastructure management, mobility, climate adaptation, and public service delivery. As cities invest in digital technologies, digital twins are increasingly used to support urban analysis, policy decisions, and the management of complex urban systems (Batty, 2018; Goodchild et al., 2024). Their rapid adoption has generated considerable enthusiasm for more insightful research and practice while also exposing a more fundamental question that extends beyond technological capability. The concept of the digital twin emerged largely from engineering and manufacturing (Grieves, 2014; Batty, 2018). It was developed to represent physical assets with clearly defined boundaries, functions, and operational objectives. Although the concept continues to evolve at pace, these origins continue to influence how digital twins are understood across many application domains. Cities, however, differ fundamentally from engineered assets. They are open socio-technical systems whose physical environments continuously interact with institutions, governance arrangements, economic activities, ecological processes, the everyday actions of diverse communities as well as long-term policy making. Urban systems are continually reshaped through planning decisions, political priorities, public participation, and unexpected events, and digital twins are now in the vanguard of such change. These characteristics have important implications for urban digital systems. A city digital twin, like any computer model, cannot function as an exact mirror of urban reality (Batty, 2018). Every digital representation reflects choices about system boundaries, data, assumptions, priorities, and intended purposes. Its value lies not in perfectly reproducing urban reality, but in supporting planning and decision-making under conditions of uncertainty (Goodchild et al., 2024). Indeed, the process of building and applying such a digital twin is a continuing process that here we call “twinning”. The central urban challenge is not simply to decide whether a digital twin accurately represents the city, but how that representation is continually produced, interpreted, revised, and used throughout the broader range of urban planning processes. Urban planning therefore requires recognizing cities as dynamic environments whose digital representations must evolve alongside them. If cities cannot be treated as stable digital objects due to their continued evolution, how should digital twins and digital twinning be understood in urban planning? 2 From digital twins to digital twinning As digital twins are increasingly applied across urban planning, infrastructure management, and urban research (Saeed et al., 2022), the concept itself has also evolved. More recent scholarship has moved beyond viewing digital twins as static digital representations. Wagg et al. (2025) describe digital twins as time-evolving systems that maintain a two-way coupling between the digital and physical domains. Goodchild et al. (2024) argue that urban digital twins support prediction and decision-making while recognizing the uncertainty inherent in urban systems. Across these developments, digital twins are increasingly understood as connected, knowledge-intensive, and purpose-driven systems whose value depends on their ability to support specific analytical objectives. This conceptual evolution has also challenged the assumption that a single definition of a digital twin can apply across all domains. Agrawal and Fischer (2024) argue that the meaning of both digital and twin depends on the use case, application context, where and how the system boundaries are drawn, their digital capabilities in analytics, prediction and design, as well as the entities being twinned. Their framework helps practitioners determine how a digital twin should be configured for a particular application by asking two fundamental questions: What is digital? and What are we twinning? Urban research has extended this discussion beyond the design of digital twins themselves. Calzati and van Loenen (2023) argue that urban digital twinning should be understood as a contextual, iterative, participatory, and sociotechnical process that links digital twin technologies with urban development. Recent conceptual discussions also recognize that digital twinning is not only a technical activity but also one that involves interpretation, evolving assumptions, and human judgment. As digital twins become more deeply embedded in planning and governance, questions of uncertainty, transparency, and the limits of digital representation become increasingly important for understanding how digital knowledge informs action (Borbach et al., 2025). Similarly, van der Meer et al. (2026) argue that mature urban digital twins should move beyond technological sophistication and automated decision-making by placing human participation, interpretation, and judgment at the center of their use in urban planning. Their perspective further emphasizes the importance of acknowledging model limitations and treating digital representations as tools that inform, rather than determine, planning decisions. These developments show that digital twinning has already been conceptualized as a process (Calzati and van Loenen, 2023; Solman et al., 2022). However, further conceptual clarification is needed regarding how this process operates as a continuing urban planning practice that connects digital representations with institutional interpretation, professional judgment, public deliberation, policy adaptation, and real-world intervention. 3 Urban digital twinning as a continuing planning practice We argue that urban digital twinning should be understood not simply as an ongoing process, but as a continuing institutional planning practice. From this perspective, the digital twin becomes one component within a broader practice that connects digital representations with interpretation, governance, and urban intervention, as illustrated in Figure 1. The urban digital twin provides digital representation and analytical capabilities, while urban digital twinning connects interpretation, governance, decision-making, intervention, monitoring, learning, and model revision across successive planning cycles. Urban digital twinning does not replace the digital twin. Instead, it positions it as one component within a broader planning practice. A digital twin remains a configured digital system that represents selected aspects of the urban environment and maintains a two-way coupling with its physical counterpart (Wagg et al., 2025) as shown in Figure 1. In urban applications, it supports monitoring, simulation, and prediction (Goodchild et al., 2024). Urban digital twinning, by contrast, is the continuing institutional planning practice through which digital representations are continuously interpreted, connected with professional judgment and institutional knowledge, and translated into urban decisions and interventions. It is through this continuing practice that digital twins acquire planning relevance and public value. From this perspective, urban digital twinning extends well beyond constructing and maintaining digital models. It includes defining and revising system boundaries, integrating heterogeneous datasets, maintaining interoperability across information systems, continuously updating digital representations, interpreting analytical outputs, incorporating professional expertise, integrating local and experiential knowledge, negotiating competing planning priorities, informing planning and policy decisions, monitoring implementation, and revising both digital models and urban interventions over successive planning cycles. These activities are not peripheral to digital twinning. They constitute the planning work that enables digital representations to remain meaningful as cities evolve. Understanding urban digital twinning as a planning practice also requires recognizing the limits of digital representation (Borbach et al., 2025). Cities cannot be fully digitized because they are continually reshaped by social relations, institutional arrangements, political priorities, and locally situated knowledge, all of which exceed complete digital representation (Borbach et al., 2025; Das et al., 2026). More specifically, urban change arises from the interplay between social interactions that generate emergent and often unintended patterns (Bettencourt, 2024) and deliberate public institutional choices that establish the rules within which urban activities unfold (Moroni, 2025). These rules provide a framework for individual actions and interactions, but do not fully determine the resulting urban order, whose development remains difficult to predict in detail (Moroni, 2025). This distinction reinforces the need for continuing interpretation of evolving urban patterns and institutional arrangements within urban digital twinning. In this context, what can be digitally represented continues to evolve as new technologies emerge. Urban digital twinning should therefore support continuous interpretation and adaptation instead of pursuing perfect replication or total control. Digital representations remain partial and purpose-specific, requiring ongoing interpretation as urban conditions, planning priorities, and available knowledge change over time (Borbach et al., 2025). This perspective recognizes uncertainty as an enduring characteristic of urban planning rather than a technical problem that can be eliminated through more comprehensive data or increasingly sophisticated models. Uncertainty takes different forms across planning activities, requiring attention to the specific decision-making situations involved (Moroni and Chiffi, 2021). In infrastructure planning, which concerns the provision of public facilities and services, uncertainty often involves future demand, system performance, and the outcomes of specific investments. In regulatory planning, which establishes rules governing the use and transformation of private land and buildings, uncertainty is more open-ended because the actions and responses of property owners, developers, and other actors cannot be fully anticipated (Moroni, 2023). This distinction has different implications for urban digital twinning. In infrastructure planning, digital twinning can support the iterative assessment of scenarios, demand assumptions, and infrastructure performance. In regulatory planning, its role is less about predicting a predetermined urban outcome and more about monitoring how rules operate in practice, interpreting emerging patterns, and informing institutional reconsideration of regulatory assumptions. In both cases, digital twinning supports continuing planning judgment and learning rather than eliminating uncertainty. Viewing urban digital twinning as a continuing planning practice also changes how digital technologies contribute to governance. The objective is not simply to automate decisions because many data streams are now automated. Strategic planning decisions, including land-use change, infrastructure investment, housing, climate adaptation, and mobility policy, continue to require professional judgment, political deliberation, and institutional accountability. Urban digital twinning therefore links future-oriented modeling with continuous planning, governance, and institutional learning. Digital representations should not only support better planning decisions but also make planning assumptions, modeling choices, system boundaries, and urban interventions more transparent, accountable, contestable, and open to revision. Public value emerges not from increasingly sophisticated digital models alone, but from the institutional capacity to interpret, question, revise, and act upon their outputs throughout successive planning cycles. This perspective also shifts attention from maintaining digital models to sustaining planning services and real-world interventions. The success of urban digital twinning should therefore be evaluated not only by the technical performance of digital twins but also by their capacity to support adaptive planning, institutional coordination, public accountability, and continuous learning across changing urban conditions. Urban digital twins, like the cities to which they are applied, continue to evolve as the coupling between the city and its twin as well as the process of generating this synthesis changes and matures. Building on existing conceptualizations of digital twinning as a process, we frame urban digital twinning as a continuing institutional planning practice that connects digital representation, interpretation, deliberation, governance, and intervention across successive planning cycles. From this perspective, digital twins become not simply technologies for representing cities, but institutional resources that support more informed, transparent, and adaptive urban planning. 4 Discussion: a research, policy, and practice agenda for urban digital twinning Conceptualizing urban digital twinning as a continuing planning practice has important implications for future urban research, policy, and practice. If urban digital twinning extends beyond maintaining digital models, the questions asked by researchers, policymakers, and practitioners must also expand beyond technical performance. For urban research, future studies should distinguish explicitly between evaluating digital twins as digital representations and evaluating urban digital twinning as a continuing planning practice. Model accuracy, computational performance, interoperability, and real-time connectivity remain important, but they provide only a partial understanding of urban value. Research should also examine interpretability, institutional learning, governance, transparency, accountability, participation, and the capacity of digital twinning to support adaptive planning and policy revision. Greater attention should also be given to how future-oriented modeling shapes contemporary planning decisions, how planning assumptions and system boundaries evolve across planning cycles, and how different forms of professional, institutional, and local knowledge enter, influence, or remain excluded from the twinning process. For urban policy, digital strategies should move beyond investing primarily in digital twin platforms and data infrastructure. Cities should also invest in the institutional capabilities required to sustain urban digital twinning over time, including governance arrangements, cross-departmental coordination, interoperability, continuous updating, model stewardship, institutional learning, transparent decision-making, and public accountability. Policy frameworks should clearly identify who defines model boundaries, who revises planning assumptions, who interprets analytical outputs, who remains accountable for planning decisions, and how assumptions and interventions remain open to public and institutional scrutiny. Urban digital twinning should make planning assumptions, modeling choices, system boundaries, and policy interventions more transparent, accountable, and contestable, recognizing that digital representations are never neutral but are continually shaped through governance and institutional choices (Borbach et al., 2025). For urban practice, city governments and planning organizations should treat urban digital twinning as an ongoing organizational capability instead of a one-time technology project. Every initiative should establish recurring processes for updating data, reviewing planning assumptions, incorporating professional expertise together with local and experiential knowledge, monitoring planning outcomes, adapting interventions, and documenting institutional learning throughout successive planning cycles. Digital twinning should be co-created with planners, infrastructure managers, public agencies, stakeholders, and communities so that digital systems continue to reflect evolving urban priorities instead of fixed technical specifications. Practitioners should therefore ask not only What digital twin should we build? but also How will urban digital twinning be sustained as a continuing planning practice that improves planning, governance, and public decision-making? The future of such urban digital systems will depend not only on building increasingly sophisticated digital twins, but also on developing the institutional capacity to practice urban digital twinning as a continuing planning practice.
