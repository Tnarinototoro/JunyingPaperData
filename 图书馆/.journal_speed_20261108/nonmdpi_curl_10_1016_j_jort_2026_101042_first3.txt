Abstract  
Active removal of self-generated litter in mountainous destinations has emerged as an important 
solution to tourism waste management. However, tourists’ participation remains limited, and 
the underlying psychological mechanisms are not fully understood. Dr awing on the low -cost 
hypothesis, this study extends the knowledge -attitude-behavior framework by incorporating 
willingness to sacrifice and personal involvement to explain proactive carry-out trash behavior. 
Based on 264 field questionnaires collected fro m tourists visiting a world -class mountain 
destination in eastern China, this study examines the psychological mechanisms underlying 
carry-out trash behavior. The results reveal a significant serial mediation process: environmental 
knowledge influences beh avioral intention indirectly through tourists’ attitude and their 
willingness to sacrifice personal convenience. This finding highlights the importance of 
subjective cost -benefit evaluation in translating positive attitude into action. Furthermore, 
personal involvement in everyday waste management negatively moderates the relationship 
between tourists’ environmental knowledge and attitude toward carry -out trash behavior. 
Specifically, the positive effect of environmental knowledge on attitude is stronger am ong 
tourists with lower levels of personal involvement. By integrating willingness to sacrifice and 
personal involvement into the KAB model, this study advances understanding of tourists’ 
environmentally responsible behavior in mountainous contexts and pro vides practical 
implications for designing targeted behavioral interventions to promote carry -out trash 
practices.

1. Introduction  
The rapid growth of mountain tourists has significantly increased the burden of waste 
management, posing a threat to the sustainable development of mountainous destinations 
(Romeo et al., 2021). Mountainous regions worldwide are dealing with a heavy burden of waste 
and environmental crisis due to excessive tourism flows (Kaseva & Moirana, 2010). “Leave no 
Trace”, a campaign which encourages visitors to proactively reduce waste and harm to the 
nature, have been widely advocated since the 1960s (Hu et al., 2019; Ng, 2023). Yet the initiative 
remains largely confined to a small cohort of dedicated outdoor practitioners, with its reach 
among the general tourist public still pending meaningful expansion.  
Tourist environmentally responsible behavior (ERB) is seen as the critical means to address 
global climate and environmental change as well as promote sustainable development (Barr, 
2003). Within the literature on waste -related ERBs, prior studies have inv estigated tourists’ 
immediate binning and resorting behavior at the point of consumption (Esfandiar et al., 2020; 
Zhong et al., 2024), while less attention has been paid to subsequent waste management 
behaviors such as carrying waste away for later disposal or reusing items (Raghu & Rodrigues, 
2020). These later -stage behaviors require sustained effort, temporary storage and continued 
environmental commitment. Many destinations face challenges in collecting and transporting 
on-site tourism waste due to limi ted labor force and challenging terrain (UNEP, 2016). As a 
result, tourists’ proactive environmentally responsible behaviors, especially taking 
selfgenerated litter out of the zones, are quite valuable. Building on Principle 3 of “Leave No 
Trace” (Dispose of Waste Properly), this study focuses on tourists’ voluntary behavior of 
carrying waste out of core scenic areas, which we term carry-out trash behavior.  
Tourists’ carry-out trash behavior in mountainous destinations poses a distinct challenge, 
as it is typically voluntary, non -mandatory, and enacted under strong physical and situational 
constraints (Matiiuk & Liobikienė, 2021). Performing this behavior req uires tourists to 
recognize environmental consequences, interpret site-specific information, know how to carry 
out trash properly, and translate such understanding into concrete actions, rendering it a 
cognitively demanding rather than purely normative or habitual response (Zhang, 2023). 
Behavioral frameworks commonly applied in tourism research, such as the theory of planned 
behavior and norm -based models (Lam et al., 2025; Ng & Cheng, 2022), focus heavily on 
intentions, social norms, and moral obligations, but tend to overlook the role of knowledge. In 
contrast, the knowledge–attitude–behavior (KAB) framework conceptualizes environmentally 
responsible behavior as a sequential process in which knowledge constitutes the cognitive 
foundation for attitude form ation and subsequent actions (Maleknia & Zamani, 2025). This 
process-oriented perspective aligns closely with the context-specific nature and knowledge/skill 
demands of tourists’ carry-out trash behavior in mountainous settings. Accordingly, this study 
adopts the KAB framework to examine how environmental knowledge translates into attitudes 
and actual carry-out trash behavior.
