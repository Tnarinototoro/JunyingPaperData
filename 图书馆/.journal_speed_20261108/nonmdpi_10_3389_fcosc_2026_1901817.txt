Academy of Scientific and Innovative Research (AcSIR), Ghaziabad, Uttar Pradesh, India Article metrics View details Abstract Integrating indigenous and scientific knowledge systems is increasingly recognised as essential tool for addressing conservation challenges in data-deficient landscapes. However, approaches for systematically incorporating Traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) into conservation science remain limited. In this study we evaluated weather TEK can function as structured ecological information for understanding the vulnerable Mishmi Takin (Budorcas taxicolor taxicolor) in the Eastern Himalayas. Between 2021 and 2023 TEK were documented from indigenous communities in Arunachal Pradesh, India. Community-derived ecological observations were integrated with camera trapping, field expeditions and direct observations. Multivariate analyses, including Principal Component Analyses (PCA), Multiple Correspondence Analyses (MCA), hierarchical clustering and regression approaches were used to examine patterns and variation in ecological knowledge. Respondents described seasonal migration, habitat use, congregation dynamics, group size, and perceived threats. Camera trap surveys (>17,000 independent captures) validated key observations, the habitat use patterns, identified critical congregation sites and seasonal aggregations. Multivariate analyses showed distinct gradients of ecological knowledge associated with landscape exposure, observation effort, and threat awareness demonstrating that TEK represents a structured knowledge system and can generate robust ecological information for data-deficient species. Our findings further underscored that the TEK can provide a reliable ecological inference and provide a robust source of evidence for understanding species ecology, identifying critical habitats and conservation. By integrating TEK with species ecology this study presents a replicable framework for conservation research and decision making in remote socio-ecological landscapes. 1 Introduction Conservation science increasingly recognises that addressing biodiversity loss requires the integration of multiple knowledge systems. Whilst in space and time, the core disciplines like ecology and evolution have played a major role in generated critical insights into species distributions, population dynamics and ecosystem functioning, many conservation challenges persist in regions where ecological data remain limited. In such contexts, Indigenous and local knowledge systems can provide valuable ecological information’s that complements scientific approaches and enhances conservation decision making. Recent global assessments, including those of the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IBPES), have emphasised the importance of Indigenous and Traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) for biodiversity conservation whilst highlighting its continued underrepresentation in research, policy and environmental governance (IPBES, 2019; Sinthumule, 2023; Sheppard et al., 2024; Zarazúa-Carbajal et al., 2022). Understanding biodiversity conservation requires recognising the multidimensional relationship between people and nature that evolved over time (Alves, 2012; Gadgil et al., 1993). Human communities influence, interpret and respond to environmental change through local knowledge, practises and institutions which could be essential to manage environment. Such knowledge can be particularly valuable in data deficient landscape or for species where conventional ecological monitoring is difficult to implement (Alves, 2012; Gonçalves-Souza et al., 2022). Consequently, in recent decades, increasing recognition to TEK systems developed through long-term interactions between people and their environment integrating this with scientific approaches can substantially enhance ecological understanding and to develop more inclusive, and effective conservation strategies (Tengö et al., 2014, 2021). TEK encompasses locally cumulative observations, beliefs, practices, and institutions it represents an empirical and adaptive knowledge system developed through long-term interactions between people and nature and transmitted across generations (Mekonen, 2017). Far from being anecdotal TEK often represents a structured body knowledge of ecological understanding capable of providing insights into species behaviour, habitat uses population trends and environmental change. Excluding such knowledge may result in incomplete ecological understandings and less effective conservation outcomes (IPBES, 2019; Gonçalves-Souza et al., 2022). Increasing trends of integrating TEK with scientific research can improve biodiversity monitoring, strengthen ecological inference, support for developing inclusive, and effective biodiversity conservation strategies particularly for less studied species and remote ecosystems (Turner et al., 2022; Sheppard et al., 2024). Many species survive in small, isolated populations and limits the availability of the ecological information for conservation and recovery, such as species-specific demographic parameters and habitat preferences through scientific research alone. In such cases, TEK can play a crucial role in bridging critical knowledge gaps and providing complementary insights into species ecology and conservation needs (Sheppard et al., 2024). Our research focused on conservation and understanding of ecological insights of Mishmi Takin (Budorcas taxicolor taxicolor) (hereafter Takin) (Neas and Hoffmann, 1987) and represents one such species for which such integrative approaches is urgently needed. The Takin is a large-bodied, mountain ungulate endemic to the Eastern Himalayas and restricted in India to Arunachal Pradesh (Cooper, 1923; Prater, 1980). It plays an important ecological role in montane ecosystem functioning (Schaller et al., 1986; Sathyakumar and Dubey, 2025). Despite its ecological and cultural significance, it remains one of the least studied mountain ungulates in India. The species is currently listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, included in Schedule I of the Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, and placed in Appendix I of CITES. Ecological research on the Takin is constrained by the rugged terrain, remoteness, harsh climatic conditions and low detectability that characterises its habitat. As a result, conventional approaches such as camera trapping, sign surveys and direct observations often yield limited ecological information (Sathyakumar and Dubey, 2025). These constraints have contributed to significant knowledge gaps regarding the species distribution, behaviour and habitat use seasonal movements, social organisation and conservation threats. In this context, Indigenous communities inhabiting the Mishmi Hills including the Idu Mishmi, Digaru Mishmi, Miju Mishmi, and Meyor maintain extensive ecological knowledge of the species derived from long term observations and experiences. This knowledge encompasses observations of habitat use, seasonal migration, congregation sites, group structure, feeding ecology, behaviour and human wildlife relationships. Importantly to notice is such knowledge is embedded in their cultural narratives, ritual practices, and customary taboos that leading a sustainable life conserving the wildlife. Documenting and integrating this knowledge offer an opportunity not only to improve understandings of the Mishmi Takin but also examine how TEK can contribute to ecological research and conservation planning (Gadgil et al., 1993; Joa et al., 2018). Despite the growing recognition of indigenous knowledge studies that quantitatively integrate TEK, with ecological data remain limited in the Indian Himalayas. Existing information on the Takin in India is fragmented and largely based on sporadic observations, opportunistic records and with the little consideration of indigenous knowledge. This gap restricts both ecological understandings and development of effective conservation strategies for the species. Despite previous studies describing the distribution, ecology, and conservation status of the Mishmi Takin, important ecological aspects, including seasonal habitat use, congregation dynamics, behavioural ecology, migration pathways, and local threat perceptions, remain poorly understood because of the species’ occurrence in remote and inaccessible mountain landscapes. TEK accumulated through long-term interactions between Indigenous communities and their environment, provides complementary ecological information that can help address these knowledge gaps, particularly where long-term scientific datasets are limited or difficult to obtain. Therefore, this study adopts an interdisciplinary approach that integrates TEK with ecological field validation to understand the ecology and conservation of Mishmi Takin in the Eastern Himalaya. Specifically, we undergone i) Documentation of TEK; ii) Assessment of Mishmi Takin occurrence, habitat use and seasonal distribution; and iii) Assessment of social-group and seasonal congregation patterns; iv) Analysis of TEK structure and community knowledge patterns; v) Assessment of perceived threats and conservation priorities. More broadly we assess whether indigenous ecological knowledge can function as a structured source of ecological evidence for a poorly studied mountain ungulate. By Integrating indigenous and scientific knowledge systems this study contributes to more inclusive, ethically grounded, and ecologically robust conservation planning for the Mishmi Takin and other data deficient species across the Eastern Himalaya. 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Study area The study was conducted in the easternmost part of Arunachal Pradesh, Northeast India, within the Mishmi Hills landscape (Dibang Valley and Anjaw District) (Figure 1). This region is characterised by steep altitudinal gradients, complex terrain, and high biological and cultural diversity. The Mishmi hills traverses across four administrative district: Dibang Valley, Lower Dibang Valley, Lohit, and Anjaw. Together, these district encompass a wide range of forest types, from tropical and subtropical forests at lower elevations to subalpine and alpine meadows at higher altitudes. Figure 1 Locations of key informative survey and TEK based camera trap conducted in Mishmi Hills located in Arunachal Pradesh. The study area lies between 27°30′ and 29°22′ N latitude and 95°15′ and 97°24′ E longitude. Elevations range from about 200 msl in river valleys to over 4,500 msl alpine zones. The region includes protected areas such as Dibang Wildlife Sanctuary, Mehao Wildlife Sanctuary, and Kamlang Tiger Reserve. The area is inhabited by multiple Indigenous communities, primarily the Idu Mishmi, Digaru Mishmi (Tawrah), Miju Mishmi (Kaman), and Meyor. These communities maintain strong cultural ties to forests and wildlife and along with extensive TEK derived from hunting, foraging, pastoral activities, and ritual practices. 2.2 Data collection Data collection was carried out between June 2021 and September 2023 which involving three corresponding components: key informant surveys for documenting TEK which were further complimented through scientific methods such as field expedition surveys and camera trapping. 2.3 Key informant surveys A total of 114 respondents from two district amongst Idu, Digaru, Miju Mishmis and Meyors were interviewed across the study area and TEK was documented through Key Informative Surveys (KIS) combining semi structured formal and informal interviews a widely used method for collecting site-specific ecological information from local knowledge holders (Joa et al., 2018). Prior to conducting survey respondents were informed about the work and consent was obtained verbally from all village heads (locally known as “Gaon-buras”) and participants, if only accepted we proceeded for interviews by targeting recognised individual within their communities as potential respondents such as shamans (locally known as “Igu”) experienced active and non-active hunters, elders, forest dwellers to from different age group. These surveys were conducted across Dibang valley and Anjaw district. During the interviews, respondents were first asked whether they regularly visited forested areas within the study region. Those who responded positively were shown standardised photographs of the Mishmi Takin (Budorcas taxicolor taxicolor), Himalayan Serow (Capricornis sumatraensis), Goral (Naemorhedus goral), and Blue Sheep (Pseudois nayaur) (No distribution in study area) to verify species identification. Following correct identification of the Mishmi Takin, respondents were asked to provide detailed information on sighting locations, travel distance from their villages (recorded in travel days and later converted to approximate kilometres), season of occurrence, habitat type, group size, feeding behaviour, seasonal movements, and perceived threats and reported group sizes represented respondents’ estimates of the number of Mishmi Takin observed during individual encounters or at seasonal congregation sites based on their direct field observations. These estimates were recorded as reported and were used to examine patterns of group size variation. These responses were subsequently divided into predefined ecological themes, including habitat use, seasonal occurrence, behaviour, group size, feeding ecology, migration, and threats, for subsequent analysis. Locations of Takin were sighted, local ecological terms and culturally specific concepts (for example names of salt licks, Sighting locations, landmarks, season) were recorded further these gathered TEK is cross validated through repeated interviews, informal discussions, and comparison across communities to reduce individual bias and enhance reliability corroborated by camera trap survey through field expedition surveys (Aiyadurai et al., 2010; Sheppard et al., 2024; Sinthumule, 2023; Gadgil et al., 1993) TEK based information on Behavioural ethograms and congregations seasons, activity pattern further validated through camera tarping. 2.4 Camera trapping To complement TEK-derived information and independent ecological validation, camera trapping (CT Locations = 118 with total 17000+ camera trap captures were defined as photographic captures/records, of Individual/Takin herd) was conducted in identified critical sites such as salt licks, congregation sites, migratory routes, sighting locations. These camera trap placement locations were based on information documented through TEK from local communities during KIS was used to identify areas likely to be used by Mishmi Takins, including potential congregation and salt-lick sites. Further, the scientific field expedition survey involving camera trap data, ocular observation and indirect evidences of Takin presence were noted and this revealed these sites as congregation areas, salt licks, migration routes, or potential hunting locations were prioritised for camera trap deployment. 2.5 Direct observation During direct observations, observers positioned themselves at vantage points overlooking the congregation area and counted visible individuals by scanning the area systematically from one side to the other. Groups were counted separately where distinguishable, and the number of individuals within each visible group was recorded. 2.6 Data analysis of ecological observations and TEK 2.6.1 Analysis of ecological observations and TEK The documented Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) on Mishmi Takin obtained through key informant surveys was organised into categories including habitat use, seasonal occurrence, social-group structure, behaviour, migration, and feeding ecology. Exploratory descriptive analyses were used to summarise sighting frequencies across seasons, habitats, travel distances, and social categories (e.g., social group, solitary, adult/subadult). Habitat types were classified into broad categories (e.g., high-altitude forest, dense forest, near-village forest, and salt-lick habitats) based on respondent descriptions and field verification. Seasonal patterns of occurrence were examined by comparing reported sightings across winter, rainy, autumn, and spring seasons. Behavioural categories were initially recorded from respondent descriptions and subsequently verified through direct field observations and camera-trap records whenever possible. These observations were compiled to develop an ethogram for the species. Camera-trap photographs and videos were also used to confirm species identification and to provide supporting evidence for behavioural patterns and habitat use reported through TEK. Travel distances reported by respondents in travel days were converted to approximate kilometres using the conversion applied during data processing. Simple linear regression was used to evaluate the relationship between the number of Mishmi Takin reported (response variable) and travel distance from villages (predictor variable). Model assumptions were assessed by visual inspection of residual plots to evaluate linearity, homoscedasticity, and major deviations from normality. All descriptive and exploratory analyses were conducted using approaches appropriate for integrating qualitative indigenous knowledge with quantitative ecological observations (Beveridge et al., 2021; Gagnon and Berteaux, 2009; Sheppard et al., 2024) in R (R Core Team, 2017). 2.6.2 Analysis of social-group and seasonal congregation patterns Seasonal variation in Takin social organisation was examined using respondent sighting information and direct field observations. For analytical purposes, social-group size refers to the number of individuals occurring within an observed social group or herd, whereas congregation size refers to the total number of Takin from multiple social groups occurring simultaneously within a seasonal congregation area. Respondent-reported social-group sizes were compared across winter, rainy, autumn, and spring seasons and amongst broad habitat categories. Particular attention was given to salt-lick habitats, where multiple social groups were observed to congregate during the rainy season. Direct ocular observations were used to document the occurrence of multiple Takin social groups at seasonal congregation sites. Individual social groups observed during field surveys generally comprised approximately 18–40 individuals, whereas larger numbers represented the simultaneous aggregation of multiple social groups at congregation sites. Direct observations of large congregations were treated as observation-event-level estimates rather than formal population counts. Observations from different dates were not summed because the same individuals could not be reliably identified between successive observation events. Variation in social-group size amongst habitat types was examined using non-parametric statistical comparisons. A Kruskal–Walli’s rank-sum test was used to evaluate whether the distribution of recorded social-group sizes differed amongst habitat categories. Seasonal patterns were summarised descriptively to identify periods associated with greater aggregation and congregation activity. Camera-trap records were used as complementary evidence for seasonal habitat use and congregation behaviour. Photographic records were treated as camera-trap records/captures and were not automatically interpreted as independent individual detections. 2.6.3 Multivariate analysis of TEK Multivariate analyses were used to examine variation in Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) and associated ecological observations amongst respondents. Respondent-derived ecological information was summarised into quantitative TEK variables. A knowledge richness score was calculated for each respondent as the number of ecological information categories (habitat use, seasonal occurrence, activity, migration, and threats) for which information was provided. Threat count represented the total number of distinct threat categories identified by each respondent, whereas behaviour count represented the number of behavioural observations reported. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to standardised continuous variables, including knowledge richness score, threat count, travel distance, and social-group size, to identify dominant gradients in the multivariate dataset. Continuous variables were standardised using z-score transformation before PCA to ensure comparability amongst variables measured on different scales. The first two principal components were retained for interpretation based on the proportion of variance explained. Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) was conducted on categorical variables, including habitat types, seasonal periods, and community observations, to examine associations amongst categorical ecological and TEK variables and to represent multidimensional relationships in reduced-dimensional space. 2.6.4 Hierarchical clustering and cluster characterisation To explore whether respondents could be grouped into distinct Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) profiles based on their ecological observations, travel experience, threat perception, and behavioural observations, agglomerative hierarchical clustering was performed. Five standardised TEK-derived variables were included in the clustering analysis: knowledge richness score, threat count, behaviour count, travel distance, and social-group size. Continuous variables were standardised using z-score transformation before clustering to minimise the influence of differences in measurement scales. Relationships amongst respondents were visualised using a hierarchical clustering dendrogram, and the characteristics of the resulting clusters were summarised using radar plots. Because the clustering analysis was exploratory in nature, the separation and consistency of the identified clusters were further examined by visualising their distribution within Principal Component Analysis (PCA) ordination space. The cluster profiles were interpreted in relation to differences in ecological knowledge, reported behaviour, travel experience, social-group observations, and perceived threats rather than being treated as predefined respondent categories. 2.6.5 Threat and conservation-perception analysis Respondent-reported threats to Mishmi Takin were summarised and compared amongst district to assess variation in threat composition and overall threat perception. Differences in the composition of reported threats amongst district were tested using the chi-square (χ²) test, whilst Cramér’s V was calculated to estimate the effect size of the association. A Relative Threat Index (RTI) was calculated to compare the proportional importance of reported threats across regions. To improve the reliability of respondent-reported predator observations, follow-up questions were used to obtain detailed descriptions of reported predation events, including the location and circumstances of each observation. Respondents were also shown standardised photographs of potential predator species to assist with species identification. Reports involving predators such as the marbled cat and bear were interpreted as opportunistic predation or scavenging events, primarily involving juveniles, subadults, or fresh carcasses, based on detailed respondent accounts. Only observations that could be consistently verified through respondent descriptions and species identification were included in the analysis. Finally, respondent perceptions regarding Takin conservation were summarised using frequency distributions and compared amongst district to identify differences in conservation perceptions. 3 Results A total of 114 respondents from across Dibang Valley, Lower Dibang Valley, Lohit, and Anjaw district contributed TEK through Key Informant Surveys. Respondents included experienced hunters, elders, and community knowledge holders, Shamans from the Idu Mishmi, Digaru Mishmi, Miju Mishmi, and Meyor communities. Results presented below are based on triangulated evidence from interviews, field observations, and camera trap records. 3.1 TEK derived ecological patterns on Takin 3.1.1 Migration in Takin During the key informative surveys to understand the seasonal migration and habitat use patterns in Takin. Respondents from the Digaru, Meyor, and Miju Mishmi tribes, unanimously reported that Takin follow a seasonal migration pattern, with 100% “Yes” responses. Amongst Idu respondents, the majority also agreed that Takin migrate, with approximately 89% “Yes”, 5% “No”, and 6% “NA” responses (Figure 2). These findings indicate strong traditional knowledge and understanding regarding Takin seasonal migration. Figure 2 Response plots of habitat and migrations in Takin. The results further showed that Takin used different habitats across seasons. During Spring, observations were recorded entirely from Jungle habitats (100%), indicating that Takin remained within forested cover whilst browsing and gradually moving upward. During Autumn, records were mainly divided between Near-village habitats (~50%) and High-altitude habitats (~50%), suggesting movement through transitional landscapes during descent. Observations for the Rainy season, were distributed across multiple habitats, with approximately 43% in High altitude areas, 23% at Salt lick sites, 12% in Near-village habitats, and 8% in Jungle habitats. This pattern indicates that Takin occupy alpine, subalpine, and salt lick habitats during the period of highest seasonal aggregation. During Winter, sightings were concentrated in High altitude habitats (~45%), followed by Jungle (~23%), Near-village habitat (~28%), and a small proportion at Salt lick sites (~4%), shows that Takin descend towards lower elevations and village forests whilst still maintaining some use of higher habitats and salt licks, particularly in Anjaw where these areas are closer together. Local respondents also observed that herds often gather along common migration routes near the snowline before moving towards accessible foraging sites, frequently following a single track and avoiding areas of harsh snow cover. Some herds were reported to reach salt lick and congregation sites earlier than others, and in certain cases Takin were said to make transboundary movements. Overall, the results show that Takin migration is a precise seasonal movement between habitats. They spend Spring in jungles, move to high-altitude areas and salt licks during the Rainy season for social congregation which are critical in the ecology of Takin acting as mating, grazing and rutting ground for them whilst fulfilling the nutrients lost in the long vertical migration and then descend towards lower forests and transitional habitats in Autumn and Winter, reflecting their generalist feeding behaviour and habitat use pattern. 3.1.2 Identification of key habitats and congregation sites TEK from the study area consistently highlighted the importance of specific habitat types for the Takin, particularly high-altitude forests, dense mid-elevation forests, near-village forests used seasonally, and mineral-rich salt licks. Amongst these, salt licks locally referred as “Aheko” (identified as salt licks) by the Idu Mishmi and further expedition surveys established that clusters of salt licks act as congregation sites. Utilising the information provided during key informant surveys and participatory field visits, six major congregation sites were identified within Dibang Valley district. These sites were typically located at elevations between approximately 3,500 and 4,500 msl and were characterised by open alpine meadows, sedge-dominated grasslands, patchy alpine scrub, and the presence of mineral-rich soils or sulphur-rich springs. Direct observations showed that more than 500 individuals could occur simultaneously within some congregation areas, with individual social groups generally comprising approximately 18–50 individuals primarily during the rainy season which is June to early September. In contrast, respondents from Anjaw district reported fewer large congregation sites, with sightings primarily concentrated in high-altitude forests rather than discrete salt lick locations. Respondents also reported that congregation is also connected to moon phase at some sites. This spatial variation and congregation pattern reflects differences in topography, habitat distribution, and accessibility across the two district. 3.1.3 Habitat use and seasonal variation A total of 133 seasonal records were obtained through key informant surveys, with observations unevenly distributed across seasons, primarily during Winter (n = 59) and the Rainy season (n = 55), and fewer records in Autumn (n = 3) and Spring (n = 1). Fifteen responses (n=15) were not included for analysis due to insufficient information. Habitat use of Takin showed clear spatial variation between Dibang and Anjaw district (Figure 2). In Dibang, sightings were most frequently associated with salt lick habitats, followed by dense forest, near-village, and high-altitude areas, with minimal use of slope or landslide habitats. In contrast, respondents from Anjaw reported Takin presence across a broader range of habitats, including high-altitude, salt lick, near-village, and dense forest areas, indicating more heterogeneous habitat use. This difference reflects the landscape-level variation between district, particularly in the elevational distribution of key habitats such as salt licks, which occur at lower elevations (1000–2500 msl) in Anjaw within subtropical forests, and at higher elevations (2500–4500 msl) in Dibang within alpine and high-altitude valleys. Habitat use derived from TEK responses was identified and classified into four categories based on field expeditions into High altitude, Jungle, Near-village habitat, and Salt lick. Habitat use varied across seasons (Figure 3). The results showed that Jungle and near-village habitats were more frequently used by takin during the Spring and Autumn seasons reflecting seasonal migration to lower elevations of tropical and subtropical forests. KIS responses indicated that the largest aggregations occurred during the rainy season. In addition to these social group observations, respondents reported substantially larger number of Takin occurring simultaneously at seasonal congregation sites with reported congregation size ranging from approximately 600 to 2,000 individuals and these respondent-reported estimates were treated as TEK rather than direct population estimates. They represent respondents’ estimates of the total number of Takins present within a seasonal congregation site. In contrast, respondents reported smaller aggregations during spring. Winter and autumn showed intermediate variation in reported congregation size (250) although these seasons did not exhibit the large-scale aggregations characteristic of the rainy season. Field observations provided further evidence that multiple Takin herds could occur simultaneously at seasonal congregation sites. During ocular observations at congregation and salt-lick sites, more than 500 individuals were observed simultaneously within the congregation area during some observation events in the study area. Camera-trap surveys generated more than 17,000 photographic captures of Takin over approximately 90 trap days across 116 camera-trap locations. These records documented repeated use of congregation and salt-lick sites by multiple Takin herds during the rainy season, from June to early September. The photographic records were used to corroborate the occurrence of multiple herds, seasonal congregation behaviour, and habitat use. During winter, Takin were more widely dispersed at lower elevations, and camera-trap observations indicated more scattered groups using forest habitats for foraging. This pattern was consistent with respondent reports that Takin descend towards forest areas near-village habitats, including areas around 700 msl elevation, during winter. Congregation size also varied amongst habitat types. Salt-lick habitats exhibited the highest median congregation size (approximately 40 individuals per recorded social group/observation unit) and greater variation in the distribution of observed group sizes compared with other habitats. High-altitude habitats showed a slightly lower median social-group size (approximately 30 individuals), included an occasional large congregation of approximately 300 individuals, particularly in Anjaw at elevations of approximately 2,500–3,000 msl. In contrast, jungle and near-village habitats were generally characterised by smaller social groups, typically below 50 individuals, although occasional larger aggregations of approximately 200–250 individuals were recorded in jungle habitats, potentially reflecting proximity to migration routes, congregation areas, or smaller salt-lick sites. These observations indicate that several social groups may aggregate within suitable habitats, particularly around salt-lick sites, whilst the size of individual social units remains substantially smaller than the total congregation. A Kruskal–Walli’s rank-sum test showed no statistically significant difference in the distribution of recorded social-group sizes amongst habitat types (χ² = 4.989, p = 0.173), suggesting that habitat type alone did not significantly explain variation in the size of individual social groups. Across seasons, the rainy season showed the greatest variability in congregation size and the largest aggregations, whereas spring showed the smallest variation. Overall, the findings indicate strong seasonal aggregation behaviour, with the rainy season associated with the concentration of multiple Takin herds at salt-lick and other congregation sites. 3.1.5 Behavioural observations and patterns across seasons Responses on behavioural observations from the respondents varied between district (Figure 4). In both Dibang and Anjaw, movement was the most frequently reported behaviour, indicating that most sightings occurred whilst Takin were actively moving. Respondents from both district also reported that Takin commonly grazed whilst in motion suggesting that they spend a considerable amount of time moving and foraging simultaneously. In Dibang, apart from movement, the most frequently observed behaviours were drinking at salt licks, resting and fighting, whereas calling, lactating and scanning were reported relatively infrequent. In Anjaw, after movement, calling and drinking were commonly observed along with sleeping and regurgitating behaviours, indicating differences in behavioural observations across different habitats (Figure 5). Tiger emerged as the dominant perceived predator in both Dibang and Anjaw, followed by Bear, Leopard, and Dhole. Human-related threats were reported less frequently but were present in both district, primarily comprising indigenous hunting. The perceived predator composition differences showed a significant association between district and perceived threat composition (χ² = 80.74, p < 0.001) with moderate to strong effect strength (Cramér’s V = 0.51). This indicates that the relative importance of threats differed significantly between Dibang (Tiger > Leopard > Dhole > Human > Bear > Marbled cat), and Anjaw (Tiger > Bear > Leopard > Dhole > Human > Marbled cat) (Figure 11). Predator records reported through interviews represent respondent perceptions based on direct observations, evidence of predation (e.g., carcasses or fresh kills), and species identification verified using standardised photographs during interviews. Reports involving smaller carnivores, such as the marbled cat, were interpreted as opportunistic predation or scavenging events involving juveniles or carcasses rather than predation on adult Takin. In Anjaw, a majority of respondents expressed support for conservation of Takin (Yes: n = 29), whilst a substantial proportion reported No need (n = 21) and a smaller group expressed ambivalent views (Either/Both ways: n= 7). In Dibang, responses were more evenly divided, with No (n = 17) slightly varying than Yes (n = 14) indicating a mixed opinion on need for conservation of Takin in both the district. 4.6 Group size, seasonal migration, activity pattern The deployment of camera traps with a sampling effort of 90 days per trap resulted in 17,300 photographic captures of Takin. These captures were concentrated within identified congregation sites, to validate and complement TEK by providing information on ethology, foraging ecology, and herd dynamics during the peak congregational period. The high frequency of photographic records provided baseline information on Takin occurrence, activity patterns, and use of congregation sites. Ocular observations also revealed that these congregation sites play a critical role in the life cycle of Takin by documenting incidents of rutting and mating, lactating behaviours. The foraging indicated a mixed-feeding pattern as the Takin were observed grazing on various grasses in congregation sites whilst browsing on bamboo leaf flushes during the day whilst resting. This dietary composition reflects a generalist herbivore strategy depending on seasonally availability, during the summer months. The congregational events were strictly seasonal, occurring from June to August. High concentrations of Takins were observed at these sites, with more than 500 individuals recorded simultaneously during some congregation events. The mean social-group size remained below 50 individuals, with individual herds typically comprising approximately 18–40 individuals. This suggests that whilst individual herds maintained stable social units, they merged into much larger aggregations during the peak summer congregation period showing a possible fission fusion in takin population (Figure 12). Figure 12 Activity pattern of Takin. The analysis of activity patterns (Figure 12) showed that the Takin remained active at congregation sites continuously over a 24-hour period, as multiple herds arrived and departed thorough the day and night, despite this, peak activity and social interactions were predominantly observed during crepuscular periods. However, a significant nocturnal activity was also observed indicating the high utilisation of congregation sites and importance of salt licks in the life cycle of takin. 5 Discussion This study showed that Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) provided robust insights into the behaviour, habitat use, seasonal movements and conservation of the Mishmi Takin. More importantly, the findings provide a structured source of ecological evidence rather than merely anecdotal information. The ecological observations shared by indigenous communities were corroborated through camera trapping, field expeditions and direct observations. These results support previous studies showing that long-term site-specific observations can complement scientific studies and provide baseline ecological information, particularly for elusive and data-deficient species inhabiting remote landscapes (Berkes, 2017; Huntington, 2000; Tengö et al., 2014). One of the key ecological patterns documented through TEK was the seasonal vertical migration of the Mishmi Takin. Responses described seasonal movements from lower-elevation forests during winter through mid-elevation dense jungles to alpine meadows, high-altitude valleys, and mineral-rich salt licks during the rainy season where large congregations occur. Camera-trap data confirmed usage of congregation sites and provided baseline information on the activity pattern and congregation dynamics. Similar seasonal altitudinal migration has also been reported for other Takin subspecies in China and Bhutan, where herds move to higher elevations during summer and return to lower elevations during colder months (Guan et al., 2013; Sharma et al., 2015; Wangchuk et al., 2016; Sangay et al., 2016; Sangay et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2020). Whilst these findings indicate that seasonal migration is a fundamental ecological characteristic of the species, variation in distribution of congregation sites and migration timing across the district in the distribution range suggests local topography and habitat location and resource availability influence movement patterns within the Mishmi Hills landscape (Schaller et al., 1986). Salt licks were identified as critical ecological features as respondents reported ecological importance of salt licks and associated congregation areas. Local respondents and field surveys consistently identified these sites utilised for social aggregation, foraging, and reproductive behaviours and these mineral lick sites are recognised as critical resources for mountain ungulate as they provide essential mineral that may be limited in montane ecosystem whilst serving as critical rutting sites (Ayotte et al., 2006; Matsubayashi et al., 2007). The aggregation sizes were derived from TEK and are community-based observational estimates rather than precise population counts. Nevertheless, the reports across respondents and with camera-trap observations showed that these seasonal congregation patterns are ecologically meaningful. The identification of these sites through TEK highlights the significance of TEK for ecology and conservation. Further protecting salt licks, congregation sites, and associated movement corridors should therefore be considered a conservation priority for the Mishmi Takin. Respondents also recognised a range of ecological and anthropogenic threats affecting the species. Predators such as tiger, dhole, leopard, and bear were considered major natural threats and responses involving smaller carnivores, particularly the marbled cat, were interpreted as opportunistic predation or scavenging events involving juveniles or carcasses, based on detailed respondent descriptions. In addition, respondents reported increasing human influences, including habitat disturbance, changing hunting practices and the gradual loss of traditional cultural values and taboos that regulated hunting in the Eastern Himalaya (Aiyadurai, 2007). These observations emphasise that effective conservation planning must consider both ecological and changing socio-cultural conditions that influence human wildlife relationships. Beyond species specific findings, this study contributes to broader discussions on the role of indigenous knowledge in conservation science. The TEK observations and ecological validations demonstrate that TEK can generate reliable ecological inference for lesser studied species. In remote mountain ecosystems where long-term ecological datasets are scarce, TEK can provide baseline information on identification of critical congregation sites and salt lick sites, activity pattern, habitat use, seasonal movements, and threats whilst helping to guide ecological surveys and conservation through contributing ecological understanding. The identification of distinct TEK profiles further demonstrates that ecological knowledge is not uniformly distributed amongst respondents but varies according to landscape exposure, observation experience, and conservation awareness. The multivariate clustering analyses indicate that different respondent groups contribute complementary ecological information, ranging from detailed behavioural observations and knowledge of seasonal movements to awareness of conservation threats and large herd aggregations. Recognising these differences can help identify key knowledge holders for participatory biodiversity monitoring, community-based conservation programmes, and future ecological surveys, thereby improving the integration of indigenous knowledge into conservation planning and the cluster separation in PCA ordination and their ecological interpretability support the usefulness of the identified TEK profiles for describing variation in respondent knowledge. This baseline study is one of a first such attempt to integrate TEK with species ecology which may have some limitations such as Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) was derived from respondent interviews and may be influenced by recall bias and differences in landscape exposure. Estimates of large Takin aggregations represent community-based observations of direct population counts, whilst TEK based camera trapping was limited to habitats, seasonal accessibility, Potential respondent bias, uncertainty in aggregation estimates which may also not have captured the full extent of seasonal movements. The hierarchical clustering was exploratory, and respondent-reported threats reflect community perceptions supported by field observations where available. Despite these limitations, integrating TEK with ecological surveys provides valuable baseline ecological information for the conservation and management of the Mishmi Takin in remote mountain ecosystems. Overall, the study demonstrates that integrating TEK with ecological methods provides a robust framework for investigating data-deficient species and identifying conservation priorities. By combining community-based ecological observations with field surveys, the research not only improves understanding of the Mishmi Takin but also highlights the broader potential of knowledge co-production for biodiversity conservation. The findings suggest that effective conservation outcomes in the Eastern Himalaya will depend on recognising indigenous communities as active knowledge holders and conservation partners whose ecological knowledge can strengthen research, monitoring, and decision-making in such remote mountain landscapes and rare wildlife species. Ethics statement This study involved voluntary interviews with indigenous community members to document Traditional Ecological Knowledge related to the Mishmi Takin. Before each interview, the objectives of the study were explained to all participants, and verbal informed consent was obtained. Participation was entirely voluntary, and respondents could decline to answer any question or withdraw from the interview at any stage. No personal identifiers were recorded, and all responses were analysed anonymously. According to institutional guidelines, formal approval from a Human Research Ethics Committee was not required because the study involved non-invasive interviews on ecological knowledge and did not collect sensitive personal or biomedical information. Interviews were conducted with respect for local customs, cultural practices, and indigenous knowledge systems. Traditional Ecological Knowledge documented during the study was collected solely for research and conservation purposes and is presented in a manner that safeguards the identity and interests of participating communities.
