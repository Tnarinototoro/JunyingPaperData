Almulhim * Department of Urban and Regional Planning, College of Architecture and Planning, Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University, Dammam, Saudi Arabia Article metrics View details 1 Introduction Cities across the Global South are entering a decisive decade for sustainable development. Rapid urbanization and intensifying climate change are placing growing pressure on urban sustainability, increasing environmental risks while challenging cities' social, economic, and infrastructural systems (Almulhim and Cobbinah, 2026; UN-Habitat, 2024). These pressures are unfolding alongside widening socio-spatial inequalities, infrastructure and resource constraints, and profound technological and economic change, with climate impacts often falling disproportionately on vulnerable and marginalized urban populations (UN-Habitat, 2024). Climate-induced migration adds another layer of complexity, as environmental stress can accelerate rural-to-urban mobility, placing additional pressure on infrastructure, services, and informal and peri-urban development in receiving cities (Almulhim et al., 2024). These pressures do not occur independently. They interact within increasingly complex urban systems, where decisions about land use, housing, mobility, infrastructure, ecosystems, and public services can generate consequences far beyond the sectors in which they originate. The significance of these interactions also extends beyond city boundaries, as urban growth increasingly shapes land cover, hydrological systems, climate, biogeochemical processes, and habitats at broader scales (Espey et al., 2024). Although the term Global South encompasses highly diverse urban contexts and should not imply a single development trajectory, the nature and intensity of urban challenges vary considerably across regions, from rapid urban expansion and informality to housing pressures, climate vulnerability, displacement, and resource scarcity (United Nations, 2026). Nevertheless, many cities across Africa, Asia, Latin America, and the Middle East face a common challenge: the pace and complexity of urban change increasingly exceed the capacity of conventional planning institutions and instruments to respond effectively. This mismatch between inherited planning approaches and contemporary urban realities has long been recognized, particularly in relation to informality, inequality, rapid urbanization, and the assumptions underpinning planning theory and practice (Watson, 2009). A decade of expanded urban policy commitments has not resolved this challenge. The United Nations (2026), in its assessment of progress in implementing the New Urban Agenda, reports that implementation continues to lag behind the growing scale and complexity of urban challenges. A recent study further shows that sustainable development outcomes remain highly uneven across space and that socioeconomic progress can coexist with environmental deterioration, reinforcing the need for planning approaches that address trade-offs across multiple dimensions of urban sustainability (Guo et al., 2026). The challenge, however, is not a lack of ideas. Sustainable urban planning now draws on a rapidly expanding repertoire of approaches, including resilience planning, nature-based solutions, participatory governance, smart-city technologies, integrated infrastructure, and data-driven decision-making. Yet the translation of these approaches into sustained urban transformation remains uneven. Climate adaptation, for example, depends on institutional capacity, meaningful participation, and the ability to address the needs of poor and vulnerable communities (Chu et al., 2016). Similarly, recent research on nature-based solutions highlights governance, financial feasibility, social acceptance, and context-specific approaches as critical conditions for effective urban planning and implementation (Mirsafa et al., 2025). Informality further complicates this transition because formal and informal institutions, economic activities, and socio-ecological processes are deeply interdependent rather than functioning as separate urban systems (Eakin et al., 2025). The central question for the coming decade is therefore no longer simply what cities should do to become more sustainable, but how planning itself must change to enable sustainable urban transitions under conditions of complexity, uncertainty, informality, and inequality. This Grand Challenge argues that incremental improvements to conventional planning will not be sufficient. It identifies six interrelated paradigm shifts: from sectoral planning to systems thinking; from static plans to adaptive and resilient planning; from formal planning to inclusive urbanism; from growth-centered planning to wellbeing and equity; from engineering-dominated solutions to ecological urbanism; and, ultimately, from planning as control to planning as continuous learning. 2 Why conventional planning paradigms are reaching their limits Conventional urban planning has played an essential role in organizing land use, guiding infrastructure investment, regulating development, and articulating long-term visions for cities. The challenge is therefore not that conventional planning has become irrelevant, but that many of its underlying assumptions are increasingly insufficient for the conditions under which cities in the Global South are evolving. Planning instruments have often been built around expectations of relatively predictable urban growth, effective formal institutions, clearly defined administrative responsibilities, reliable data, and development that can be managed through formal regulatory systems. In many rapidly urbanizing contexts, however, urban change unfolds through a more complex combination of formal and informal processes, uneven institutional capacity, infrastructure deficits, resource constraints, and increasingly uncertain climatic and socioeconomic conditions. This mismatch is increasingly visible at the implementation level. The United Nations (2026), in its assessment of progress in implementing the New Urban Agenda, reports that despite the wider adoption of national urban and housing policies, implementation has not kept pace with the growing scale and complexity of urban challenges. Persistent gaps between national policy frameworks and municipal planning instruments, limited local capacity, fragmented institutional mandates, weak monitoring systems, and inadequate financing continue to constrain implementation. These broader constraints are also evident in rapidly urbanizing Saudi cities, where population growth, environmental pressures, implementation limitations, and mismatches between national priorities and local urban conditions have challenged planning practice (Almulhim and Cobbinah, 2024). These conditions reinforce the need for context-sensitive planning approaches in the Global South, particularly given the limitations of uncritically transferring planning knowledge and models developed in substantially different socioeconomic and institutional contexts (Verdini et al., 2025). A second limitation lies in the fragmented treatment of problems that are systemically interconnected. Land-use decisions influence mobility and infrastructure demand; housing conditions shape exposure to climate hazards; water, energy, ecosystems, and public health interact across administrative and sectoral boundaries. Yet institutions responsible for these systems often operate through separate mandates, budgets, datasets, and planning cycles. The United Nations (2026) similarly identifies fragmented institutional mandates and weak coordination across levels of government as persistent barriers to translating urban policy into spatially coordinated investment and implementation. Evidence from urban climate governance illustrates how fragmented institutional arrangements can constrain integrated responses even where climate risks are recognized (Pazhuhan, 2023). Similar problems appear in adaptation finance: analysis of projects in the Global South found limited attention to uncertainty and relatively weak mechanisms for generating transformative effects across sectors (Forino et al., 2022). Climate change further exposes the limits of planning based predominantly on stable baselines and anticipated trajectories. Increasing climatic variability, compound risks, migration, economic disruption, and rapid technological change make urban futures progressively harder to predict. This does not eliminate the need for long-term plans; rather, it changes what long-term planning must be capable of doing. Earlier work on cities in the Global South already argued that climate adaptation requires governance approaches able to connect different spatial and temporal scales, accommodate multiple forms of knowledge, and integrate formal and informal responses (Birkmann et al., 2010). More recent research similarly emphasizes that adaptive governance must be interpreted through the particular institutional and social conditions of the Global South rather than transferred uncritically from elsewhere (Yasmin et al., 2019). These limitations should therefore not be understood as isolated failures of individual plans or institutions. Collectively, they point to a deeper mismatch between a planning paradigm designed largely around sectoral organization, predictability, formality, and control, and urban realities increasingly characterized by interdependence, uncertainty, informality, and rapid change. The task ahead is not to abandon established planning instruments, but to reposition them within a broader paradigm capable of integrating systems, adapting to uncertainty, recognizing diverse forms of urbanism and knowledge, prioritizing equity and ecological processes, and learning continuously from implementation. Rather, it represents a reorientation of planning toward more systemic, adaptive, inclusive, equitable, ecological, and learning-based approaches capable of responding to increasingly complex and uncertain urban conditions. 3 Six paradigm shifts for sustainable urban planning in the Global South The limitations outlined above suggest that the challenge is not simply to improve individual planning instruments, but to reconsider some of the principles through which urban planning understands and responds to change. The six shifts presented below are neither mutually exclusive nor intended as universal prescriptions for the highly diverse cities of the Global South. Rather, they identify complementary directions through which planning can become more capable of engaging with interdependence, uncertainty, informality, inequality, ecological limits, and institutional change. Their value lies not in replacing established planning practices wholesale, but in expanding the ways in which urban problems are framed, decisions are made, and implementation is evaluated. Overall, the six shifts provide an integrated, Global South-focused perspective for framing the interconnected challenges that sustainable urban planning must address over the next decade and for linking these challenges to the research priorities outlined in Section 4. 3.1 From sectoral planning to systems thinking Urban sustainability is inherently multidimensional and multiscalar, meaning that interventions that generate benefits in one dimension or at one spatial scale may produce different outcomes or trade-offs in others (Seto et al., 2017). Urban sustainability challenges rarely conform to the administrative and sectoral boundaries through which cities are planned. Decisions about land use influence mobility, infrastructure demand, housing, ecological conditions, and exposure to environmental risks, while water, energy, transport, and other infrastructure systems depend on one another. Sustainable urban systems are therefore increasingly understood through the interactions among social actors, institutions, ecological processes, and interconnected infrastructures rather than through individual sectors alone (Ramaswami et al., 2012). A housing decision, for example, may alter travel demand, energy consumption, emissions, environmental exposure, and ultimately patterns of inequality. This need for integration is also reflected in the United Nations (2026) assessment of the New Urban Agenda, which emphasizes more coordinated approaches across the social, economic, and environmental dimensions of sustainable urban development. A systems perspective requires planning to focus on the relationships, dependencies, trade-offs, and feedbacks between these domains. The planning unit should therefore increasingly become the urban system rather than the individual sector. Concepts such as urban metabolism, infrastructure interdependencies, cross-sector dependencies, and water-energy-land relationships can help reveal how interventions in one domain generate consequences elsewhere and support more integrated decision-making (Kennedy et al., 2012). Yet systems thinking should not become synonymous with increasingly complex modeling. In many Global South cities, incomplete data, fragmented institutional responsibilities, and limited technical capacity may constrain highly data-intensive approaches. More importantly, models alone cannot resolve competing interests, unequal power, or questions about who benefits from particular planning choices. The Grand Challenge is therefore to translate systems thinking from a compelling conceptual ideal into actionable planning practice under real institutional, data, and resource constraints. Research must determine which interdependencies matter most for planning decisions, how cross-sector trade-offs can be assessed transparently, and how integrated approaches can remain practicable where data and governance capacity are uneven. 3.2 From static plans to adaptive and resilient planning Long-term urban planning has traditionally depended on the ability to anticipate future population, land-use, infrastructure, and development needs with reasonable confidence. Yet the conditions shaping urban futures are becoming increasingly uncertain. Climate extremes, migration, resource scarcity, demographic change, economic shocks, and geopolitical disruptions can alter development trajectories in ways that fixed plans cannot fully anticipate. Evidence from Dhaka, for example, demonstrates how uncertainties associated with both climate change and urbanization can push existing strategies beyond their effective limits, reinforcing the need to move from predictive planning toward approaches based on continuous monitoring and adaptation (Ahmed et al., 2018). Adaptive and resilient planning therefore requires a shift from treating planning and implementation as a largely linear process toward an iterative approach in which anticipation, implementation, monitoring, evaluation, and adjustment are continuously connected. This shift is consistent with resilience-oriented planning, which conceptualizes urban planning not as a static process but as one that must respond to the dynamic and non-equilibrium nature of urban systems (Sharifi and Yamagata, 2018). Scenario planning, adaptive pathways, risk-informed decision-making, periodic reassessment, and resilience thinking can enable cities to prepare for multiple plausible futures rather than relying on a single expected trajectory. This does not imply abandoning long-term planning. Rather, long-term sustainability objectives should provide strategic direction while allowing the pathways toward those objectives to evolve as risks, knowledge, technologies, and urban conditions change. Experience from Quito, Surat, and Durban further indicates that sustained political leadership, institutional coordination, stakeholder involvement, learning, and capacity building are important for embedding adaptation within urban governance rather than treating it as a one-off planning exercise (Anguelovski et al., 2014). The Grand Challenge is therefore to institutionalize adaptive planning without allowing flexibility to become short-termism or reactive decision-making. Research should examine how monitoring thresholds, periodic review mechanisms, and adaptive pathways can be embedded within statutory planning systems while preserving accountability, institutional continuity, and long-term sustainability objectives. 3.3 From formal planning to inclusive urbanism In many cities across the Global South, the lived city extends far beyond the city represented in statutory plans and formal regulatory systems. The scale of this challenge is substantial: more than 1.1 billion people worldwide were living in slums or informal settlements in 2022, an increase of over 120 million since 2016 (United Nations, 2026). Informal settlements, informal economies, peri-urban development, community networks, and locally organized forms of service provision are therefore not peripheral exceptions but integral components of how urbanization occurs and how cities function. Research across African cities similarly shows that formal planning often struggles to accommodate informal, temporary, and citizen-led urban processes, despite the important role these practices play in shaping urban space and responding to unmet needs. Rather than treating such practices simply as deviations from formal planning, more inclusive approaches can recognize citizens as active place-makers and better integrate appropriate informal and adaptable forms of urban development into planning processes (Andres et al., 2021). This direction is also reflected in the United Nations (2026) assessment of the New Urban Agenda, which calls for integrated and place-based approaches to informal settlements that combine adequate housing with climate resilience, public health, safety, and livelihoods while avoiding forced evictions. Yet planning systems can marginalize these realities when informality is treated primarily as a regulatory deficit to be eliminated rather than as a complex urban condition that must be understood and engaged. Importantly, informality should not be equated exclusively with poverty or marginality, as it may also be produced through entrepreneurship, wealth, power, and the actions of actors operating across formal and informal institutional boundaries (Swapan et al., 2020). Research on community-based flood adaptation in informal settlements across the Global South shows that local knowledge, grassroots organization, participatory governance, and locally appropriate innovations can support adaptation, while limited funding, weak governance, and legal exclusion constrain their wider and longer-term impact (Bhanye, 2025a). Evidence from diverse Global South contexts further demonstrates the importance of locally grounded knowledge, community networks, and participatory approaches in enabling adaptation, while highlighting institutional, financial, and governance constraints on community responses (Adelina et al., 2020). More broadly, community-based adaptation scholarship emphasizes context-specific knowledge, inclusive decision-making, local ownership, and attention to power relations, while also underscoring the need to connect locally led initiatives with wider governance structures (Selje et al., 2024). Inclusive urbanism therefore requires more than extending formal planning procedures into informal areas. It requires planning to recognize residents and community organizations as producers of urban knowledge and active participants in shaping urban development. Evidence from Nairobi illustrates how co-production of infrastructure and public services can create spaces for more inclusive governance when communities and state actors engage in sustained deliberation (Otsuki, 2016). However, participation alone is insufficient: evidence from informal settlements in Accra shows that having a voice in participatory processes does not necessarily translate into substantive influence over decisions (Bandauko et al., 2025). The Grand Challenge is therefore not simply how to formalize informal urban systems, but how planning can recognize, engage with, and improve them without eroding the social and economic functions on which residents depend. Research should identify institutional arrangements through which co-production, local and indigenous knowledge, and meaningful participation can influence statutory planning while strengthening rights, accountability, and access to services. 3.4 From growth-centered planning to wellbeing and equity Urban development has long been evaluated through indicators of economic growth, investment, infrastructure provision, and physical development. However, recent global evidence shows that economic growth alone is a poor proxy for multidimensional sustainable development, as gains in socioeconomic activity can coexist with worsening environmental conditions and substantial spatial inequalities (Guo et al., 2026). While these dimensions remain important, they provide an incomplete account of whether urban transformation improves people's lives or distributes its benefits fairly. Sustainability planning itself has been framed as requiring a broader paradigm shift toward long-term and holistic thinking across environmental, economic, and social-equity objectives (Wheeler, 2016). A more meaningful conception of sustainable urban development must therefore ask not only whether cities are becoming more productive, efficient, or environmentally sustainable, but for whom these gains are produced, whose knowledge and priorities shape planning decisions, and at whose expense particular urban futures are pursued. This requires shifting attention toward quality of life, health, accessibility, affordability, and the distributive, procedural, recognitional, and spatial dimensions of urban justice. Health is particularly important in this broader conception of urban wellbeing because planning and built-environment decisions shape population health through environmental exposures, physical activity, mobility, access to green space, housing, and social conditions (Almulhim, 2026a). Sustainable transport, green infrastructure, and health-supportive urban design can therefore generate benefits that extend beyond environmental performance to physical and mental health and quality of life. More broadly, contemporary urban health thinking increasingly recognizes health and health equity as outcomes of interconnected social, environmental, economic, and institutional conditions, requiring multisectoral and systems-based approaches to urban planning and governance (Grant et al., 2025). Yet health remains insufficiently integrated into urban climate planning: an analysis of 55 city climate adaptation plans found that although health impacts were widely recognized, comprehensive health adaptation was largely absent, with mental and social health particularly neglected (O'Donnell and Sovacool, 2026). This gap reinforces the need to treat health as an explicit dimension of sustainable urban planning and equity, rather than only as an indirect co-benefit of environmental interventions (O'Donnell and Sovacool, 2026). Comparative research on urban sustainability initiatives has found that, although some principles of just sustainability are reflected in practice, distributive and procedural dimensions of justice remain relatively rare, reinforcing the need to place justice more explicitly within sustainability agendas (Broto and Westman, 2019). This question is particularly important in the Global South, where planning frameworks influence whose knowledge is recognized, how environmental risks are framed, and which sustainability futures become actionable (Liwur, 2026). Crucially, sustainability interventions are not inherently equitable. Urban greening, climate adaptation, and other sustainability initiatives can generate uneven benefits, shift risks between communities, or contribute to displacement when questions of justice are insufficiently addressed (a, nd Sekulova Kotsila et al.2022). Research on green gentrification, for example, demonstrates that environmental improvements can contribute to socio-spatial inequalities when increased environmental amenity is accompanied by displacement or unequal access to its benefits. Evidence from Medellín illustrates this risk in the Global South, where green infrastructure interventions have dispossessed low-income communities of land, location, and social capital (Anguelovski et al., 2018). Equity should therefore be treated not as a secondary co-benefit of sustainable planning, but as a fundamental criterion by which its success is evaluated. More broadly, sustainability transformations should be assessed not only by their environmental outcomes but also by their social consequences and the extent to which affected groups are included in decision-making, highlighting the importance of distributive, procedural, and recognitional dimensions of justice (Bennett et al., 2019). The Grand Challenge is to develop planning frameworks and evaluation methods capable of determining not only whether urban sustainability goals are being achieved, but who benefits, who bears the costs, and who has influence over the decisions that shape those outcomes. Research should move beyond aggregate urban indicators toward approaches that reveal how sustainability outcomes are distributed across places and social groups and whether interventions reduce or reproduce existing inequalities. 3.5 From engineering-dominated solutions to ecological urbanism Urban sustainability has long relied on engineered infrastructure to manage flooding, water, heat, waste, and other environmental pressures. Such infrastructure remains indispensable, but engineering-dominated approaches alone are increasingly inadequate for challenges shaped by interactions among ecological processes, infrastructure systems, and urban development. The required shift is therefore not from engineering to ecology, but toward a broader conception of urban infrastructure in which technical and ecological systems are planned together. This is particularly relevant in the Global South, where ecological planning has increasingly emphasized ecosystem services, ecological restoration, and the translation of ecological knowledge into urban action, while also confronting weak institutional support, limited investment in ecosystems and their services, and inadequate policy and planning frameworks (Cilliers et al., 2021). The United Nations (2026) similarly emphasizes the need to embed nature-based solutions within land-use planning and local investment decisions rather than treating them as stand-alone environmental interventions. Nature-based solutions, blue-green infrastructure, biodiversity-sensitive planning, ecosystem-service approaches, and circular resource systems can expand this infrastructure portfolio by providing multiple ecological and social functions alongside conventional systems. Circular economy approaches can complement this transition by shifting urban resource management away from linear patterns of extraction, consumption, and disposal toward more efficient and cyclical use of materials, energy, and other resources, while reducing waste and expanding opportunities for reuse and recycling (Muñoz and Navia, 2021). Studies of Global South cities show that nature-based solutions are being applied through green and blue infrastructure to address objectives including climate resilience, biodiversity support, ecosystem restoration, and public-space provision (Lakshmisha et al., 2024). A synthesis of research on urbanization in Africa similarly shows that ecosystems can provide foundations for green infrastructure that serves urban needs while safeguarding biodiversity (Güneralp et al., 2018). Recent research also identifies water management and climate resilience as prominent functions of urban nature-based solutions in the Global South, alongside biodiversity conservation, place regeneration, and social objectives (Castaldo et al., 2025). The effectiveness of these approaches, however, depends on the social, ecological, economic, and infrastructural contexts in which they are implemented, reinforcing the need for context-sensitive and integrated planning (McPhearson et al., 2025). Implementation also remains shaped by practical and institutional constraints, including financial sustainability, institutional capacity, awareness, and access to information (Castaldo et al., 2025). Further research points to continuing challenges related to governance, financing, equity, and long-term assessment (Zakaria et al., 2026). The planning challenge is therefore not to choose between gray and green infrastructure, but to determine how they can be combined within hybrid infrastructure systems suited to local ecological, social, and institutional conditions. The Grand Challenge is to develop robust approaches for evaluating, financing, maintaining, and scaling these systems while ensuring that their benefits are equitably distributed. Research should assess not only long-term environmental performance, but also governance capacity, accessibility, social outcomes, and potential unintended effects, including exclusion and displacement. 3.6 From planning as control to planning as learning Planning has traditionally sought to reduce uncertainty through prediction, regulation, and control. Yet the growing complexity and interdependence of urban systems make it increasingly difficult to anticipate how environmental change, technological disruption, demographic pressures, and social responses will interact over time. Planning must therefore strengthen the capacity of cities to learn and adjust as conditions change. Monitoring and evaluation mechanisms can support this adaptive capacity by enabling planning strategies to be revised in response to new information and emerging circumstances (Almulhim, 2025). This requires a more iterative approach in which planning becomes a continuous process of observing, experimenting, evaluating, learning, and adapting. Digital technologies can strengthen this learning capacity by expanding how urban conditions are observed, interpreted, and tested. The United Nations (2026) identifies persistent gaps in timely, comparable, and disaggregated urban data, while highlighting the potential of geospatial technologies, remote sensing, and artificial intelligence to strengthen urban data collection and analysis. Urban observatories, GIS, citizen-generated data, real-time monitoring, scenario modeling, artificial intelligence, and digital twins can support monitoring, experimentation, and the exploration of alternative futures. However, their maturity and practical value vary considerably. A recent study of smart technologies and urban resilience finds relatively stronger empirical support for GIS and IoT applications in risk mapping, early warning, and infrastructure management, whereas AI and digital twins remain more commonly at pilot or conceptual stages (Varzeshi et al., 2025). Research on urban digital twins similarly highlights their potential for understanding complex urban processes and exploring possible futures, while identifying data management, model integration, usability, and stakeholder involvement as continuing implementation challenges (Therias and Rafiee, 2023). Yet planning as learning cannot depend on technological sophistication alone. In the Global South, the adoption of digital technologies and AI remains conditioned by digital infrastructure, institutional and governance capacity, financial constraints, and unequal access to technology (Das, 2025). Citizen-generated data and crowdsourcing can broaden the knowledge available to planners by incorporating local observations and experiences, but their effectiveness is similarly shaped by technological, administrative, socio-economic, and cultural conditions (Diop et al., 2022). Digital divides can systematically influence who contributes to crowdsourced data and, consequently, what information becomes represented in the resulting datasets (Young et al., 2020). AI-based planning raises related concerns, as data quality, institutional readiness, model transparency, digital inequalities, and risks of algorithmic exclusion can constrain the effectiveness and inclusiveness of AI applications (Bhanye, 2025b). Digital transformation should therefore not be treated as a technological substitute for participatory planning. Digital approaches can enhance scalability, efficiency, and data-informed decision-making, but persistent digital divides and risks of tokenistic participation reinforce the need to combine digital and non-digital forms of engagement (Almulhim and Abubakar, 2026). The Grand Challenge is therefore to build planning institutions that can learn continuously from both digital and situated knowledge while ensuring that increasingly data-driven systems remain accountable and inclusive. The central questions are not only what AI and digital technologies can do for planning, but who controls the data, whose knowledge becomes visible, who participates in interpretation, and who may be excluded from the learning process. 4 Research priorities for the next decade If these six paradigm shifts define the direction of travel, the research challenge for the next decade is to determine how they can be operationalized, evaluated, and scaled across highly diverse Global South contexts. This challenge extends beyond identifying new planning approaches to understanding the conditions under which they can generate durable and equitable urban transformations. Recent urban research agendas similarly emphasize complexity, equity and inclusion, environmental change, and innovation in lower-income contexts, alongside stronger connections between knowledge production and urban action (Bai et al., 2025). Looking further toward 2050, emerging priorities also highlight the need to move beyond proof of concept toward implementation and evaluation at scale, while integrating nature, inclusion, governance, and technological change into urban sustainability research (Elmqvist et al., 2026). This implementation challenge is also fundamentally local: the United Nations (2026) emphasizes that translating global urban commitments into tangible outcomes depends on empowered local and regional governments, effective multilevel governance, adequate institutional capacity, and stronger coordination across levels of government. Seven research priorities follow from this challenge (Figure 2). First, systems integration: how can integrated urban systems models become usable and context-sensitive planning tools in cities where data, institutional capacity, and technical resources remain constrained? Second, planning under uncertainty: how can adaptive institutions make and revise long-term decisions under deep climatic, demographic, economic, and political uncertainty without sacrificing strategic direction? Third, informality and local knowledge: how can informal urban systems, community practices, and locally grounded knowledge be incorporated into planning without generating displacement, exclusion, or livelihood losses? Fourth, equity and justice: which context-sensitive methods and indicators can reveal inequalities in health and wellbeing alongside the distributive, procedural, spatial, and intergenerational dimensions of urban justice, rather than obscuring them within aggregate measures of urban performance, while enabling meaningful comparison across cities and over time? Fifth, ecological and hybrid infrastructure: under what ecological, institutional, financial, and social conditions can nature-based and hybrid infrastructure provide durable resilience while remaining equitable and financially viable? Sixth, AI and digital planning: how can AI, digital twins, remote sensing, and citizen-generated data strengthen planning while protecting privacy, reducing algorithmic bias, and preventing existing digital inequalities from being reproduced in new forms? Seventh, implementation and learning: how can the gap between policy ambition and implementation be reduced, and how can promising experiments move beyond isolated pilots to become institutionalized, adaptable, and scalable planning practices, particularly where governance capacity, financing, technical resources, and institutional continuity are constrained? Figure 2 Research priorities for operationalizing paradigm shifts in sustainable urban planning in the Global South. The seven research priorities translate the proposed paradigm shifts into a research agenda aimed at strengthening the capacities needed for sustainable urban planning over the next decade. The seven research priorities are intentionally cross-cutting and connect to multiple paradigm shifts, identifying research needs that can advance the proposed transformation in sustainable urban planning. They are intended to strengthen the planning capacities needed for the next decade: understanding urban complexity, responding to uncertainty, enhancing inclusion, advancing equity and justice, building resilient futures, and learning and adapting over time. These capacities represent the practical capabilities through which the proposed paradigm shifts can be translated into more responsive and context-sensitive planning across diverse Global South cities. Addressing these questions will also require a methodological shift. Greater emphasis should be placed on comparative and longitudinal research capable of explaining how planning interventions perform across contexts and over time, alongside transdisciplinary and co-produced research that brings academic, governmental, professional, and community knowledge into the research process. The need for more integrative and comparative urban sustainability research across disciplines, regions, and spatial scales has been recognized for some time (Seto et al., 2017). Experiences from African cities demonstrate both the potential of transdisciplinary approaches to connect different forms of knowledge and the institutional and practical challenges involved in sustaining them (Thondhlana et al., 2021). Comparative transdisciplinary research can generate learning across cities but requires methodological care to account for contextual differences across diverse urban settings (Simon et al., 2018). Most importantly, cities in the Global South should not remain primarily sites in which theories and planning models developed elsewhere are tested or applied. Research should increasingly recognize them as sites of conceptual innovation and theory building, capable of reshaping how urban sustainability itself is understood. This direction also requires greater attention to the capacities of Global South cities for innovation and experimentation and to the novel pathways to urban sustainability that may emerge from their particular conditions (Arku and Marais, 2021). Persistent geographical imbalances in urban knowledge production and research networks further reinforce the need for stronger and more multidirectional knowledge exchange across regions and contexts (Almulhim, 2026b; Bai et al., 2025). Recent work on Global South climate urbanism similarly calls for planning knowledge to be grounded more strongly in historical and local contexts and for new forms of knowledge co-production (Verdini et al., 2025). This requires not only broader geographical representation, but a redistribution of authority over which urban questions are asked, which forms of knowledge count, and who participates in building the theories that will guide sustainable urban planning over the next decade. 5 Conclusion The six paradigm shifts described here are not independent trends. They point toward a deeper transformation in how sustainable urban planning is understood and practiced. Planning for sustainable cities increasingly requires moving from deterministic assumptions toward adaptive approaches, from sectoral interventions toward systemic thinking, from technocratic decision-making toward more inclusive forms of governance, from growth-centered measures of progress toward wellbeing and equity, from infrastructure-centered responses toward integrated socio-ecological systems, and from planning as control toward planning as a continuous process of learning and adaptation. These shifts do not imply abandoning established planning tools, engineering expertise, formal institutions, or technological innovation. They call instead for repositioning these capacities within a broader planning paradigm capable of responding to complexity, uncertainty, ecological limits, social inequality, and diverse forms of urban knowledge. Such a paradigm cannot be translated into a single model for the Global South. The term encompasses cities with profoundly different histories, institutional arrangements, ecological conditions, economic capacities, political structures, technological resources, and patterns of formal and informal urbanization. Context responsiveness must therefore be a defining principle of sustainable urban planning rather than an adjustment made after universal models have been formulated. The challenge is to develop approaches that can travel across contexts without assuming that they will take the same form, produce the same outcomes, or require the same institutional pathways everywhere. The value of this framework lies in bringing these interrelated directions together within a Global South-focused perspective and connecting them to a forward-looking research agenda for the next decade. Progress over this period should consequently be judged not only by the sophistication of new plans, technologies, models, or sustainability interventions, but by whether they contribute to healthier, more equitable, and ecologically resilient urban futures and whether planning develops the capacity to understand urban complexity, respond to uncertainty, enhance inclusion, advance equity and justice, build resilient futures, and learn and adapt over time. The defining challenge of the next decade is therefore not simply to improve conventional planning, but to redefine sustainable urban planning around the realities, capacities, knowledge systems, and aspirations of cities in the Global South.
