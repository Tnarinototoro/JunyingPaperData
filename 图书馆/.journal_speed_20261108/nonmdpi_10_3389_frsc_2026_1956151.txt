Urban Greening Volume 8 - 2026 | https://doi.org/10.3389/frsc.2026.1956151 The design–stewardship disconnect in urban greening governance: a structured evidence synthesis XY Xiaoyan Ye * YX Yian Xu JT Jinyuan Tu Cai Yuanpei School of Art and Design, Shaoxing University, Shaoxing, Zhejiang, China Article metrics View details Abstract While global investments in urban green infrastructure (UGI) are accelerating, the long-term performance of these nature-based solutions is increasingly compromised by a critical disconnect between initial design and post-construction stewardship. To unpack this governance blind spot, this study analyzes 62 peer-reviewed publications. Operating under a specific methodological boundary condition, we applied a threshold-based selection criterion focusing exclusively on high-impact (Q1) journals. Consequently, this study is explicitly positioned as a structured synthesis of the mainstream theoretical discourse shaping current global UGI policy, rather than a globally exhaustive empirical census. Records were coded across five critical dimensions: funding, governance, aesthetics, equity, and technology. Our synthesis reveals frequent co-occurrences of governance and funding deficits, particularly exacerbating technology gaps and spatial inequity. Crucially, we identify a structural paradox: capital-intensive designs frequently intersect with profound institutional and financial barriers, whereas naturalized designs recurrently face strong esthetic friction. We conceptualize this phenomenon as the “CAPEX-OPEX divide” in urban environmental governance. Based on these systemic associations, we advance a comprehensive framework comprising three core stewardship propositions—administrative under-resourcing, esthetic conflict, and unequal stewardship burdens. This synthesis shifts the academic and policy focus from mere UGI creation to sustainable, equitable long-term maintenance. 1 Introduction Urban green infrastructure (UGI) and nature-based solutions have become prominent components of urban climate adaptation, biodiversity, and public-health strategies (Kabisch et al., 2016; Pauleit et al., 2019; Seddon et al., 2020). Cities invest heavily in interventions ranging from street-tree programs and rain gardens to urban forests and large blue–green systems. The long-term viability of these interventions, however, is not secured at installation. Their persistence fundamentally depends on routine care, adaptive management, technical knowledge, institutional continuity, and sustained operational expenditure (Fors et al., 2018; Randrup et al., 2017; Roman et al., 2021). In this study, we explicitly define UGI stewardship not merely as routine physical maintenance, but as the continuous, legally and financially embedded institutional process required to sustain ecological functions and socio-cultural values over time. Closely related to this is the concept of place-keeping (Dempsey and Burton, 2012), which we define as the active, long-term socio-political commitment to maintaining the physical and social qualities of a space after the initial place-making (capital investment) is complete. A recurring concern in the literature is the structural temporal separation between visible project delivery and less visible long-term stewardship. Initial capital expenditure (CAPEX) is often politically and administratively easier to authorize than the sustained operational expenditure (OPEX) required for vital monitoring, maintenance, repair, and community engagement. When governance responsibilities and financial resources are misaligned, ecological performance can rapidly decline and social benefits may be unevenly distributed (Anguelovski et al., 2019; Haase et al., 2017; Kronenberg, 2025; Randrup et al., 2017; Roman et al., 2021). While context-dependent, this structural misalignment is associated with increased vulnerabilities in the foundational objectives of global urban greening agendas. While recent reviews, such as Fernandes et al. (2025), have comprehensively cataloged technical maintenance barriers, this study specifically advances the discourse by applying an urban political ecology lens to interrogate the structural drivers behind these persistent barriers. Concurrently, empirical studies continue to document funding constraints, fragmented governance, technical knowledge gaps, and conflicts over naturalized esthetics (Cristiano et al., 2023; Hoyle et al., 2017; Pecina et al., 2021; Wojnowska-Heciak et al., 2025). However, this evidence remains highly dispersed across disciplines and scales. To move beyond isolated case studies, a structured synthesis is urgently needed to map how these multifaceted stewardship challenges co-occur and systematically interact across diverse UGI configurations. Drawing on urban political ecology (UPE), this study conceptualizes the CAPEX–OPEX divide not merely as a budgetary technicality, but as a critical structural driver of socio-ecological outcomes in urban environmental governance (Dempsey and Burton, 2012; Heynen et al., 2006; Swyngedouw and Heynen, 2003). By structuring the empirical evidence across five critical stewardship domains (funding, governance, esthetics, equity, and technology), we aim to untangle the complex realities of UGI post-construction management. Accordingly, this structured evidence synthesis addresses three core research questions (RQs) concerning the distribution and co-occurrence of stewardship challenges: RQ1 (Geographic and Scalar Distribution): How are post-construction stewardship challenges distributed across geographic contexts and spatial scales in the selected corpus? RQ2 (Co-occurring Challenge Domains): Which stewardship challenge domains are most frequently reported together? RQ3 (Design-Stewardship Associations): Which reported design characteristics co-occur with particular stewardship challenge domains? Ultimately, this analysis synthesizes dispersed empirical evidence into a coherent framework, advancing interpretive propositions to guide future comparative research and the sustainable, equitable governance of urban green assets. 2 Conceptual framework UPE emphasizes that urban nature is continuously produced and negotiated through intersecting flows of capital, power, labor, and institutional authority (Heynen et al., 2006). From this theoretical perspective, green infrastructure is not a static spatial object but an ongoing socio-ecological process. Consequently, physical place-making through initial design and construction must be intricately coupled with sustained place-keeping through long-term maintenance, monitoring, social learning, and institutional negotiation (Dempsey and Burton, 2012; Lawrence et al., 2013; Randrup et al., 2017; Svendsen and Campbell, 2008). To operationalize this perspective, we propose the “CAPEX-OPEX divide” to conceptualize the structural mismatch between the highly visible capital investments dedicated to project delivery and the less visible, chronic deficits in resources required for long-term care. Rather than a mere accounting metric, this divide serves as a powerful interpretive lens to decode the systemic vulnerabilities embedded within municipal environmental governance. To systematically unpack this divide, the synthesis organizes reported stewardship challenges into five intersecting analytical dimensions: funding and costs; governance and institutions; esthetics and perception; space and equity; and technology and data. The methodological coding approach utilized a mixed deductive-inductive design: the core dimensions were initially informed by established urban forestry and environmental-governance scholarship, and subsequently refined iteratively against the empirical realities documented in the selected publications. By structuring the evidence into these specific co-occurring challenge domains, the framework captures the multidimensionality of stewardship failures, allowing for a rigorous examination of how structural deficits cluster and compound one another in practice, without prematurely forcing linear causal assumptions onto complex socio-ecological systems. Within this framework, multiple dimensions are frequently coded for a single record, enabling a nuanced mapping of co-occurrence and systemic overlap. This network-oriented perspective acknowledges the inherent complexity of urban environments, where stewardship outcomes are shaped by a matrix of contextual factors, including project maturity, ecological baseline, technical complexity, local policy capacity, and varying study designs. By mapping these structural associations, the conceptual framework moves beyond isolated barrier identification to synthesize three archetypal stewardship configurations. These configurations function as robust interpretive propositions, providing a theoretically grounded architecture to guide future empirical validation, comparative policy analysis, and adaptive management strategies. 3 Materials and methods 3.1 Review design and search strategy This study was designed as a structured evidence synthesis, utilizing a PRISMA-guided selection flow to systematically map and analyze the literature (Page et al., 2021). Web of Science Core Collection and Scopus were searched on 5 March 2026. Our primary Web of Science query was: TS = (“planting design” OR “planting scheme” OR “landscape design” OR “landscape architecture” OR “urban greening” OR “green space*”) AND TS = (“maintenance” OR “survival” OR “establishment” OR “post-planting” OR “post-occupancy” OR “long-term performance” OR “plant health”). The equivalent Scopus query utilized TITLE-ABS-KEY fields with the same two term blocks. The search returned 1,170 Web of Science records and 1,672 Scopus records (2,842 in total). Database filters limited the records to 2015–2025, English, and articles or reviews, leaving 2,292 records. This primary query was specifically structured to capture the critical intersection of the design–maintenance nexus, ensuring a highly targeted corpus for subsequent evidence extraction. 3.2 Eligibility and selection After removal of 816 duplicates, 1,476 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. To ensure reliability, a pilot inter-rater reliability test was conducted. A random sample of 50 records was independently screened by two reviewers, producing high agreement (Cohen’s kappa = 0.85). Following this calibration, two authors independently completed the screening and full-text assessment; disagreements were resolved through discussion and final adjudication by the lead author. Title and abstract screening excluded 880 records, leaving 596 full texts (Figure 1). Figure 1 Conceptual framework for urban green infrastructure stewardship. Rather than employing purposive sampling (which selects cases for information richness), this study utilized a threshold-based selection criterion based on journal quartiles. This step retained publications exclusively from Q1 journals (excluding 343 non-Q1 records) and excluded 52 publications lacking empirical data. This methodological decision was not intended to evaluate individual study quality, but rather to deliberately capture and synthesize the high-impact, mainstream theoretical discourse that currently dominates and shapes international UGI policy and governance frameworks. A further 139 records were excluded during thematic full-text assessment, yielding 62 publications for the final synthesis (Figure 2). The highlighted journal-quartile restriction step indicates a purposive sampling filter utilized to define the selected corpus of high-impact literature. 3.3 Role of artificial intelligence in data extraction During the data extraction phase, a large language model (Gemini 3.1) was utilized to assist in locating specific thematic keywords and synthesizing complex paragraphs within the retrieved literature. In strict accordance with publication guidelines regarding AI usage, this tool did not participate in eligibility screening or qualitative coding. All AI-assisted summaries were manually cross-referenced against the original PDF manuscripts to verify context and authenticity. Final coding decisions, matrix construction, and theoretical interpretations were executed exclusively by the human authors. 3.4 Data extraction and coding framework A codebook-guided qualitative-to-quantitative transformation strategy was applied to extract post-construction stewardship evidence (Braun and Clarke, 2021). We developed a mixed deductive-inductive coding framework comprising five interrelated challenge dimensions (Table 1): funding and costs, governance and institutions, esthetics and perception, space and equity, and technology and data. Revised operational definitions for the five stewardship-challenge dimensions. The primary analytical unit in this evidence matrix was defined as a single peer-reviewed publication (n = 62). Each publication was systematically coded for geographic context, spatial scale, design characteristics, and the binary presence (1) or absence (0) of each challenge dimension to rigorously quantify co-occurrence patterns. For geographic classification, countries were categorized into the “Global North” or “Global South” strictly according to the World Bank’s income group classifications, where high-income economies are designated as the Global North, and low- to middle-income economies constitute the Global South. While this binary coding strategy (present = 1/absent = 0) effectively captures macro-level patterns across the literature, it inherently introduces a loss of analytical precision. By coding at the publication level, this approach may mask important intra-article variations, such as contrasting successful and unsuccessful cases within the same study. To address the potential conceptual overlap between “Funding and Costs” and “Governance and Institutions,” strict binary coding boundaries were established. The “Funding” dimension was triggered only when records explicitly reported absolute financial deficits (e.g., lack of maintenance budgets, capital shortages). Conversely, “Governance” was coded specifically for structural and administrative failures (e.g., policy gaps, fragmented jurisdictions, lack of clear stewardship responsibilities). In instances where a primary source explicitly described a funding shortage as a direct manifestation of governance failure, both dimensions were coded. This approach was designed to accurately capture the intersectional reality of stewardship degradation rather than artificially suppressing valid co-occurrence frequencies. To systematically categorize UGI design characteristics, we applied the following operational definitions during the coding process: (1) Capital-Intensive/conventional: highly manicured, traditional engineered landscapes requiring substantial upfront investment and continuous intensive maintenance; (2) Naturalized/wild: low-intervention, ecologically driven designs emphasizing spontaneous vegetation, succession, and biodiversity; (3) Tech-Heavy/blue-green: specialized infrastructure focusing on engineered hydrological/ecological functions (e.g., Sponge City interventions, stormwater management); and (4) General/distributed UGI: ubiquitous, standard small-scale greening without extreme technical or capital requirements (e.g., street trees, typical pocket parks). In cases where a UGI intervention exhibited multiple characteristics, category membership was assigned based on the primary focal characteristic explicitly emphasized as the core subject or primary intervention in the original source article, ensuring mutually exclusive coding for the chord diagram visualization. To ensure coding reliability, two authors independently coded a random sub-sample of the dataset. Inter-coder reliability was calculated using Krippendorff’s alpha (nominal variant), achieving robust agreement (α = 0.88). Disagreements were resolved through consensus discussions. The complete classification codebook (Supplementary Appendix B), the 62-row evidence matrix (Supplementary Appendix C), and the qualitative audit trail with supporting quotations (Supplementary Appendix D) are provided in Supplementary Information. 3.5 Study quality and methodological scope In alignment with the foundational objectives of a structured evidence synthesis, this study aims to systematically map the thematic landscape of stewardship challenges rather than to evaluate quantitative intervention efficacy. Consequently, a formal risk-of-bias appraisal was deliberately omitted for the 62-publication corpus. The analytical focus remains strictly on capturing descriptive co-occurrence patterns and generating interpretive frameworks, rather than grading evidence certainty or establishing weighted causal relationships. 3.6 Descriptive synthesis and visualization Data processing and visualization were conducted using a combination of open-source analytical platforms to robustly map thematic intersections. Co-occurrences among the five stewardship-challenge dimensions were quantified and visualized using an UpSet plot (Lex et al., 2014). Additionally, an alluvial diagram and a chord diagram (Mauri et al., 2017) were generated via RAWGraphs 2.0 to delineate cross-sectional thematic distributions within the literature. To ensure visual clarity and highlight the most prominent structural associations in the chord diagram, a relational threshold was applied, retaining only edge connections with n ≥ 5. These visual analytics are purposefully employed to elucidate structural patterns and combinatorial challenge profiles, providing a robust heuristic foundation for the proposed stewardship framework. 4 Results 4.1 Descriptive and bibliometric overview The final selected corpus comprised 62 publications. Temporally, the literature exhibits a distinct upward trajectory, with 51 records (82%) published from 2019 onward (Figure 3A). This acceleration underscores a rapidly growing academic recognition of post-construction stewardship as a critical frontier in urban environmental research. (b) Disciplinary distribution, highlighting the prominent academic venues currently leading the discourse on UGI governance. The publications were concentrated in high-impact journals related to urban forestry, landscape architecture, planning, and environmental management, with Urban Forestry and Urban Greening accounting for 41.9% of the corpus (Figure 3B). This concentration clearly delineates the primary academic venues currently driving the discourse on UGI maintenance and governance. Beyond the specific prominent pathways visualized in the subsequent sections, the complete corpus also includes broader records documenting localized operational barriers and context-specific greening outcomes across diverse administrative settings (Chabba et al., 2022; Chishaleshale et al., 2015; Dawson et al., 2025; Dinkins et al., 2025; Froehlich and Ciach, 2020; Lefebvre et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Riedman et al., 2024; Shackleton and Njwaxu, 2021; Sunding and Dempsey, 2025). 4.2 Geographic and scalar patterns (RQ1) Geographically, our sample exhibits a pronounced bias, with nearly 68% of the analyzed literature (n = 42) focused on cases in the Global North—predominantly Europe (n = 25), North America (n = 11), and Oceania (n = 6). This geographic concentration reflects the established centers of UGI research output and highlights the persistent global disparity in documented urban greening literature, leaving innovative grassroots governance models from the Global South (e.g., South America and Africa, representing merely 8% of the corpus) structurally underrepresented (Fongar et al., 2019; Rigolon et al., 2024). The mapped flow trajectories in the alluvial diagram (Figure 4) reveal distinct structural associations across spatial scales and design typologies. At macro and meso scales, records frequently linked General/Distributed UGI with governance, funding, and equity dimensions (Randrup et al., 2021; Roy et al., 2017; Sen et al., 2021). Specifically, the decentralized nature of distributed UGI (e.g., street trees, rain gardens) often shifts maintenance burdens onto local residents; in under-resourced neighborhoods, this dynamic directly exacerbates spatial equity challenges. Conversely, specific front-end design characteristics exhibited highly targeted associative pathways. Records analyzing Naturalized/Wild Ecologies critically intersected with Esthetics and Perception challenges (Hoyle et al., 2019). Furthermore, studies focusing on Tech-Heavy/Blue-Green and Capital-Intensive interventions demonstrated prominent associations with Funding and Costs, as well as Technology and Data deficits (Randrup et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2023; Williams et al., 2019). Governance and Institutions, alongside Funding and Costs, possessed the largest individual set sizes and appeared in almost all major intersections (Basit et al., 2022; Cristiano et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2016; Roberts et al., 2022). Their overwhelming frequency underscores that institutional and financial capacities form the structural core of UGI maintenance barriers. The most frequent co-occurrence was the triple-intersection of Governance + Funding + Technology/Data (n = 14). Within the synthesized literature, studies in this intersection frequently reported how bureaucratic fragmentation and funding deficits overlapped with profound informational gaps. This highlights critical scenarios where municipalities lacked both the capital and the requisite monitoring technologies to sustain greening efforts (Ko et al., 2015; Li et al., 2025; Lubaina et al., 2025; Ugolini et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2019; Yoshino and Asahiro, 2025). Furthermore, the quadruple-intersection of Governance + Funding + Technology + Space and Equity occurred in seven publications. Studies clustered in this intersection frequently documented how overlapping administrative, financial, and technical deficits systematically co-occurred with environmental injustice in marginalized communities (Fisher et al., 2021; Grabowski et al., 2023; Jimenez-Caldera et al., 2024; Nobles and Moore, 2024). Recurrent thematic patterns in these specific records included the diversion of maintenance funds from older, peripheral neighborhoods to new affluent developments (Arhuire-Ossio et al., 2023; Mekala et al., 2015; Randrup et al., 2017), as well as the shifting of stewardship responsibilities to resource-poor residents without adequate financial or technical support (Kahane et al., 2025; Pike et al., 2025; Wikantiyoso and Suhartono, 2018). 4.4 Descriptive associations between design characteristics and stewardship challenges (RQ3) While descriptive frequencies highlight where challenges occur, the chord diagram (Figure 6) maps the structural co-occurrences between front-end design characteristics and stewardship challenge domains. It serves as a powerful visual index of the systemic vulnerabilities embedded within different UGI typologies. To filter out idiosyncratic single-case observations (noise) and highlight the most prevalent, statistically robust structural interactions in the dataset, a relational threshold of n ≥ 5 was applied to this visualization. Within the corpus, Capital-Intensive or conventional projects were most frequently linked with Governance and Institutions (n = 21) and Funding and Costs (n = 18). Studies in this pathway frequently documented how high-cost greening developments outpaced municipal operational capacities, leading to profound institutional friction and funding deficits (Brocki et al., 2025; Ignell et al., 2025), as illustrated by specific case reports on sky garden maintenance (Law et al., 2021) and rigid lawn-care regimes (Yang et al., 2019). Conversely, Naturalized or wild designs critically intersected with Esthetics and Perception challenges (n = 16). These records consistently highlighted socio-cultural resistance to spontaneous urban ecologies when designs lacked explicit cues to care (Dobbie and Farrelly, 2022; Hu and Sun, 2025; Hwang et al., 2019; Lefebvre et al., 2022; Ramer and Nelson, 2020; Wojnowska-Heciak et al., 2025). These associative patterns vividly underscore the tension between operational capacity requirements and public esthetic expectations. Furthermore, Tech-Heavy or blue-green projects were prominently linked with Technology/Data (n = 15) and Funding/Costs (n = 12), recurrently reported in the context of specialized infrastructure vulnerable to depleted post-construction monitoring budgets (Li et al., 2025; Pecina et al., 2021; Sule et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Williams et al., 2019). Crucially, General or distributed UGI was strongly associated with Space/Equity (n = 14) and Governance/Institutional (n = 11) challenges. Thematic patterns within these specific records frequently highlighted the offloading of stewardship responsibilities onto marginalized communities without adequate institutional or financial support (Kahane et al., 2025; Pike et al., 2025; Trentanovi et al., 2021). Taken together, the geographic distributions, domain intersections, and design-domain co-occurrences expose clear structural fault lines in UGI governance. These empirical associations provide a robust foundation for the three interpretive stewardship propositions discussed in the subsequent section. 5 Discussion The synthesis reveals that post-construction stewardship is fundamentally understood within the high-impact literature not merely as a horticultural task, but as a highly complex, multidimensional governance challenge. Within the selected corpus, funding deficits, institutional fragmentation, and equity issues frequently co-occurred. This structural entanglement strongly aligns with the theoretical proposition that project delivery (CAPEX) and long-term care (OPEX) are governed through conflicting budgets, time horizons, and accountability arrangements (Bechtel et al., 2025; Breger et al., 2019; Dempsey and Burton, 2012; Fongar et al., 2019; Fors et al., 2018; Marcos Martinez et al., 2025; Randrup et al., 2017, 2021). At the core of our interpretation is the CAPEX-OPEX divide, which functions as a critical structural catalyst for UGI degradation. The empirical co-occurrences extracted from the literature demonstrate that stewardship vulnerabilities are deeply path-dependent, where front-end design categories often proxy for underlying project scale, technical complexity, and the specific political contexts shaping urban governance. Synthesizing these structural associations, we advance three core interpretive propositions (Table 2) that conceptualize the distinct trajectories of stewardship degradation, offering a robust heuristic framework for future empirical testing and policy intervention. Before detailing the specific stewardship challenges, it is critical to distinguish between the descriptive co-occurrence patterns observed in our sample and explanatory mechanistic inferences. Co-occurrence data (e.g., the frequent intersection of governance, funding, and technology) indicate that these dimensions are highly associated in the current literature. However, they do not inherently prove linear causal chains. Building upon these descriptive associations and our theoretical framework, we propose the following three propositions. These propositions should be interpreted as heuristic explanations for UGI stewardship degradation, rather than definitive causal pathways proven by statistical frequencies. The foundational urban greening design typology draws upon Konijnendijk (2023). 5.1 Proposition 1: bureaucratic starvation and technocratic blindness (the CAPEX-OPEX divide) The frequent co-occurrence of governance, funding, and technology challenges powerfully underscores the proposition of bureaucratic starvation and technocratic blindness. This structural configuration demonstrates that unclear institutional responsibilities, depleted operational budgets, and deficient monitoring capacities systematically converge, particularly within large-scale or technically complex UGI programs. Within the synthesized literature, this pattern is frequently theorized as a profound structural discrepancy between CAPEX and OPEX, deeply tied to the logics of neoliberal urbanism (Heynen et al., 2006; Marcos Martinez et al., 2025). Political expediency consistently favors the high-visibility moments associated with rapid CAPEX deployment, while the OPEX required for long-term ecological monitoring remains highly vulnerable to municipal austerity (Mekala et al., 2015). Specific empirical cases within the corpus vividly illustrate this structural gap: from US tree planting initiatives allocating merely 5% of total budgets to watering (Nguyen et al., 2017), to systematic budget cuts across UK parks (Hoyle et al., 2019), and chronic institutionalization gaps that render nationwide planting programs structurally precarious (Eisenman et al., 2021). Collectively, these cases across diverse geopolitical settings highlight the urgent necessity to mandate lifecycle costing and secure explicit post-construction administrative accountability before capital deployment. 5.2 Proposition 2: the esthetic backlash (esthetic conflict) Naturalized and spontaneous designs were frequently documented alongside intense public concerns regarding neatness, fear of pests, and visible cues to care (Babington et al., 2023; Dobbie and Farrelly, 2022; Hoyle et al., 2017, 2019; Hu and Sun, 2025; Hwang et al., 2019; Lefebvre et al., 2022; Nassauer, 1995; Pröbstl-Haider et al., 2024; Ramer and Nelson, 2020; Wojnowska-Heciak et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2019). Crucially, this esthetic backlash is frequently linked to complex socio-cultural contexts rather than solely public ecological knowledge; rather, landscape preferences are profoundly and structurally shaped by cultural expectations, site use, perceived safety, and deeply ingrained institutional maintenance norms. The synthesized records reveal that this conflict is profoundly institutionalized. For example, reports prominently document how local governance actively yields to administrative anxieties, such as French public administrators projecting their fear of appearing “poorly managed” onto pesticide-free spaces (Lefebvre et al., 2022, 2024), or punitive operational regimes in Xi’an, China, where the mere presence of spontaneous vegetation in lawns triggers wage deductions for frontline maintenance workers (Yang et al., 2019). These narratives forcefully demonstrate that without participatory adaptation, clear communication, and recognizable “cues to care,” top-down ecological designs remain highly susceptible to societal rejection, administrative suppression, and premature ecological regression. 5.3 Proposition 3: systemic marginalization (unequal stewardship burdens) The robust co-occurrence of governance, funding, and equity challenges directly supports the proposition of systemic marginalization. Evidence from the synthesized corpus highlights that institutional disinvestment does not occur randomly; rather, it structurally disproportionately affects marginalized communities (Fisher et al., 2021; Nobles and Moore, 2024; Pike et al., 2025; Swyngedouw and Heynen, 2003). This systemic marginalization manifests in concrete spatial injustices, prominently including: the geographic accumulation of park fees in wealthy Los Angeles districts that systematically restricts capital in disadvantaged areas (Rigolon et al., 2024), the outsourcing of essential tree watering to unpaid residents in semi-arid zones (McNamara et al., 2022), the erection of physical fences excluding marginalized users in Bengaluru (Sen et al., 2021), and the structural exclusion of indigenous communities from collaborative governance in New Zealand (Carlin et al., 2025). These compelling cases emphasize a critical caveat in contemporary UGI governance: while community stewardship is frequently championed, devolving maintenance labor without legal recognition, financial compensation, or technical support risks actively accelerating green gentrification and exclusionary spatial practices (Wolch et al., 2014; Xiao and Cen, 2025). 5.4 Contextual modifiers and future research trajectories While this synthesis utilizes a UPE lens to critically unpack structural inequalities, the trajectory of UGI performance is inevitably mediated by a complex interplay of localized operational mechanisms and boundary conditions. Beyond macro-level capital accumulation or municipal austerity, site-specific factors—such as ecological uncertainty, extreme climate events, species-site mismatches, technical maturity, procurement design, staff turnover, professional knowledge, project age, and reasonable public safety concerns—also critically dictate long-term viability. While our synthesis predominantly maps degradation pathways, it is essential to contrast these trajectories with emerging successful stewardship models. Although less frequently centered in the analyzed literature, mechanisms such as endowed maintenance funds (where OPEX is capitalized upfront), community land trusts, and legally binding public-private co-management agreements demonstrate that the CAPEX-OPEX divide can be bridged. These successful models interrupt the degradation pathways by securing institutional continuity and local empowerment. Furthermore, boundary conditions vary significantly based on localized governance systems, land tenure arrangements, neighborhood socio-demographics, and the explicit distribution of responsibilities over a project’s lifecycle. Rather than viewing these as mere confounding variables, future longitudinal research must systematically compare these contextual modifiers across successful, failed, and mixed-outcome cases to rigorously test and refine the proposed structural configurations. 5.5 Implications for governance and practice: toward collaborative stewardship capacity A primary practical implication derived from this synthesis is that UGI appraisal must legally codify lifecycle costs, organizational responsibility, and equity before capital construction is approved (Bechtel et al., 2025; Kazemi et al., 2022; Nawrath et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2019). These mandatory measures must be formally incorporated into municipal procurement, interdepartmental agreements, and asset-management systems. Furthermore, while community stewardship is frequently championed to broaden participation, it must not serve as a cost-free substitute for public responsibility; viable collaborative models require appropriate ongoing finance, legal recognition, and technical support. To conceptualize this paradigm shift, we advance the term collaborative stewardship capacity as a normative policy framework. We define it as the durable legal, financial, technical, and participatory capacity of responsible public agencies and communities to care for local ecological assets, actively shielding them from the volatility of short-term project funding cycles. While related to broader concepts like polycentric governance or urban commons, which often describe macroscopic administrative networks or shared property regimes, our proposed term specifically emphasizes the localized, pragmatic pooling of legal, financial, and technical resources necessary for sustained UGI maintenance. Unlike community stewardship alone, this concept strictly necessitates enforceable public obligations and secured long-term operational funding; unlike simple decentralization, it explicitly rejects the assumption that maintenance burdens should simply be offloaded from local governments. To operationalize “collaborative stewardship capacity,” municipalities must implement specific, measurable policy mechanisms. These include (1) enacting zoning ordinances that require fully capitalized OPEX trust funds before building permits are issued for UGI, (2) establishing legally binding community co-management charters, and (3) incorporating long-term ecological performance metrics into mandatory annual municipal budget audits. This normative framework provides a robust foundation for future comparative testing against existing theories of polycentric governance, urban commons, and public investment equity (Beck et al., 2024; Buijs et al., 2016; Egerer et al., 2024; Focacci et al., 2025), offering a critical theoretical pathway to prevent climate adaptation initiatives from transitioning into exclusionary green ruins. 5.6 Limitations of the synthesis While this structured synthesis provides critical exploratory insights and theoretical propositions, its methodological boundaries must be explicitly acknowledged to contextualize the findings. First, the systematic search was confined to English-language literature within Web of Science and Scopus, and omitted several broader asset-management search terms to maintain focus on the design-stewardship nexus. Consequently, gray literature, municipal reports, and non-English case studies are structurally underrepresented. Second, our threshold-based selection of Q1 journals represents a significant methodological boundary condition. We acknowledge that journal impact factor is not a valid proxy for individual study quality. More critically, this filter systematically excludes a substantial body of rigorous research published in non-Q1 regional journals. As a result, our sample exhibits a distinct geographic bias, potentially overlooking critical stewardship challenges and innovative grassroots governance models in the Global South. While this approach successfully captures the mainstream theoretical discourse driving dominant policy agendas, the resulting three propositions should be interpreted as heuristic explanations of mainstream academic consensus rather than universally generalizable causal pathways. Third, the primary analytical unit was the publication. Because multi-case publications were compressed into binary codes, the analysis simplifies within-study variation and context-dependent narratives. Furthermore, because the review specifically targeted literature reporting stewardship challenges without including a systematic comparison group of successful UGI projects, the synthesized intersection frequencies illustrate associative patterns but cannot be utilized to estimate global failure prevalence or general risk rates. This limitation is further compounded by a prevailing publication bias toward deficit narratives within the UGI governance literature. Academic inquiry frequently gravitates toward investigating governance tensions, spatial inequalities, and ecological failures, resulting in a disproportionate lack of comparative success cases in our sample. Consequently, our three propositions predominantly describe degradation pathways. Future empirical research must prioritize analyzing successful, resilient stewardship configurations to provide a more balanced comparative framework. Finally, consistent with the objectives of mapping macro-thematic patterns, a formal study-level risk-of-bias or evidence certainty appraisal was not conducted. While natural language processing (Gemini 3.1) was strictly utilized under human supervision as an assistive retrieval tool, qualitative interpretation remains central to the thematic coding. Consequently, these findings function as a hypothesis-generating structural framework, offering interpretive propositions for future causal identification rather than validating deterministic collapse pathways. 6 Conclusion In the selected 62-publication corpus, post-construction stewardship challenges were commonly reported in combination rather than as isolated operational missteps. Governance and funding challenges frequently co-occurred with deficits in monitoring and technical capacity; naturalized designs were often discussed in relation to esthetic expectations; and records recurrently connected stewardship arrangements with unequal access or labor burdens. These structural patterns expose the profound institutional tension between initial project delivery and long-term care, providing a robust empirical foundation for future comparative research. Moving beyond symptom-based treatments, this structured synthesis advances the discourse on UGI maintenance toward a systemic environmental governance perspective through three primary contributions: Theoretical Contribution: We conceptualize the structural CAPEX-OPEX Divide and advance three archetypal propositions of stewardship degradation: Bureaucratic Starvation and Technocratic Blindness, The Esthetic Backlash, and Systemic Marginalization. This framework critically integrates urban political ecology with ground-level landscape management, translating abstract governance dynamics into testable, structurally grounded configurations. Methodological Contribution: We demonstrate the utility of a qualitative-to-quantitative transformation strategy, repurposing visual analytics (e.g., UpSet and Chord diagrams) to transparently and rigorously map the multi-dimensional entanglement of stewardship deficits across complex global literature. Practical Contribution: We introduce the normative paradigm of collaborative stewardship capacity, urging municipal authorities to bridge structural financial divides through mandatory life-cycle impact assessments, explicit operational accountability, and polycentric co-stewardship models that legally and equitably resource civic labor. Ultimately, the concept of “collaborative stewardship capacity” proposed in this study serves as a direct institutional antidote to the CAPEX-OPEX divide. By securing legally mandated, sustained operational funding mechanisms (OPEX) prior to capital investment (CAPEX), collaborative stewardship capacity transforms maintenance from an optional municipal burden into an embedded infrastructural right. Sustainable urban greening is not achieved on the day of planting; it is a continuous socio-ecological contract. While our interpretive propositions invite rigorous future testing across diverse governance systems—particularly within the rapid urbanization dynamics of the Global South—the synthesized evidence unequivocally demonstrates that high-cost, top-down greening initiatives frequently outpace municipal operational capacities. Resilient green infrastructure fundamentally requires well-funded, data-informed, and socio-culturally inclusive governance. Failing to secure the legal, financial, and participatory mechanisms necessary to sustain these ecological assets risks transforming contemporary climate adaptation projects into exclusionary green ruins.
