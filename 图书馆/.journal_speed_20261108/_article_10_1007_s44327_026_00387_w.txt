Under the framework of green and low-carbon development, understanding the multiscale spatiotemporal coupling between urban expansion and carbon storage is vital for informing sustainable urbanization strategies. Taking the Chengdu-Chongqing urban agglomeration in western China as a representative case of rapid urbanization, this study integrates multitemporal remote-sensing data from 2000 to 2020 to investigate the dynamic patterns and interactions between urban growth and carbon storage. Carbon storage was quantified by integrating the InVEST model with the biomass expansion factor (BEF) method, which replaces the uniform class-mean carbon densities used in conventional InVEST applications with stock-volume-derived, province-specific forest biomass densities. The results indicate that: (1) built-up area increased from 3,140.3 km² in 2000 to 15,550.5 km² in 2020. After a slight slowdown during 2005–2010, absolute expansion accelerated markedly and reached its highest rate during 2015–2020, while the spatial pattern remained dominated by Chengdu and Chongqing; (2) regional carbon storage showed an overall increasing trend with significant spatial heterogeneity, with high-value zones primarily located in the northeastern and southwestern parts of the study area; (3) the coupling coordination degree (CCD) between urban expansion and carbon storage exhibited scale-dependent trajectories: the city-scale mean increased slightly from 0.368 in 2000 to 0.394 in 2020, whereas the county-scale mean decreased continuously from 0.557 to 0.490, with high-CCD counties concentrated in the forest-dominated periphery and low-CCD counties in the urbanized cores. These results provide an empirical basis for balancing urban development and ecological conservation in rapidly urbanizing regions. Urban Policy Urban Ecology Geography Urban Economics Development Geography Urban Sociology 1 Introduction With the accelerating pace of global urbanization, urban expansion has become one of the core driving forces of land-use change [ 19 ]. It not only reshapes regional spatial patterns but also profoundly affects key ecosystem services such as carbon storage [ 54 ]. The expansion of urban land is often accompanied by the conversion of vast areas of natural ecological land into built-up areas, resulting in weakened carbon sequestration capacity and increased carbon emissions [ 55 ]. This poses severe challenges to regional ecological security and the achievement of global climate targets [ 49 ]. Under the guidance of international and national strategies such as the United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development and China’s dual-carbon goals of carbon peaking and carbon neutrality, there is an urgent need to deepen scientific understanding of the relationship between urban expansion and carbon storage, and to promote the coordinated development of urbanization and ecological conservation. Urban agglomerations, as key carriers of national strategic development and population concentration [ 50 ], have land-use structures and spatial layouts that are closely related to regional carbon emissions and carbon storage capacities [ 14 ]. Clarifying the spatiotemporal relationship between urban expansion and carbon storage under the “Dual Carbon” strategy is of great significance for formulating spatially targeted ecological protection and restoration policies and for enhancing ecosystem carbon-sequestration potential [ 17 ]. Driven by rapid urbanization, population growth, and socio-economic development, land use/land cover (LULC) has undergone dramatic changes, becoming a major driver of changes in ecosystem service functions [ 36 , 43 ]. Among these services, carbon storage has attracted increasing attention in the academic community as a critical component of ecosystem functioning. Changes in land use can significantly impact ecosystem carbon cycling by altering vegetation composition, soil properties, and landscape patterns [ 41 ]. Researchers have extensively studied the impact of land-use change on carbon storage [ 57 ], the spatiotemporal variation of carbon stocks [ 26 , 46 ], the driving factors [ 1 , 32 , 58 ], ecosystem service assessments [ 37 ], carbon sink valuation [ 35 , 38 ], and carbon sequestration potential [ 28 , 30 , 31 , 47 ]. With advances in ground-based observation, remote sensing, and GIS, the scope of research has continuously expanded to encompass forests, grasslands, soils, and other ecosystems [ 2 ], using spatial units such as watersheds, cities, and regions to assess carbon storage and ecosystem services [ 5 , 12 ]. Among the existing methods, the Integrated Valuation of Ecosystem Services and Trade-offs (InVEST) model is widely used to estimate carbon storage by coupling land-use types with carbon pool coefficients [ 33 ]. As a mainstream and relatively mature tool for quantifying ecosystem services, the InVEST model is central to simulating ecosystem service changes under different land-use scenarios. However, the model assumes uniform carbon density for the same land-use type across different regions, which may not reflect spatial heterogeneity in carbon storage and may introduce uncertainty in regional carbon stock estimations [ 39 ]. At the same time, research on the spatial and temporal patterns of urban expansion has evolved across multiple scales, including national [ 20 ], urban agglomeration [ 21 ], watershed [ 42 ], provincial [ 4 ], and municipal levels. Many of these studies regard study areas as isolated spatial units and overlook spatial interconnections across regions. Multiscale comparative research on urban expansion and carbon storage responses remains relatively limited [ 3 , 16 ]. The Chengdu-Chongqing Urban Agglomeration (CCUA) is a critical ecological barrier in the upper Yangtze River Basin, vital for regional environmental security. However, rapid economic growth, urban sprawl, cropland loss, and soil erosion have imposed mounting ecological pressures, and degraded terrestrial carbon sinks, threatening long-term sustainability. Using the CCUA as a case study, we investigate the spatiotemporal dynamics and coupling coordination between urban expansion and carbon storage from 2000 to 2020. Urban built-up areas are extracted via integrated density and composite threshold methods, with expansion measured by indices. Carbon storage is estimated using the InVEST model combined with the biomass expansion factor (BEF) method. The entropy weight and coupling coordination degree models are then applied to evaluate their coordination. While previous studies have examined urban expansion or carbon storage separately in the CCUA, systematic multiscale characterization of their coupling coordination remains limited [ 10 ]. Moreover, existing carbon storage estimates often overlook spatial heterogeneity in carbon densities. This study therefore: (1) integrates InVEST with a localized BEF method for spatially differentiated carbon-storage estimation; (2) quantifies the temporal variation and spatial heterogeneity of a descriptive coupling-coordination index; and (3) provides a transparent multiscale analytical framework for rapidly urbanizing agglomerations. 2 Study area and data 2.1 Overview of the study area CCUA lies at the intersection of the “Belt and Road” initiative and the Yangtze River Economic Belt, and connects eastern and western China. The study used a fixed boundary comprising 142 county-level units. This boundary follows the Outline of the Construction Plan for the Chengdu–Chongqing Twin-City Economic Circle, with Kaizhou District and Yunyang County fully included to maintain complete county boundaries. An entropy-weighted urban-expansion index and standardized total carbon storage were then used to calculate coupling and coordination degrees at the city and county scales. 3.1 Extraction of urban built-up areas (1) Kernel density. Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) is a sophisticated method for estimating the density of point and line elements, independent of grid size and specific locations [ 45 ]. In this study, the centers of GAIA impervious-surface cells were used as input point features, and the impervious-surface value was used as the feature weight. For each location, features within the specified search radius contributed distance-decayed weights to the density estimate. The calculation formula is as follows: $$\:{P}_{i}=\frac{1}{n\pi\:{R}^{2}}{\sum\:}_{j=1}^{n}{K}_{j}{\left(1\:-\frac{{D}_{ij}^{2}}{{R}^{2}}\right)}^{2}$$ (1) \(\:{P}_{i}\) is the kernel-density estimate at location i, \(\:{K}_{j}\:\) is the weight of object j, \(\:{D}_{ij}^{2}\) is the distance between location i and object j; \(\:R\) is the bandwidth ( \(\:{D}_{ij}<R\) ); n is the number of research objects within the bandwidth. Recognizing that the bandwidth selection in KDE markedly affects the outcome, as informed by the literature [ 34 ], bandwidths of 500 m, 1000 m, and 1500 m were evaluated. Upon comparison, a 1500 m bandwidth led to blurred details, whereas a 500 m bandwidth resulted in excessively fragmented patterns. Consequently, a 1000 m bandwidth with a pixel resolution of 30 m was chosen for the analysis. (2) Geometric average method. The fusion of multi-source data frequently uses the geometric mean method, which effectively reduces the impact of extreme values, retains the integrity of original image data, minimizes nightlight data overflow and background noise, and improves the accuracy of extraction. Given that impervious surface data correlates positively with nightlight and negatively with NDVI, this study applies the geometric mean method to compute the comprehensive density index, as shown by the formula: $$\:DH=\sqrt[3]{ISA\times\:NI\times\:\left(1-NDVI\right)}$$ (2) Where: DH represents the comprehensive density value; ISA is the impervious surface core density value; NI is the nightlight value; NDVI is the vegetation index value. All three components were normalized using the min–max method. Impervious-surface density and nighttime-light intensity were treated as positive components, whereas NDVI was inversely transformed before geometric-mean integration. 3.2 Urban expansion measurement The measurement of urban expansion should distinguish absolute spatial growth from relative change. Following previous studies [ 40 , 52 ], this study uses expansion speed, expansion intensity and expansion amplitude to characterize built-up-area change in the CCUA during the study period. The formulas are as follows: $$\:M=\frac{{S}_{b}-{S}_{a}}{{\Delta\:}t}$$ (3) $$\:E=\frac{{S}_{b}-{S}_{a}}{U\times\:{\Delta\:}t}\times\:100$$ (4) $$\:UEI=\frac{{S}_{b}-{S}_{a}}{{S}_{a}}\times\:100$$ (5) In the formulas, M denotes the expansion speed, defined as the average annual increase in built-up area. E represents the expansion intensity, reflecting the annual proportion of newly expanded built-up area relative to the total land area of the corresponding city. UEI denotes the expansion amplitude, capturing the relative growth of built-up area during the study period. \(\:{S}_{a}\) and \(\:{S}_{b}\) are the built-up areas at the beginning and end of the study period, respectively. \(\:{\Delta\:}t\) denotes the time span (years), U represents the total land area of the corresponding city, county, or region. 3.3 Carbon storage estimation (1) Carbon storage calculation using the InVEST model. The Carbon Storage and Sequestration module in the InVEST model calculates carbon storage for different land types based on the carbon density values of land-use types in the study area [ 53 ]. The input carbon density values in the model are influenced by regional annual precipitation [ 44 ] and average annual temperature. The carbon density values for the study area are adjusted according to relevant studies [ 29 ]. From this source, we derived soil organic carbon densities for each land-use type, which were then used as fixed coefficients in the InVEST model, following standard practice. The resulting coefficients (Table 2 ) were held constant across 2000, 2010, and 2020 so that temporal changes reflected land-use change rather than time-varying coefficients. County-specific inventory coefficients were unavailable for cropland and grassland; therefore, climate-adjusted regional class means were used for these classes. The formula for carbon storage is as follows: $$\:{C}_{total\left(i\right)}\equiv\:{C}_{above\left(i\right)}+{C}_{below\left(i\right)}+{C}_{soil\left(i\right)}+{C}_{dead\left(i\right)}$$ (6) Where: i represents the i-th land-use class; \(\:{C}_{total\left(i\right)}\:\) represents the total carbon sequestration of class i; \(\:{C}_{above\left(i\right)}\) represents the aboveground biological carbon; \(\:\:{C}_{below\left(i\right)}\:\) represents the belowground biological carbon; \(\:{C}_{soil\left(i\right)}\) represents the soil organic carbon; \(\:{C}_{dead\left(i\right)}\) represents the dead organic carbon. Table 2 Carbon-density coefficients used in the InVEST model (t C ha⁻¹) Full size table Construction land was assigned nonzero belowground and soil carbon densities because the 30-m construction-land class contains residual soil and vegetated fractions within mixed pixels; these coefficients represent class-mean land carbon pools rather than carbon stored in buildings. The belowground and soil coefficients assigned to construction land are consistent with reported values for urban soils. Ding et al., [ 6 ] measured a mean soil organic carbon density of 62.1 ± 39.0 t C ha⁻¹ (0–100 cm) beneath impervious surfaces across 43 Chinese cities, and Lindén et al., [ 27 ] reported 104 t C ha⁻¹ in soil (0–90 cm) and 22–28 t C ha⁻¹ in trees for constructed urban parks. The combined belowground and soil coefficient adopted here (163.782 t C ha⁻¹) therefore represents mixed 30-m pixels that include parks, roadside greenery, and other vegetated fractions, and remains below the corresponding totals for cropland (190.541 t C ha⁻¹) and forest land (241.899 t C ha⁻¹). Construction land accounts for only 0.78–1.96% of total carbon storage (Table 6 ). (2) Biomass expansion factor method. Existing studies often leverage the InVEST model for estimating regional carbon stocks, owing to its modest data requirements and ease of use. However, the model tends to overlook spatial variation in carbon density within the same land-use type across large regions. Therefore, this study adopts the biomass expansion factor method, following the approach of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) [ 8 ], to evaluate forest land carbon stock changes. This approach entails calculating biomass and then determining carbon storage by multiplying it by the carbon content. The formula applied is as follows: $$\:{C}_{\text{forest}}=V\times\:\overline{BEF}\times\:\overline{D}\times\:\left(1+\overline{R}\right)\times\:\overline{CF}$$ (7) Where: \(\:{C}_{\text{forest}}\) represents the carbon storage of forest land; V represents the forest stock in the study area (m 3 ); \(\:\overline{BEF}\) represents the average biomass expansion factor; \(\:\overline{D}\) represents the average basic density of wood (t/m 3 ); \(\:\overline{R}\) represents the root to stem ratio of average tree species; \(\:\overline{CF}\) represents the carbon content of average tree species. Forest-stock volumes for Sichuan were 1.386, 1.650, and 1.916 × 10⁹ m³ in 2000, 2010, and 2020, respectively; the corresponding Chongqing values were 0.06261, 0.12121, and 0.241 × 10⁹ m³. The 2000 and 2010 values were obtained from the Sichuan and Chongqing statistical yearbooks, and the 2020 values from the Sichuan Forestry and Grassland Administration and Chongqing Forestry Administration. Province-specific mean forest biomass-carbon densities were derived from stock volume and forest area, assigned to forest pixels, and summarized by city and county using zonal statistics. The two province-level parameter sets were applied to their respective subregions: the Sichuan set to the Sichuan portion and the Chongqing set to the Chongqing portion. BEF-derived forest biomass carbon replaced the InVEST above- and belowground biomass estimates for forest land; forest soil and dead-organic-matter pools were retained from InVEST, while all four InVEST pools were retained for nonforest land. Thus, BEF and InVEST biomass estimates were not superimposed [ 22 , 51 ]. The parameters are listed in Table 3 . The composite conversion factors of the two sets, BEF × D × (1 + R) × CF, are 0.490 for the Sichuan set and 0.530 for the Chongqing set; exchanging the two sets would change the estimated forest biomass carbon by about + 8.1% or − 7.5%, which is smaller than the ± 30% range examined in Sect. 4.4 (Tables 8 and 9 ). The dominant tree species of the basin-hill and peripheral-mountain zones are largely shared across the study area, and the forest stock-volume statistics underlying the parameterization are reported at the provincial level; comparably consistent zone-specific statistics are not available. Refining the parameterization by vegetation zone is therefore a priority for future work. The urban-expansion subsystem comprised 15 positive indicators describing urban construction, expansion, population, and landscape pattern: urban land-use ratio, urban growth rate, compactness, expansion type, expansion speed, expansion intensity, expansion amplitude, population density, urbanization rate, NP, PD, LSI, AI, LPI, and SHAPE. For the benchmark years 2000, 2010, and 2020, interval-based expansion indicators were calculated for 1999–2000, 2009–2010, and 2019–2020. Weights were calculated separately at the city and county scales. The formulas are as follows: $$\:{Y}_{ij}=\left({x}_{ij}-Min{x}_{ij}\right)/\left(Max{x}_{ij}-Min{x}_{ij}\right)$$ (8) $$\:{Z}_{ij}=\frac{{Y}_{ij}}{{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{m}{Y}_{ij}}$$ (9) $$\:{e}_{j}=-\frac{1}{\text{ln}m}{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{m}{Z}_{ij}\times\:\text{ln}{Z}_{ij}$$ (10) $$\:{{\upalpha\:}}_{j}=1-{e}_{j}$$ (11) $$\:{w}_{j}=\frac{{{\upalpha\:}}_{j}}{{\sum\:}_{k=1}^{n}{{\upalpha\:}}_{k}}$$ (12) Where \(\:{w}_{j}\) is the entropy weight of indicator j , \(\:{\alpha\:}_{j}\) is the information utility value of indicator j , and n is the total number of indicators. The weights satisfy \(\:{0\le\:w}_{j}\le\:1\) and \(\:{\sum\:}_{j=1}^{n}{w}_{j}=1\) . After min–max normalization, a constant of 10⁻⁶ was added to the normalized values to avoid zero terms in the entropy calculation. The entropy weights of the 15 urban-expansion indicators were calculated separately at the county and city scales for 2000, 2010, and 2020 (Table 4 ), and the comprehensive urban-expansion index ( \(\:{u}_{1}\) ) was obtained through weighted aggregation. Compactness and expansion type were included in the construction of \(\:{u}_{1}\) . Total carbon storage was normalized using the same procedure, and its standardized value was used to represent the carbon-storage subsystem ( \(\:{u}_{2}\) ). \(\:{u}_{1}\) and \(\:{u}_{2}\) were subsequently used in the coupling coordination analysis. This model assesses the coordinated development between two systems. The coupling degree indicates the strength of interaction between the two systems, while the coupling coordination degree signifies the level of coordination within their interaction [ 7 , 25 ]. The formula is as follows: $$\:C=\frac{2\sqrt{{u}_{1}\times\:{u}_{2}}}{{u}_{1}+{u}_{2}}$$ (13) $$\:D=\sqrt{C\times\:T},\:T=\alpha\:{u}_{1}+\beta\:{u}_{2}$$ (14) Where, C represents coupling degree, D represents coordination degree, T represents the coordination index of the two subsystems, and α and β are undetermined coefficients. \(\:{u}_{1}\) and \(\:{u}_{2}\) respectively represent the comprehensive index of urban expansion and standardized carbon storage index. Equal weights were assigned to the two subsystems ( \(\:\alpha\:=\beta\:=0.5\) ), following common practice in coupling coordination studies. Both C and D were classified into four levels using the same thresholds: low ( \(\:0\le\:x<0.3\) ), lower ( \(\:0.3\le\:x<0.5\) ), higher ( \(\:0.5\le\:x<0.8\) ), and high ( \(\:0.8\le\:x<1.0\) ), where \(\:x\) denotes C or D. The coupling coordination types were determined by combining the corresponding classes of C and D. The cut-offs follow the four-stage classification of the coupling degree that is widely adopted in urbanization–environment coupling studies, in which C intervals of 0-0.3, 0.3–0.5, 0.5–0.8, and 0.8-1.0 correspond to the low-level coupling, antagonism, mutual adaptation, and high-level coupling stages, respectively [ 11 ]; the same intervals were applied to D so that the two indices are evaluated on an identical scale, consistent with the coordination framework of Li [ 25 ]. 4 Results 4.1 Urban expansion analysis Built-up areas in the Chengdu–Chongqing urban agglomeration were extracted from the composite-density surfaces. For each study year, the final segmentation threshold was determined by averaging the thresholds derived from the Otsu and Jenks natural-breaks methods. The resulting thresholds were 18.5, 22.0, 23.0, 22.0, and 24.5 for 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020, respectively. Following threshold segmentation, the patch containing the county-government seat was identified as the core built-up patch in each county. Isolated patches no larger than 0.5 km² within 5 km of the core-patch boundary and those no larger than 1 km² beyond this distance were removed. The retained patches were subsequently dissolved by county to generate the final built-up-area products. Boundary consistency was evaluated against the GUB dataset using 34,244 randomly distributed sample points within the fixed 142-county study area. Overall agreement for 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020 was 98.94%, 98.63%, 98.52%, 97.14%, and 96.00%, respectively, and the corresponding Kappa coefficients were 0.588, 0.615, 0.684, 0.586, and 0.693. Mean overall agreement and Kappa were 97.84% and 0.633, respectively. Because GUB and the impervious-surface input share the GAIA data lineage, these statistics indicate boundary consistency rather than independent classification accuracy. The high overall agreement relative to Kappa reflects the predominance of non-built-up samples, as shown by the large true-negative counts in Table 5 . As a supplementary cross-product sensitivity check, GLC_FCS30D class 190 was sampled at 6,600 stratified points per year, with 3,300 points allocated to each extracted class. Balanced-sample overall agreement ranged from 66.30% to 77.38%, and Kappa ranged from 0.326 to 0.548; built-up producer’s accuracy ranged from 94.71% to 96.51%, whereas user’s accuracy ranged from 34.30% to 56.88%. Area-adjusted overall agreement ranged from 93.04% to 97.27%. Because GLC_FCS30D represents pixel-scale impervious surfaces whereas the present product delineates contiguous built-up areas after patch screening, this comparison was interpreted as cross-product sensitivity rather than ground-truth accuracy. The relatively low user’s accuracy in the GLC_FCS30D comparison indicates that the extracted built-up areas are more extensive than pixel-scale impervious surfaces, which is expected because the present product delineates contiguous built-up patches after morphological processing and patch screening rather than impervious pixels per se. Two implications follow. First, the expansion indices may moderately overstate the absolute built-up extent, although the temporal pattern is unlikely to be affected because identical extraction rules were applied to all years. Second, the impact on carbon-storage estimation is limited and indirect, because carbon storage was computed from the CLCD land-use raster rather than from the extracted built-up product. If some CLCD construction-land pixels in fact contain cropland or forest—the commission-error direction suggested above—carbon storage would be underestimated by approximately 57 t C ha⁻¹ for cropland to 122 t C ha⁻¹ for forest per hectare of misclassified land, so the reported increase in regional carbon storage is, if anything, conservative. The built-up area increased from 3,140.3 km² in 2000 to 15,550.5 km² in 2020, representing a net gain of 12,410.1 km² and an increase of 395.2%. The average annual increase over the two decades was 620.5 km² a⁻¹. The spatial expansion exhibited clear stage-specific characteristics. From 2000 to 2015, built-up growth was dominated by contiguous outward expansion from existing urban cores, with urban land spreading around Chengdu, Chongqing, and other prefecture-level cities. During 2015–2020, the expansion accelerated markedly and became increasingly polycentric, as newly developed land extended from the two core cities towards surrounding districts and secondary urban nodes. Around Chengdu, substantial expansion occurred in Shuangliu, Xindu, Longquanyi, and Pidu districts, whereas the expansion of Chongqing was concentrated mainly in Yubei and the western and southwestern districts, including Jiulongpo, Shapingba, Jiangjin, and Yongchuan. Beyond the two core cities, Luzhou, Nanchong, and Meishan also emerged as important secondary growth nodes. At the county level, the largest net increases were recorded in Shuangliu District, Yubei District, Xindu District, Longquanyi District, Jiangjin District, Jiulongpo District, Pidu District, and Shapingba District. Overall, the spatial pattern evolved from predominantly core-centred outward expansion towards a dual-core and multi-node agglomeration structure. Fig. 4 Full size image Spatial distribution of county-level urban expansion characteristics from 2000 to 2020 4.2 Spatiotemporal changes in carbon storage 4.2.1 Temporal characteristics As shown in Table 6 , total carbon storage in the Chengdu–Chongqing urban agglomeration increased from 4319.2 × 10⁶ t C in 2000 to 4626.0 × 10⁶ t C in 2020. The increase was 123.5 × 10⁶ t C during 2000–2010, corresponding to an average annual increase of 12.4 × 10⁶ t C yr⁻¹, and 183.3 × 10⁶ t C during 2010–2020, corresponding to 18.3 × 10⁶ t C yr⁻¹. Thus, absolute carbon-storage growth accelerated during the latter decade. During the same period, rapid economic growth and urban expansion coincided with extensive conversion of cropland to construction land, while changes between forest land and cropland may have partly offset the carbon-storage gains associated with forest expansion. Considering different land types, from 2000 to 2020, carbon storage in cropland gradually decreased from 2797.9 × 10 6 t C to 2583.2 × 10 6 t C. In contrast, carbon storage in forest land, water bodies, construction land, and unused land gradually increased, with the largest increase occurring in forest land, which rose from 1426.3 × 10 6 t C to 1879.5 × 10 6 t C. Carbon storage in grasslands exhibited a decreasing-then-increasing trend, declining from 16.8 × 10⁶ t C in 2000 to 15.4 × 10⁶ t C in 2010 and then increasing to 16.2 × 10⁶ t C in 2020. Over the two decades, cropland and forest land accounted for the largest proportion of total carbon storage, while grasslands, water bodies, and construction land each contributed no more than 2%, and unused land accounted for less than 0.1%. The reduction in cropland carbon storage and the increase in forest carbon storage were temporally aligned with the relatively slow pace of urban development between 2000 and 2010, as well as with the rollout of the Grain-for-Green Program. This period witnessed a contraction in cropland area and an attendant loss of carbon storage, contrasting with an expansion of forest land and a corresponding gain in forest carbon. These concurrent trends suggest a potential connection between ecological restoration efforts and carbon sequestration outcomes. From 2010 to 2020, accelerated economic development was accompanied by continued cropland conversion, while some previously afforested areas were reportedly reconverted to cropland to meet food security objectives. These competing land-use pressures corresponded with a larger net increase in forest carbon storage and a greater decline in cropland carbon storage relative to the preceding decade. In the InVEST–BEF configuration, biomass density is derived from forest stock volume and therefore varies over time; the resulting forest carbon storage is 17.2%, 10.0%, and 0.3% lower than the all-InVEST estimate in 2000, 2010, and 2020, respectively. The uniform coefficients therefore overestimate the biomass carbon of the younger, lower-stocked forests of the early study period. More importantly, the all-InVEST configuration yields an increase of only 9.4% over 2000–2020, driven solely by area change, whereas the InVEST–BEF configuration yields a 31.8% increase, which additionally reflects growth in stock volume. The relationship between urban expansion and carbon storage in the Chengdu–Chongqing urban agglomeration from 2000 to 2020 showed marked spatial heterogeneity. In core urban areas, carbon storage generally showed a declining trend, whereas peripheral areas with relatively well-preserved natural vegetation tended to have higher carbon storage. This spatial correspondence may be associated with land-cover conversion during urban development. Urban expansion is typically accompanied by more building development and infrastructure construction, which may occupy vegetated and other natural land and consequently reduce regional carbon storage. In certain areas, carbon storage remained relatively stable despite continuing urban expansion. This pattern may be associated with the retention of natural vegetation and urban green spaces, as well as differences in baseline land-cover conditions. Forest carbon storage increased, whereas cropland carbon storage decreased. These changes coincided temporally with ecological restoration and continued urban expansion; however, the relative contributions of land-use transitions, ecological policies, natural forest regeneration, and climatic variability cannot be distinguished by the present analysis. The CCD showed scale-dependent temporal patterns. The city-level mean increased slightly from 0.368 in 2000 to 0.392 in 2010 and 0.394 in 2020, whereas the county-level mean decreased from 0.557 to 0.533 and 0.490. The city-level changes were modest and remained within the same coordination class, while the county-level result shifted to a lower class by 2020. These patterns indicate spatial and scale heterogeneity in the association between urban expansion and carbon storage rather than a uniform regional trajectory. This scale dependence arises from the spatial mismatch between the two subsystems, compounded by aggregation effects. At the county scale, expansion and carbon storage are concentrated in different places. In Chenghua District, a core district of Chengdu, the urban-expansion index ranged from 0.572 to 0.806, whereas the standardized carbon-storage index was low and declined from 0.319 to 0.227, and D fell from 0.704 in 2000 to 0.600 in 2020. Yuzhong District of Chongqing followed the same pattern, with D declining from 0.751 to 0.622. In contrast, forest-dominated peripheral counties combined high carbon-storage indices with low expansion indices. In Xuanhan County the carbon-storage index was 1.000 in all three years and the expansion index never exceeded 0.178, while D declined from 0.650 to 0.607; Shimian County and Gulin County showed similar declines in D, from 0.632 to 0.582 and from 0.558 to 0.487, respectively. Because the coupling degree C is penalized when the two subsystem indices are imbalanced, this widening core–periphery mismatch lowered the county-scale mean D even where both subsystems improved in absolute terms. At the city scale, the 16 prefecture-level units aggregate core districts and peripheral counties into single spatial units. Each city’s total carbon storage grew, driven mainly by its peripheral forests, while its expansion index also grew, driven by its core districts; the two aggregated indices therefore increased together and the mean D rose slightly. The divergence between scales is therefore an aggregation, or modifiable areal unit, effect superimposed on a genuine and widening spatial decoupling of urban expansion and carbon storage within the agglomeration. Compared with the large eastern urban agglomerations, the CCUA trajectory is distinctive. In the Yangtze River Delta, carbon storage declined continuously by 6.56 × 10⁷ t during 2000–2020 as the urbanization level more than tripled [ 18 ]; carbon-storage supply there decreased by 2.75% while demand increased by 226% [ 48 ]. In the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei urban agglomeration, carbon storage decreased by about 3.5 × 10⁷ Mg between 1990 and 2020, with the largest losses during 2000–2010 [ 13 ] Carbon storage in the CCUA, by contrast, increased by 7.1% over 2000–2020. This difference is consistent with the CCUA’s role as an ecological barrier in the upper Yangtze River, where forest carbon gains associated with the Grain-for-Green Program offset expansion-related losses. Coordination levels, however, are broadly comparable across regions: county-level coupling coordination in the Yellow River Basin also concentrated in the imbalance-to-low-coordination classes [ 11 ], similar to the county-scale range of 0.490–0.557 found here. Moderate coupling with strong core–periphery gradients thus appears to be common among Chinese urban agglomerations, whereas the direction of the carbon-storage trend is region-specific. This study has several limitations. First, the GUB comparison is not an independent validation because GUB and the impervious-surface input share the GAIA data lineage. Second, resampling coarse nighttime-light and NDVI data to 30 m aligns grids but does not create finer spatial information. Third, the InVEST–BEF estimates remain sensitive to land-use classification and carbon-density assumptions, including the use of regional class-mean coefficients for cropland and grassland, and the present robustness tests do not cover all parameter and scale uncertainties. Independent high-resolution validation, alternative KDE bandwidth tests, locally observed cropland and grassland coefficients, and broader carbon-pool perturbations should therefore be included in future work. Finally, urban expansion and carbon storage are not statistically independent because both are linked to land-cover information. The coupling coordination degree should therefore be interpreted as a descriptive index of relative co-variation, not as evidence of causal mechanisms or policy effects. Future research should combine the present framework with independent validation and spatial causal models. 6 Conclusion Based on the above analyses, the main findings of this study are summarized as follows. (1) The Chengdu-Chongqing urban agglomeration underwent continuous urban expansion from 2000 to 2020, with the built-up area increasing from 3,140.3 km² to 15,550.5 km². Expansion speed and intensity decreased slightly during 2005–2010 and subsequently increased, reaching their highest levels during 2015–2020. In contrast, expansion amplitude peaked during 2010–2015 and decreased slightly during 2015–2020. Spatially, urban expansion was concentrated around Chengdu and Chongqing and progressively extended towards surrounding districts and secondary urban nodes, forming a dual-core and multi-node spatial pattern. (2) Regional carbon storage exhibited an overall increasing trend from 2000 to 2020, rising from 4319.2 × 10⁶ t C to 4626.0 × 10⁶ t C. The increment during 2010–2020 was greater than that during 2000–2010, indicating an acceleration in absolute carbon-storage growth during the latter decade. Spatially, high carbon storage values were persistently concentrated in the mountainous eastern and southwestern peripheries, whereas low values predominated in the central urbanized corridor. A marked decline in cropland carbon storage was offset by a substantial increase in forest carbon storage—a pattern that coincided temporally with the implementation of the Grain-for-Green Program. However, attributing these changes solely to policy intervention is precluded by the concurrent influences of accelerated urban expansion, which converted both cropland and revegetated areas to built-up areas, as well as potential contributions from natural forest regeneration and climatic variability. The findings thus indicate a broad association between land-use transitions and carbon stock dynamics, while the precise causal mechanisms warrant further investigation. (3) The CCD exhibited scale-dependent temporal patterns from 2000 to 2020. The city-level mean increased slightly from 0.368 to 0.394, whereas the county-level mean decreased from 0.557 to 0.490. Spatially, higher values occurred in both peri-urban and forest-dominated counties, indicating marked heterogeneity in the relative association between urban expansion and carbon storage. These results support differentiated land-use and ecological-management strategies but do not establish causal policy effects. Based on these findings and the spatial distribution of the coupling coordination degree, a “strict control in cores, optimization in peripheries” strategy is proposed for the CCUA’s dual-core, multi-node structure. First, in the low-coordination core districts of Chengdu and Chongqing such as Chenghua, Jinjiang, and Yuzhong Districts, strict urban growth boundaries should be enforced, with priority given to the redevelopment and infill of existing built-up areas, compensation of urban green space for converted vegetated land, and integration of soil-carbon-loss assessment into the environmental impact assessment of new development. Second, in the high-coordination, forest-dominated peripheral counties such as Xuanhan, Shimian, Gulin, and Ebian Counties, ecological protection redlines should remain inviolable, and ecological compensation and carbon-sink trading pilots should be expanded so that carbon-storage gains are translated into development benefits rather than converted to intensive construction. Third, secondary nodes such as Luzhou, Nanchong, and Meishan should pursue compact expansion along existing corridors and avoid encroachment on high-carbon-storage forest land, so that multi-node growth does not reproduce the core–periphery mismatch observed at the county scale.
