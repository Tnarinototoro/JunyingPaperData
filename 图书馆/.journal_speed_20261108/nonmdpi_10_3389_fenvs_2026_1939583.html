Abstract

The synergy of industrial transformation and ecological security is an important pair in the regional high-quality development. Previous studies of the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration have mainly examined industrial development, ecological carrying capacity, low-carbon transition, or environmental resilience separately, leaving the coordinated evolution of industrial structure optimization and urban ecological resilience insufficiently understood. This gap is particularly relevant for late-developing coastal urban agglomerations, where rapid industrial restructuring occurs alongside increasing ecological pressures. To address this issue, taking the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration as the study area, an evaluation system for industrial structure optimization and urban ecological resilience was constructed, and the coupling coordination between the two systems was examined from 2012 to 2022. The contribution of this study lies in integrating industrial structure optimization (ISO) and urban ecological resilience (UER) within a unified analytical framework and simultaneously examining their temporal evolution, spatial differentiation, subsystem-lagging characteristics, and interacting driving factors. The findings extend the empirical understanding of industrial–ecological coordination in late-developing coastal urban agglomerations and provide a quantitative basis for differentiated sustainability governance. Results indicate that the industrial structure optimization and the urban ecological resilience had steadily improved over the study period, however, there remained large inter-city variation. The average coupling coordination degree rose from 0.338 in 2012 to 0.432 in 2022, which reflects the changing trend of mild disorder-to-near-order but overall systems still not into the coordinated development stages. At the spatial level, the coordination pattern was featured with a remarkable core, higher values in northern regions and differentiated performance among coastal port cities. Nanning booth performed as a leader city and FangchengGang better Chongzuo always weak. The results of the geographical detector show that development of digital economyhas a dominant explanatory power on gross regional environmental quality, followed by science–technology input, economic development, transport accessibility and environmental regulation. The results of interaction detection indicate that the synergistic effect of digital economy and science-technology input has the strongest explanatory power. These findings identify the major factors and interaction patterns associated with spatial differences in industrial–ecological coordination and provide quantitative evidence for differentiated governance in late-developing coastal urban agglomerations.

1 Introduction

The green and low-carbon transformation is increasingly a necessary condition for high-quality regional development. The urban agglomerations have not only carried the gathering of population, industry and infrastructure construction but also accumulated high-density resource consumption, environmental pressure and climate hazards. In this context, the coordination between industrial structure optimization and urban ecological resilience is an important issue for regional sustainable development. The decoupling of industrial carbon-emission has been revealed as a key issue for Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration in new research works (Ma et al., 2025). Early low-carbon city initiatives also laid an institutional foundation to regional transition. Newer evidence have revealed that carbon reduction is largely done through green innovation and urban-agglomeration expansion (Tao and Huang, 2026).

Marine ecological construction in the Beibu Gulf has been embedded within a framework of regional economic coordination (Liu, 2020). Carrying-capacity studies also reveal other limits related to marine resource availability and environmental stress (Li Y. et al., 2023). Basic ecological-footprint accounting indicates regional ecological-assets demand is not uniformly dispersed across regions (Pang et al., 2022); and coastline research shows the economic output is charged at different rates in relation to ecological impacts being sustained (Sun et al., 2020).

The Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration is located in the southwestern coastal region of China, and is an important site for the New International Land-Sea Trade Corridor and China-ASEAN cooperation. It contains the following cities: Nanning, Beihai, Qinzhou, Fangchenggang, Yulin and Chongzuo. This region combines characteristics of both coastal, border and late-developing zones. Ports, logistics corridors, and outward-oriented economic sectors have rapidly grown during the last decade. The region also needs to contend with practical difficulties, such as an above-average share in energy-intensive industries, sensitive marine ecosystems and rising exposure to extreme climate events. Thus the conflict between industrial development and ecological limits has become more starkly visible.

Prior studies investigated collaborative industrial development in Beibu, Gulf city group and the competitiveness differences between ports and cities (Zhang et al., 2012). Early studies on the construction of low-carbon city region (Liu Z. et al., 2011). The drivers of human-resource development (Qian, 2014) and logistics agglomeration (Zhang, 2010) as well as rural human-capital constraints were also examined. In a related study, low-carbon urban construction was extended to eco-green regional culture (Liu Z. H. et al., 2011). Each of these studies, combined, define the region as a unique urban coastal agglomeration that will phase into development last.

There have been many studies on coupling coordination in developed urban agglomerations (e.g., Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei region, Yangtze River Delta and Pearl River Delta) by researchers. Though systematic evidence for coastal urban agglomerations that are late developers remains lacking. 1.4. Previous studies thus referred to industrial coordination (Zhang and Wei, 2009), low-carbon urbanization (Liu Z. et al., 2011; Liu Z. H. et al., 2011), marine ecological-economic coordinated development (Liu, 2020), and environmental carrying capacity (Li Y. et al., 2023). Thus, an additional risk-response perspective has been incorporated into flood-resilience research by (Deng et al., 2024).

In this study, urban ecological resilience is defined as the capacity of an urban ecological system to absorb environmental pressures while maintaining essential ecological functions, to support recovery following disturbance, and to adapt to longer-term environmental change. Because consistent city-level observations of post-disturbance recovery trajectories are unavailable for the full study period, UER is operationalized primarily as a set of ecological, infrastructural, and governance capacities that support resilience rather than as a direct measurement of dynamic recovery following a specific shock.

Existing studies have provided important evidence on industrial coordination, port competitiveness, low-carbon transition, marine ecological-economic development, environmental carrying capacity, and disaster resilience in the Beibu Gulf region. However, these research streams have largely developed in parallel. Industrial restructuring and ecological conditions are commonly examined as separate development outcomes, and relatively little attention has been given to whether changes in industrial structure optimization and urban ecological resilience evolve in a coordinated manner. In addition, previous regional studies rarely integrate temporal evolution, spatial differentiation, subsystem-lagging characteristics, and the interaction of driving factors within a unified analytical framework.

Accordingly, this study makes three contributions. First, industrial structure optimization and urban ecological resilience are incorporated into an integrated analytical framework to examine their interdependence rather than treating industrial transformation and ecological resilience as independent processes. Second, their coupling coordination is traced over an eleven-year period, allowing temporal evolution, inter-city spatial differentiation, and subsystem-lagging patterns to be identified simultaneously. Third, factor detection and interaction detection are combined to examine both the individual explanatory power of potential drivers and the effects arising from their interactions.

2 Literature review and research gap

2.1 Industrial structure optimization

Optimization of industrial structure has long been one of the core topics in economic development study. Traditional studies generally stressed the development of three-sector structure and a movement from primary and secondary sectors to higher value adding service sectors. Against the backdrop of deeper green development and digital transformation, this concept has gradually extended from a two-dimensional framework of upgrading and rationalization to a multidimensional framework that encompasses the dimensions of greenization and digitalization as well. Thus, the industrial structure optimization mentioned here does not only refer to the change of industrial shares but also focus on several indices including allocation efficiency, technological intensity, resource-use efficiency and environmental performance.

In the context of Beibu Gulf, city-group coordination (Zhang and Wei, 2009), harbor-city competitiveness (Zhang et al., 2012) and human-resource constraints on development zoning (Qian, 2014; Mo, 2011) were first approached in an industrial way. Further studies analysed Port cost efficiency (Jian, 2015) and enclave-economy cooperation (Feng and Xiang, 2014). Recent studies have assessed competitiveness in port-groups (Li et al., 2025), urban business environments in (Lu et al., 2024) and logistics competitiveness under carbon context (Lu, 2024). Low-carbon industrial transfer has been increasingly evidenced in carbon-emission decoupling (Ma et al., 2025) and green innovation (Tao and Huang, 2026).

2.2 Urban ecological resilience

Urban ecological resilience, which is an extension of this theory at the urban scale and focuses on urban ecological systems ability to resist shocks while maintaining structure and functionDisturbanceAdaptive capacitylearning and transformation also determines whether or not a system can transition or adapt does accommodate shock. Most existing evaluation frameworks consider ecological context, environmental quality indicators, infrastructure capacity and governance ability. Coastal ecological resilience, disaster resilience and marine ecological security have become important research directions with the worsening of climate risk. Nonetheless, systematic assessment of ecological resilience in coastal metropolitan areas continues to rely on more theoretical implementations (e.g., Modeling Environmental Resilience) (Tao and Huang, 2026), particularly where both port industries and marine ecosystem conservation within close proximity compete for space (Liu, 2020).

Ecological risk was first investigated in regard to initial tsunami exposure (Huang et al., 2015) and distribution of urban heat-island (Wang and Jing, 2015). Pollution studies focused on coastal pollution sources related to shipping and industry (Lao et al., 2021) and assessments of offshore wind potential for renewable energy purposes (Chen et al., 2020). This was followed by remote-sensing research that tracked land subsidence (Wang et al., 2022) and dynamics of reclamation (Lu et al., 2023). Anthropogenic pressure on intertidal biodiversity (Lei et al., 2025) and drivers of vegetation-cover change were studied ecologically (Xu et al., 2025). Air pollution recording was related to stroke hospitalisation evidence from public-health (Li M. J. et al., 2023), and regional risk assessments expanded the spatial hazard view (Wang et al., 2016). Emergency-response research examined the transport of relief supplies (Yue and Panichakarn, 2023), and sediment articles reconstructed pollution chronologies (Guo et al., 2020). Recent studies have examined the disturbance of mangroves (Tao et al., 2025), particulate air pollution (Zhang et al., 2021), and how urban areas developed into heat-island effects (Qin et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Groundwater quality (Dai et al., 2013), offshore wind-energy justice (Nie et al., 2024), mangrove microbial communities (Gong et al., 2019) and storm-surge vulnerability (Liu et al., 2023).

Studies including analyses of marine ecological-economic coordination (Liu, 2020) and marine tourism development (Wu et al., 2020) are also among researches on ecological-resilience. An assessment of resource and environmental carrying capacity has been conducted at the urban-agglomeration (Li Y. et al., 2023) as well as coastal-territorial scales (Ou et al., 2022). Studies of the mountain-river-sea transition zone have estimated ecosystemservice values (Zhang L. L. et al., 2023) and assessed future eco-spatial carbon sinks using multi-scenario models (Qin et al., 2023). Further, ecological security patterns (Zhang Z. et al., 2023) and carbon-oriented territorial restoration (Qin et al., 2025) support the integration of ecological function with spatial governance. These can also be completed by ecological-footprint (Pang et al., 2022) and coastline-sustainability studies (Sun et al., 2020).

Mapping coupling coordination model is popularly used to analyze the correlation between Ecological system, Economic system, Social and Industrial systems. It is common in the field of regional sustainable development to analyse the coupling relationship between industrial structure and ecological environment, economic development and environmental quality (or urbanization and ecological security). Nevertheless, the majority of studies focus on static evaluation or single-scale analysis with less attention to temporal evolution, space differentiation, lagging types and driving mechanism simultaneously. Moreover, limited empirical evidence is available for the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration.

Marine resources (Li Y. et al., 2023) and coastal territorial systems (Ou et al., 2022) have also been characterized with carrying-capacity approaches. Complementary metrics have come from ecological-footprint accounting (Pang et al., 2022), ecosystem-service valuation (Zhang L. 2.4 Research gap

Research on the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration has examined industrial coordination, low-carbon urban development (Liu Z. et al., 2011; Liu Z. H. et al., 2011), port efficiency (Jian, 2015), marine ecological–economic coordination (Liu, 2020), environmental carrying capacity (Li Y. et al., 2023; Ou et al., 2022), ecological-footprint pressure (Pang et al., 2022), disaster resilience (Deng et al., 2024), and ecological restoration from different perspectives (Qin et al., 2025). These studies provide an important foundation for understanding regional economic transformation and ecological constraints, but the corresponding research streams have largely developed separately.

These limitations are not unique to the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration. Research on other coastal cities and urban agglomerations has increasingly addressed the relationship between economic development and ecological sustainability. For example, studies of the Yangtze River Delta have examined the coupling coordination between economic and environmental systems and its spatiotemporal differentiation (Deng et al., 2022), while research on the Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Greater Bay Area has explored the optimization of land use by balancing economic linkages and ecological values (Pan et al., 2022). Ecological resilience research in the Hangzhou Bay Metropolitan Area has further revealed the spatial and temporal dynamics of resilience and its responses to land-use change (Xu et al., 2024). At a broader international scale, research covering port cities across Asia, Africa, and Europe has examined the coordination between urban expansion and vegetation dynamics and identified substantial heterogeneity across different development levels (Dong et al., 2024). These studies demonstrate growing interest in the interaction between development and ecological systems in coastal regions. However, they generally focus on economy–environment coordination, urbanization–ecological relationships, land-use optimization, or ecological resilience as distinct analytical themes. Relatively limited attention has been paid to the direct coupling between industrial structure optimization and urban ecological resilience, particularly within an integrated framework that simultaneously considers temporal evolution, spatial differentiation, subsystem-lagging characteristics, and interactions among multiple driving factors.

Against this broader research background, three gaps remain particularly relevant. First, industrial restructuring and ecological resilience are frequently examined as separate development processes. Although previous studies provide important evidence on economic transformation and ecological conditions, relatively few directly evaluate whether industrial structure optimization and urban ecological resilience evolve in a mutually coordinated manner.

Second, existing research remains fragmented in terms of analytical dimensions. Many studies investigate temporal change, spatial heterogeneity, ecological resilience, or influencing factors, but relatively limited research integrates long-term temporal evolution, inter-city spatial differentiation, and subsystem-lagging diagnosis within the same analytical framework.

Third, economic, technological, infrastructural, institutional, and ecological drivers are often assessed individually or through their independent effects. This approach may underestimate the combined influence of digitalization, technological investment, transport accessibility, environmental regulation, and economic capacity on industrial–ecological coordination.

To address these gaps, this study integrates entropy-weighted evaluation, coupling coordination analysis, subsystem-lagging diagnosis, and geographical detector analysis. By applying this integrated framework to the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration, the study not only provides new evidence for a late-developing coastal urban agglomeration but also contributes to the broader understanding of how industrial transformation and ecological resilience interact in coastal regions undergoing rapid economic restructuring.

3 Materials and methods

3.1 Study area and data sources

The selected study area includes six prefecture-level cities of Nanning, Beihai, Qinzhou, Fangchenggang, Yulin and Chongzuo in the Guangxi part of the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration. The period of the study is 2012–2022, leading to an eleven-year city-level panel dataset.

The study covers the period from 2012 to 2022 for three reasons. First, 2012 represents an appropriate starting point for observing a relatively continuous stage of accelerated regional integration, industrial restructuring, and ecological governance in the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration. The period therefore captures a major phase of regional economic and environmental transformation. Second, using an eleven-year observation window makes it possible to distinguish short-term fluctuations from longer-term changes in industrial structure optimization, urban ecological resilience, and their coupling coordination. Third, 2022 was selected as the endpoint because it was the latest year for which the required economic, environmental, infrastructure, and governance indicators could be consistently matched across all six cities when the dataset was constructed. Consequently, the 2012–2022 period provides a comparatively consistent city-level panel for examining the long-term evolution of the two systems. The data are mostly from the China City Statistical Yearbook, Guangxi Statistical Yearbook, statistical yearbooks of six cities, statistical communiques on national economic and social development, China Environmental Statistical Yearbook ecological environmental quality bulletin, China Urban Construction Statistical Yearbook fiscal yearly book local financial final account.

A small number of missing observations occurred in the annual statistical series because some indicators were not reported for every city in every year. Where an observation was missing between two available annual observations, linear interpolation was used to estimate the missing value. This method was selected because most indicators used in this study evolve on an annual basis and generally exhibit gradual rather than instantaneous changes. Linear interpolation therefore preserves the temporal continuity of the city-level panel while avoiding the loss of complete city-year observations. Interpolation was used only for missing values within the observed time series and did not replace available original observations.

6 Discussion

Results show that the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration has accomplished continuous industrial structure optimization and urban ecology resilience enhancement, yet insufficient coordination across these two systems still exists. Such finding is as per for the development level of late-developing coastal urban agglomerations. Economic growth associated with rapid industrial expansion and infrastructure construction, on the other hand, is realized much more rapidly; however, ecological resilience often does not keep pace owing to the long time horizons required for environmental governance capacity enhancement, ecological restoration, and co-ordination of institutions.

This interpretation is consistent with evidence that industrial carbon emissions and economic growth may become diplomatically decoupled at best only slowly (Ma et al., 2025). Research on flood-resilience similarly demonstrate that ecological risk and response capacity in cities diverge (Deng et al., 2024). The studies of green innovation also indicate that the institutional and technological adjustment may fall behind the urban-agglomeration expansion (Tao and Huang, 2026).

This pattern of industrial lagging behind is shown at the overall level and points to further prioritisation of industrial transformation. Ecological resilience improved continuously, but the industrial system is still not development more to high-quality, rationalized and green. As for Yulin and Chongzuo, the most important task is to upgrade industries and foster growth in higher value-added sectors. By contrast, Qinzhou and Fangchenggang address an ecological-lagging issue with port-based heavy industries putting higher environmental pressures. This is why not all policies can be the same.

The important policy implication comes from the dominant of development of digital economy and science-technology input. The connection of industrial upgrading and ecological governance with digital technology through smart manufacturing, pollution monitoring and environmental early warning is a new access to realize the decoupling of economic development from resource consumption. Such linkage is motivated by green-innovation evidence (Tao and Huang, 2026). Such offshore wind-energy assessments indicate how technical implementation may redefine regional energy systems (Chen et al., 2020). Multi-scenario carbon-sink studies are an instance that shows how digital and modelling tools can contribute to ecological governance (Qin et al., 2023), whereas restoration studies oriented toward carbon-production reflect the importance of linking technological ability to spatial ecological security (Qin et al., 2025). We argue that it is not unreasonable to make simultaneous investments in the rural and digital infrastructure alongside innovation capacity.

The enduring core-periphery inequality is another issue that warrants attention. What gives Nanning the stronger economic, digital and innovation capacity to stay on top. While it does radiate some effect to other nearby cities its done so in a limited manner. The advantages of digital transformation, scientific innovation and ecological governance may remain limited to the core city without more robust regional coordination mechanisms. As a result, internal disparities must be lessened by means of cross-city industrial cooperation, eco-compensation, tech transfer and joint environmental governance.

The synergy between logistics and the base in Nanning proves that the advantage of core cities can strengthen concentration (Zhang, 2010). Studies of port-group competitiveness, likewise, expose uneven intercity competencies (Li et al., 2025). Spatial assessments of business environments have reported stable agglomeration effects (Lu et al., 2024), while low-carbon logistics studies found a non-linear positive influence on connectivity and environmental performance (Lu, 2024). These results provide support for more robust coordination mechanisms across cities.

From a sustainability perspective, the improvement in coupling coordination reflects more than the simultaneous growth of industrial and ecological indicators. It indicates the extent to which economic restructuring can proceed without generating ecological pressures that exceed the adaptive and governance capacities of the urban system. Sustainable industrial transformation therefore requires both improvements in economic productivity and reductions in the environmental intensity of development. For the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration, this relationship is particularly important because port-based industrialization, coastal ecological sensitivity, and rapid infrastructure expansion coexist within the same regional system. The observed improvement in coupling coordination suggests progress toward a development pattern in which industrial upgrading and ecological protection become increasingly compatible, although the differences among cities indicate that this transition remains spatially uneven.

The results also highlight the multidimensional nature of urban sustainability. Digital economy development and science-technology input are associated not only with industrial upgrading but also with improvements in resource allocation, environmental monitoring, pollution control, and green technological diffusion. Their strong interaction effect suggests that technological transformation may contribute to sustainability most effectively when digital infrastructure and innovation capacity develop jointly. Similarly, the interaction of transport accessibility, economic capacity, and environmental regulation indicates that sustainability cannot be achieved through environmental policy alone. Economic, technological, infrastructural, and institutional conditions need to be coordinated so that economic development, ecological protection, and regional connectivity reinforce rather than constrain one another.

Spatial inequality in coupling coordination also has implications for regional sustainability. Persistent core–periphery differences may limit the diffusion of green technology, environmental governance capacity, and high-value-added industries from leading cities to less-developed cities. If these disparities persist, environmental pressures may become concentrated in cities specializing in resource-intensive or port-based industries, while the benefits of technological upgrading remain concentrated in regional cores. Strengthening inter-city technology transfer, ecological compensation, coordinated environmental governance, and green industrial cooperation is therefore relevant not only to regional economic integration but also to the long-term environmental and social sustainability of the urban agglomeration.

Overall, the findings suggest that sustainable development in the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration depends on the coordinated transformation of industrial, technological, ecological, and institutional systems. Improvements in coupling coordination should therefore be understood as part of a broader transition toward a development model that combines industrial upgrading, efficient resource use, ecological protection, technological innovation, and spatially balanced regional development.

7 Policy implications

The first, build to strengthen the dual-wheeled drive of digital technology and scientific innovation. Port industries, manufacturing processes and ecological governance are combined with IT through industrial internet, big data and smart monitoring systems as well. We need to create a regional green technology trading and transfer platform, so as to speed up the diffusion of low-carbon technologies, pollution-control technologies, and marine ecological restoration technologies.

Secondly, differentiated city-specific strategies should be employed. To strengthen the regional innovation and service center functions, give full play to the aggregation effect of reform investment, and improve spillover effect. We feel that it is necessary for Beihai, Qinzhou and Fangchenggang to tackle ecological-lagging problems by raising the accessibility standards of environment industry-based structure, speeding up the transformation of petrochemicals, steel and other industries towards green direction but also cultivating marine biomedicine, coastal tourism and other emerging green industries. Yulin and Chongzuo NEED TO tackle industrial-lagging problems by modernizing traditional industries, developing unique industries and elevating the quality of the border-oriented logistics trade. Install high-resolution remotely-sensed tools catering near coastal cities that would enable the assessment of marine production and restoration spaces (Du et al., 2025). For instance, as stated previously, coastal-zone protection (Cheng et al., 2021) must be designed in consideration of impacts on livelihoods and locally embedded marine-resource practices can inform development strategies adapted to particular cultural contexts (Luo et al., 2023).

Third, they need better regional coordination mechanisms. Integrate industrial planning and ecological protection into the urban agglomeration governance system. Promote horizontal ecological compensation, develop cross-city ecological restoration, jointly protect the marine environment and form green logistics networks. Core cities should ensure transfer of technology, industry and capacity to peripheral ones.

Fourth, environmental regulation should be improved and synchronized with innovation policy. Regulation must be finely tuned, flexible and aligned with specific local industrial frameworks. Public green finance can be combined with innovation incentives and market-based instruments, such as emission trading, energy-use rights trading and carbon trading to promote endogenous green transformation of enterprises.

8 Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the construction of the indicator system is limited by the availability of data and mainly involving traditional statistical indicators. Instead, future research can apply marine ecological indicators such as mangrove area, nearshore water quality and marine disaster risk; industrial indicators such as share of port-based industries and penetration rate of industrial digitalization. Second, while the geographical detector model finds important dominant factors, its ability to identify transmission mechanisms is limited. Future research may extend the use of mediation models, spatial econometric and structural equation modelling, to investigate causal pathways and spatial spillover effects. The third limitation, the spatial scale of this paper is only limited to prefecture-level cities. The data compiled for each of the microbe size classes is ideally suited to allow future research at the county-level or by employing remote sensing data that can provide a more precise spatial analysis. Fourth, strengthening system dynamics or agent-based modeling for policy simulation and scenario analysis can also provide forward-looking policy evidence.

Mangrove disturbance and intertidal biodiversity should be considered for future indicator refinement (Tao et al., 2025; Lei et al., 2025). Remote sensing can monitor reclamation dynamics (Lu et al., 2023), while offshore energy production changes the geography of distribution and governance (Nie et al., 2024). Storm-surge vulnerability should be explicitly represented (Liu et al., 2023). Further, modelling for biomass carbon-sinks (Qin et al., 2023), and approaches targeting carbon-focused ecological restoration (Qin et al., 2025) can add value to long-term assessments of resilience; high-resolution UAV-based mapping in the coastal area can also explicitly support spatial precision (Du et al., 2025).

9 Conclusion

This study constructed an integrated evaluation framework for industrial structure optimization and urban ecological resilience and examined their coupling coordination in the Beibu Gulf Urban Agglomeration from 2012 to 2022. Based on entropy-weighted evaluation, coupling coordination analysis, spatial differentiation analysis, and geographical detector analysis, three main conclusions can be drawn.

First, the optimization level of industrial structure and urban ecological resilience was significantly improved during the study period, but there were differences among cities. The systems were dominated by Nanning, with Fangchenggang notably weak. Industrial upgrading, infrastructure improvement became the main sources for system enhancement; industrial greenization and ecological governance capacity improvement need to be further strengthened.

Second, the coupling coordination degree presented continuous uptrend but still in a relatively low level. The average from 0.338 in 2012 increased to 0.432 in 2022, moving from mild disorder to close to disorder. The entire region was not yet for development coherence. Most lagging type was dominated by industrial lagging, while due to the environmental pressure of port-based industries in Qinzhou and Fangchenggang (2), characteristics of ecological-lagging appeared.

Third, both the development of digital economy and science-technology input were core driving factors of coupling coordination, while economic development, transport accessibility and environment regulation acted as important support factors. The strongest interaction occurred between digital economy development and science-technology input, with a q value of 0.854. Thus, their joint interaction explained 85.4% of the spatial differentiation in coupling coordination, substantially exceeding the explanatory power of either factor considered separately.
