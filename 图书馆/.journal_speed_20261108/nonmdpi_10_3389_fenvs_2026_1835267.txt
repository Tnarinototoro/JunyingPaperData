Department of Silviculture, Faculty of Forestry and Wood Technology, Mendel University in Brno, Brno, Czechia Article metrics View details Abstract Introduction: Large-scale forest disturbances caused by bark beetle outbreaks, windstorms, and climate change have created extensive forest clearings that require rapid reforestation. In practice, however, reforestation is frequently delayed by shortages of seedlings, labor, and financial resources. Such delays accelerate soil degradation, reduce the productivity of future forest stands, and increase long-term restoration costs. Although pioneer tree species are widely recognized for their ecological benefits, their role as an economically efficient emergency reforestation strategy has received little attention. Methods: This study develops a simulation model to evaluate the long-term economic performance of alternative reforestation strategies under post-disturbance conditions. Using detailed data from Czech forestry, the model simulates 160 years of forest development while incorporating silvicultural operations, harvesting, timber revenues, varying levels of soil degradation caused by delayed reforestation, and stochastic variation in forest characteristics and market conditions. Eight alternative reforestation strategies, including direct sowing of birch, planted birch, and two-phase regeneration combining birch with beech, are evaluated using free cash flow, net present value, and internal rate of return. Results: The results demonstrate that direct sowing of birch substantially reduces establishment costs while maintaining favorable long-term economic performance. Among the broadleaved alternatives, birch consistently provides the highest economic returns and remains financially attractive even under increasing soil degradation associated with delayed reforestation. Although Norway spruce generates higher expected timber revenues under historical price conditions, these returns are accompanied by substantially greater ecological and production risks that are not reflected in conventional financial indicators. The simulation further shows that establishing pioneer birch stands followed by subsequent conversion to beech represents a viable strategy that combines rapid site stabilization with long-term forest diversification. Discussion: These findings suggest that birch should not be regarded merely as a temporary pioneer species but as a strategic component of climate-resilient forest management. The proposed simulation framework provides a practical decision-support tool for selecting economically efficient emergency reforestation strategies following large-scale forest disturbances and can readily be adapted to forest ecosystems beyond the Czech Republic. 1 Introduction Forest ecosystems are increasingly affected by natural disturbances such as windstorms, bark beetle outbreaks, and wildfires, all of which have far-reaching consequences for forest management. Although preventive measures–including maintaining appropriate forest stand composition and installing fire detection systems in high-risk areas–are continuously being implemented, the impacts of such disturbances cannot be entirely eliminated. Following a catastrophic event, rapid mitigation of its consequences is essential, as delayed intervention may further aggravate forest degradation. During bark beetle outbreaks, infested trees are harvested and processed to prevent the further spread of the pest. However, when infestations become extensive, forest management operations may reach their limits in terms of available labor and harvesting machinery. Consequently, large forest clearings may emerge that cannot be reforested immediately, despite their undesirable ecological and economic consequences. This study investigates possible approaches to addressing this situation and evaluates the consequences of delayed reforestation of forest clearings. Although the proposed model is based on forest management conditions in the Czech Republic, which belongs to the European interior mixed forests bioregion within the Western Eurasian forest biome, the proposed methodology is sufficiently general to be applicable to other forest bioregions worldwide. 1.1 Situation in Czech forestry Over the past two decades, forestry in the Czech Republic has experienced a dramatic increase in disturbance events associated with climate change. In 2007, forests were severely affected by the Kyrill windstorm, while bark beetle infestations began to spread rapidly from 2016 onwards. The outbreak culminated in 2020, when approximately 47% of forest stands, predominantly Norway spruce monocultures, were severely affected. As a consequence, salvage logging increased dramatically between 2017 and 2022, as illustrated in Figure 1. This unprecedented situation substantially increased the demand for forestry workers and consequently contributed to wage growth within the forestry sector (Hampel et al., 2021). Current reforestation strategies for disturbance-affected areas increasingly incorporate climate change projections, particularly those concerning rising temperatures and increasingly irregular precipitation patterns. Recent studies have therefore proposed approaches for optimizing the local tree-species composition according to expected future climatic conditions (e.g., Hlásny et al., 2022). The prevailing strategy is to establish species-rich, structurally diverse, and uneven-aged forest stands, which also play a key role in maintaining ecosystem multifunctionality (Lee et al., 2025). One of the key decisions when reforesting areas affected by bark beetle outbreaks is whether to use natural or artificial regeneration. Natural regeneration allows forest clearings to develop without direct human intervention, relying on seed dispersal from surrounding trees. Although naturally regenerated forests require a longer establishment period, they generally exhibit greater resilience. In contrast, nursery-grown seedlings often suffer from root damage during transplantation, making them particularly vulnerable to drought stress, which may result in high mortality rates and necessitate repeated planting. Where neighboring forest stands exhibit high species diversity, natural regeneration may also produce more diverse forest communities than those established using nursery-grown seedlings. From an economic perspective, natural regeneration is highly attractive because it requires virtually no establishment costs during the initial reforestation phase. Its principal disadvantage is the need for subsequent silvicultural interventions to achieve the desired stand structure, resulting in additional management costs. Owing to the exceptional extent of the bark beetle outbreak, the statutory deadline for reforesting harvested areas in the Czech Republic was extended from two to 5 years, thereby creating favorable conditions for natural forest regeneration wherever site conditions permitted. The principal limitation of natural forest regeneration is its feasibility within a reasonable time frame. The need for rapid removal of infested trees, particularly in areas of extensive disturbance, led to the widespread deployment of harvesters and forwarders. However, the intensive use of heavy machinery caused considerable soil compaction, thereby reducing the potential for successful natural regeneration. This problem has been further exacerbated by prolonged periods of drought. Moreover, in large forest clearings, only a limited number of mature trees remain capable of producing sufficient quantities of seed. Consequently, an effective restoration strategy requires a combination of natural and artificial regeneration. For example, the Forest Enterprise of Mendel University in Brno recorded approximately 200 ha of catastrophic forest clearings in 2020. Its primary strategy was to promote natural forest regeneration wherever feasible; where this approach proved unsuitable, the enterprise relied on seedlings produced in its own forest nurseries. However, current nursery production satisfies only about half of the required planting stock. Figure 2 illustrates recent trends in timber harvesting and reforestation methods in the Czech Republic. Historically, natural and artificial regeneration have been used in relatively stable proportions, whereas since 2021 the share of natural regeneration has increased noticeably. Nevertheless, since 2018, the overall rate of forest regeneration has remained insufficient relative to the area harvested, resulting in the persistence of extensive forest clearings. This shortage resulted primarily from the unprecedented increase in demand, particularly for broadleaved species, following extensive salvage logging (Figure 2). Although forest nurseries substantially increased production and gradually adapted to changing species demand (Figure 3), they were unable to satisfy total market demand. At the same time, seedlings were also required for other large-scale restoration projects, particularly the reclamation of surface lignite mines. Furthermore, prolonged drought caused substantial mortality in previously reforested areas, creating additional demand for replacement planting. Seed production was further constrained by the exceptionally poor acorn and beech mast crop recorded in 2021. Besides seedling availability, planting costs also became an increasingly important factor influencing reforestation activities. Figure 4 presents a comparison of price lists from one of the leading forest nursery companies in the Czech Republic. Overall, seedling prices increased by approximately 60% between 2016 and 2021. This considerable increase was driven by rapidly growing demand for planting material, partial failures during seed production, and rising labor and production costs associated with nursery operations. More generally, the importance of seedling production as a critical component of successful reforestation has been highlighted by Mousavijad et al. (2022). Taken together, these circumstances demonstrate that the immediate reforestation of forest clearings was not feasible and consequently became a long-term challenge for Czech forestry. Large clearings frequently arise following the harvesting of Norway spruce monocultures severely affected by bark beetle outbreaks. It is reasonable to assume that soils at these sites are depleted due to long-term monoculture management and, in particular, exhibit increased acidity (De Vries et al., 2014). Although acidic soils are unsuitable for many tree species, Scots pine and birch generally tolerate these conditions well. This problem is not confined to Western Eurasia but has been documented worldwide. Dinesh et al. (2003) demonstrated that deforestation frequently results in soil degradation and nutrient depletion. Similarly, Kassa et al. (2017) reported that deforestation adversely affected soil fertility as well as soil carbon and nitrogen stocks in the White Nile Basin of Ethiopia. An et al. (2008) investigated the effects of deforestation on soil degradation in the Ziwuling region of China and found that soil organic carbon and nitrogen declined sharply during the years immediately following forest removal. Deforestation has also been shown to reduce the availability of essential soil nutrients, including nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and sodium (Iticha et al., 2016), as well as micronutrients such as iron, copper, manganese, and zinc (Akhtaruzzaman et al., 2018). Likewise, Hayat et al. (2021) reported that tree removal in the community forests of Lower Galliyat, Pakistan, led to increased soil erosion, declining soil quality, and substantial nutrient losses. Collectively, these findings indicate that even relatively short periods of unreforested forest clearings can lead to progressive soil degradation, underscoring the importance of minimizing their duration. Under the resource constraints that typically accompany large-scale forest disturbances, pioneer tree species–including birch, aspen, alder, and rowan–represent a promising option for rapid reforestation. Pioneer species are generally characterized by their ability to colonize a wide range of habitats and climatic conditions, including environmentally harsh sites. They exhibit rapid juvenile growth, high tolerance to adverse environmental conditions, and contribute to soil recovery through both physical and biochemical processes. The influence of pioneer tree species on forest productivity and nutrient cycling has been examined by Stark et al. (2015). A comprehensive review by Jonczak et al. (2020) describes the mechanisms through which birch modifies soil properties, including nutrient cycling, microbial activity, organic matter turnover, and overall soil functioning. Their review highlights the important role of birch in regulating soil processes during early succession and in the restoration of degraded sites. These properties are particularly valuable in regions with a long history of cultivating non-native Norway spruce (Martiník et al., 2017). Integrating ecological theory with empirical observations, Mikola et al. (2021) suggested that early-successional pioneer species, such as birch, allocate resources preferentially to rapid growth under conditions of abundant light and limited competition, enabling them to establish successfully on disturbed sites before being replaced by late-successional competitors. Similarly, Kitao et al. (2022) quantified the competitive performance of Asian white birch (Betula platyphylla) relative to herbaceous vegetation and identified the environmental conditions under which birch can successfully outcompete non-woody species. Pioneer tree species also produce abundant quantities of seeds capable of dispersing over long distances, facilitating their rapid colonization of disturbed habitats. However, these species are frequently browsed by wild herbivores, meaning that the success of forest regeneration also depends on the intensity of wildlife damage. A comprehensive overview of forest regeneration using pioneer tree species is provided by Souček (2021). In Central Europe, birch, poplar, alder, and rowan are generally regarded as the most suitable pioneer broadleaved species for the reforestation of extensive forest clearings. Leugner et al. (2021) introduced the concept of two-phase forest regeneration, in which pioneer tree species are first used to establish an initial forest stand. During the second phase, these stands are enriched by planting the target tree species intended for the mature forest. Silver birch (Betula pendula), a light-demanding pioneer species, frequently colonizes forest clearings naturally; however, spontaneous regeneration does not always provide a stand structure that is economically acceptable (Martiník, 2014). Martiník et al. (2013) analyzed the actual costs of establishing birch stands through direct sowing together with Norway spruce and European beech and compared them with the costs of conventional artificial regeneration using planted seedlings. The costs associated with birch regeneration included mechanical site preparation to improve seedbed conditions, seed procurement, and direct sowing. The total direct cost of establishing a stand that met the required standards of economic viability, in terms of both tree density and spatial distribution, amounted to USD 1,426 per hectare over the 2-year observation period. Mechanical site preparation represented the largest cost component, accounting for USD 1,140 per ha. By comparison, the establishment of a Norway spruce–European beech stand at the same site during the same period exceeded USD 5,723 per ha, more than four times the cost of birch regeneration. As further reported by Martiník et al. (2018), these differences became even more pronounced as the stands matured and required subsequent silvicultural treatments. While directly sown birch stands achieved the characteristics of planted birch stands within four to 5 years without requiring any additional management, Norway spruce–European beech stands reached a comparable level of stabilization only after approximately 10 years because of their slower growth, by which time cumulative regeneration costs had increased to approximately USD 11,288 per ha. Martiník et al. (2018) further demonstrated that the costs of birch regeneration can be reduced even further through broadcast sowing. This approach reduces both site preparation costs and seed consumption by lowering the seeding rate from approximately 20–40 kg ha-1 to 4–5 kg ha-1. 1.3 Simulation and case studies in forestry The findings presented above highlight the potential of pioneer tree species as an effective solution for the rapid reforestation of forest clearings created by large-scale forest disturbances. However, the relatively infrequent occurrence of catastrophic forest events means that comprehensive datasets suitable for analyzing forest management systems and comparing alternative reforestation strategies are generally unavailable. Consequently, forest management systems must be investigated using simulation techniques based on existing empirical knowledge and generally available data. The application of simulation methods in forestry is well established. Ziesak et al. (2004), for example, examined the use of simulation models in forest operations and concluded that simulation models provide an effective tool for improving estimates of productivity and operational costs. Similarly, Roessiger et al. (2011) evaluated the optimality of clear-cutting and monoculture-based forest management under conditions of risk. Their study applied Monte Carlo simulation and bootstrapping techniques to maximize the Value-at-Risk criterion and demonstrated that a ‘close-to-nature’ management strategy, based on a mixture of Norway spruce and European beech harvested between 50 and 120 years of age, represents a favorable compromise between economic return and production risk. Monte Carlo simulation has also been widely applied to the economic evaluation of forest management systems. Knoke et al. (2005) used this approach to analyze the economics of mixed-species forests, while Moore et al. (2012) employed similar techniques to evaluate the profitability of boreal forest management in Canada. Friedrich et al. (2021) developed a simulation-based forest management framework that explicitly quantifies the uncertainty associated with different stand compositions while simultaneously considering financial returns, carbon sequestration, and forest ecosystem stability when identifying long-term optimal management strategies. Likewise, Yousefpour and Hanewinkel (2009) proposed a simulation-optimization framework for forest conversion planning that integrates timber value, carbon sequestration, and biodiversity indicators. Simulation studies have also been applied to evaluate the environmental consequences of forest management decisions. When analyzing alternative harvesting technologies, Hájek et al. (2024) demonstrated a clear trade-off between economic efficiency and environmental performance: the most cost-effective harvesting methods were simultaneously associated with the greatest ecological impacts. More generally, simulation models have become indispensable tools for investigating forest ecosystem dynamics and forest valuation. Comprehensive reviews of forest resilience modeling have been provided by Albrich et al. (2020), while Shifley et al. (2017) summarized the historical development of forest dynamics models and Maréchaux et al. (2021) discussed their role in advancing forest ecology. As emphasized by Janová et al. (2024), rigorous validation remains an essential prerequisite for the credibility of any simulation model regardless of its intended application. Simulation models are frequently combined with optimization methods to support strategic forest management planning. Nakajima et al. (2016) presented a simulation-based planning framework capable of predicting timber production, forestry profits, and labor requirements while evaluating the consequences of alternative harvesting strategies. Similarly, Janová and Hampel (2016) developed a deterministic optimal control model with an infinite planning horizon to determine the optimal allocation of forest areas among individual tree species. Berrill and Boston (2019) modeled the transition towards the historically observed dominance of coniferous forests in northern California while accounting for both carbon sequestration and timber production. Because comprehensive empirical datasets are often unavailable, simulation techniques may also be employed to generate representative datasets suitable for estimating production, cost, and revenue functions. Hampel and Janová (2014) proposed such a methodology, which has subsequently been applied to analyze the economic impacts of bark beetle outbreaks in the Czech Republic (Hampel and Viskotová, 2020; Janová and Viskotová, 2022). These studies demonstrated that simulation-based approaches can provide valuable insights into long-term economic consequences even when direct observations are limited. Simulation models are commonly complemented by scenario analysis, enabling researchers to assess the implications of alternative future developments. Sullivan et al. (2006), for example, evaluated the financial viability of reforestation on reclaimed mining land by considering low-, medium-, and high-intensity reforestation scenarios under different cost-sharing arrangements between landowners and mining companies. Montigny and MacLean (2006) developed 64 alternative allocation scenarios for a triad forest management system comprising extensively managed forests, intensively managed forests, and protected reserve areas, assessing their effects on both timber and non-timber forest values. Knoke et al. (2021) investigated the economic consequences of natural disturbances in Norway spruce forests using Monte Carlo simulation under current climatic conditions, projected future climates, and scenarios incorporating extreme disturbance events. Wildberg and Möhring (2021) simulated the long-term transformation of a conventionally managed forest enterprise into a system generating stable timber revenues under both perfect and imperfect capital market conditions. Nordkvist et al. (2023) focused on the susceptibility of Norway spruce stands to bark beetle infestation, demonstrating that both the presence of birch and forest soil characteristics substantially influence stand vulnerability. Finally, Chreptun et al. (2023) optimized forest landscape composition by simultaneously considering eight forest types and the ecosystem services they provide. 1.4 Research gap and study objectives The literature reviewed above clearly demonstrates that prolonged forest clearings have adverse ecological consequences, particularly through the progressive degradation of forest soils. Pioneer tree species, including birch, have repeatedly been identified as promising candidates for the rapid reforestation of disturbed areas and may be established not only by planting seedlings but also through direct sowing. Nevertheless, several important research gaps remain. Previous studies have primarily focused on the ecological performance of pioneer species or on the technical and silvicultural aspects of their establishment. Far less attention has been paid to situations in which forest managers must respond to large-scale disturbance events under severe resource constraints. In particular, the existing literature does not adequately address crisis conditions characterized by extensive bark beetle outbreaks, shortages of planting stock, rapidly increasing reforestation costs, and the gradual degradation of soils caused by delayed reforestation. Moreover, although the economics of forest regeneration has been investigated in several contexts, there is currently no comprehensive assessment comparing the long-term economic performance of conventional and alternative emergency reforestation strategies under such disturbance conditions. Because disturbances of this magnitude occur only rarely, the long-term consequences of delayed reforestation cannot yet be evaluated directly using empirical forest management data. The necessary observations will only become available after several decades. Simulation modeling, therefore, represents an appropriate methodology for estimating these long-term impacts. A simulation framework based on historical data makes it possible to incorporate different levels of soil degradation, evaluate alternative reforestation strategies employing different tree species, and quantify their long-term economic consequences. Furthermore, such an approach enables the assessment of whether emergency reforestation strategies can simultaneously improve future forest species composition while maintaining acceptable economic performance. The objective of this study is to evaluate the long-term economic viability of alternative reforestation strategies for areas severely affected by bark beetle outbreaks, with particular emphasis on the use of birch as a pioneer tree species. To achieve this objective, a simulation model is developed to compare the costs and economic benefits of different reforestation strategies under varying levels of soil degradation associated with prolonged forest clearings. The proposed framework enables the evaluation of both conventional and alternative management approaches under conditions representative of large-scale forest disturbance. Unlike previous studies, our approach explicitly integrates ecological processes, post-disturbance resource constraints, and long-term economic evaluation within a single simulation framework, thereby enabling the assessment of emergency reforestation strategies that cannot yet be evaluated empirically. The study addresses the following research questions: RQ1. How do different tree species affect the economic viability of reforestation under varying levels of soil degradation in forest clearings? RQ2. Is the use of birch for the reforestation of forest clearings an economically viable management strategy under post-disturbance conditions? 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Materials description Data were collected on local forest structure, land characteristics, and the techno-economic factors involved in silviculture, thinning, and timber sales. The data were obtained from state bodies (the Czech Statistical Office, the Forest Management Institute of the Czech Republic), the University Forest Enterprise of Mendel University in Brno, and the document ‘Costs of usual measures’ of the Ministry of the Environment of the Czech Republic. The data are primarily presented in unit-cost or revenue tabulations that do not account for potential size-related changes in the stumped area. We are focusing on the most common forest species in the area: spruce (Picea abies), pine (Pinus sylvestris), larch (Larix decidua), beech (Fagus sylvatica), oak (Quercus sp.), and birch (Betula sp.). Timber prices for specific tree species at specified timber qualities were calculated as averages of annual data from 2014 to 2016. Price ranges in the country were maintained until 2016, when the bark beetle infestation began. All cost and revenue data are expressed in 2015 prices. The original local currency, the Czech crown, was converted to US dollars at the 2015 average exchange rate (1 USD = 24.6 CZK) to present results in a global currency. Birch timber prices were reported quarterly until 2009, so data from 2007 to 2009 were used to estimate the variability of prices for particular timbers. Despite expert warnings on the issue of spruce stands, neither the public nor most forest managers perceived spruce planting as a problem at this time. Therefore, this dataset enables us to describe forest management in the absence of bark beetle infestations and the influence of drought. The simulation study focuses on the reforestation of forest clearings across approximately 72,000 ha, comparable to the area of the Drahanská highlands. The Drahanská highlands represent a forested region with natural conditions typical for the European interior mixed forests bioregion. In addition, the University Forest Enterprise of Mendel University in Brno, whose data are used for the simulation, is located on this upland. The general simulation settings and the settings specific to individual replications are described in the corresponding sections below. For a given replication, the simulation runs year by year, and within each year, each forest plot is managed as follows: first, the presence or absence of a forest clearing on a plot is determined. These forest clearings are permitted to persist for a maximum of 2 years. In subsequent years, maintenance activities such as thinning or wildlife-damage treatments may be required, or felling may be necessary. In all scenarios, the associated costs or revenues are recorded. Following felling, reforestation is automatically initiated in later years of the simulation, incurring appropriate expenses. FIGURE 5 Diagram of the simulation process. In this way, we obtain (among other data) the values of revenues Revryp, costs Costryp, and cultivated forest types Ftyperyp for replications r = 1, …, 1,000, years y = 1, …, 160, and plots p = 1, …, 8,000. For each plot, an area Arearp is stored. For specific replications of the simulation r, we can express the development of areas, revenues, and costs for individual forest types (i) as a function of the years. By summing up revenues and costs over the years, we obtain total revenues and costs for forest types in replication r. By averaging across individual replications, we obtain simulation estimates for the costs and revenues of individual forest types. Technically, the simulation uses the structure array data type to represent the forest plots. This array organizes related data into fields. Each forest plot includes fields with single numeric data types (area, terrain type, land quality class, reforestation delay) that remain fixed for a particular replication. It also includes numeric arrays that track the forest plot age and tree species over time. The step of the specific simulation is 1 year. The lengths of these arrays adjust dynamically to match the simulation horizon and the number of replications. The simulation, conducted in MATLAB R2024b, consisted of 1,000 replications. Validation of the simulation procedure was done using face validation (Janová et al., 2024). 2.3 Global settings The whole simulated area is divided into 8,000 relatively homogeneous forest plots. This figure is derived from the number of forest plots of the mentioned University Forest Enterprise (1,076 plots on 9,679 ha). A total of 8 tree species options are considered for the reforestation of individual plots. Table 2 provides an overview of these, the optimal forest age for felling, and the proportions of tree species based on the actual forest composition. Except for forest type (8), forest plots contain a single forest species; however, small plots arranged in strips, for example, can create a form of mixed forest. Forest type (8), “sown birch before beech,” describes a two-phase reforestation strategy in which birch is first established by direct sowing in a forest clearing, followed by the introduction of beech. During the first phase, birch serves as a pioneer and preparatory tree species, rapidly establishing an initial forest stand on the cleared site. Ten years after sowing, the birch stand is partially reduced to create sufficient growing space for the subsequent establishment of beech. Beech seedlings are then planted within the partially reduced birch stand, initiating the second phase of regeneration. The retained birch may facilitate beech establishment and early development by providing partial shade and moderating microclimatic conditions, potentially reducing excessive solar radiation and soil moisture losses. At the time of planting and for the next 5 years of the forest age, the cost of seedling protection against browsing (Supplementary Table S1) must be calculated for types (1) to (6), whereas for type (8), this cost is incurred only after the beech has been planted, i.e., at forest age 11 to 15. The annual cost of weed control (Supplementary Table S1) relates to types (1) – (6) for 5 years after planting. The additional cost is related to thinning (Supplementary Table S1), which is carried out for all tree species at 10 years of age, with a 20% surcharge per type (8). The thinning of beech trees of type (8) is also conducted at 20 years of forest age (i.e., 10 years after the beech tree is planted) at a reduced cost of 50% of the normal cost. The cost overview also includes the cost of harvesting (Supplementary Table S3) at the felling age, which depends on the terrain type of the forest plot, as well as the harvesting technology (tractor or cable car). Note that detailed cost calculations related to cable yarding systems are demonstrated in Søvde (2015). The unit selling prices of timber (listed in Supplementary Table S4) are used to calculate yields in the simulation, which are differentiated by species and timber quality. The volume of timber harvested is also included in the Supplementary (see Supplementary Table S5) and is based on land quality class. The duration of the prediction is set to 160 years, which covers the optimum felling age of all the considered forest species. 2.4 Replication-specific settings For each simulation replication, specific parameters are set by randomizing the collected data. These parameters are as follows: The areas (in hectares) of the plots are generated from a Gamma distribution with parameters 2.543 and 3.537; the mean of the generated plot area is 8.995 ha (the parameters of the distribution are estimated from the areas of the forest plots in the University Forest Enterprise). The terrain type by harvesting is generated randomly based on a ratio of 85:15 for the tractor and cable type. The soil quality levels of individual plots, classified as 1, 2, and 3 in the tables, are simulated using ratios of 70%–90%, 5%–25%, and 2%–7%, respectively. Tree species are assigned to each plot at random according to randomized proportions given in Table 2. A delay of 0, 1, or 2 years in reforestation is also simulated for each forest plot. The reforestation strategy for forest species composition is implemented as follows: due to a shortage of broadleaf seedlings, spruce is used at unchanged proportions for reforestation during the first 2 years of the simulation. In subsequent years, cleared plots are reforested, reducing the total area covered by spruce to a coefficient randomly drawn from the interval (0, 1). The timber prices for particular combinations of forest species and qualities are generated based on mean prices and estimated standard deviations (see above). Schematically, randomized prices can be expressed as the mean price plus the standard deviation multiplied by a random variable governed by the standard normal distribution. 2.5 Cost-benefit analysis The primary measure used to compare the profitability of individual forest types is free cash flow (FCF), defined as the difference between revenues and costs in each year of the evaluation period. FCFs are calculated in real 2015 prices and include all relevant costs associated with establishing, protecting, and cultivating stands, as well as expected revenues from timber harvesting. To illustrate the relationship with the entire area of forest types, total FCF is calculated; subsequent analyses use FCF converted to a per-hectare basis. The economic efficiency of managing individual forest types is assessed using discounted cash flow analysis. For a comprehensive economic evaluation, two standard investment indicators–net present value (NPV) and internal rate of return (IRR) – are derived from the FCFs. NPV represents the present value of all expected net cash flows generated by a reforestation strategy over the planning horizon. It is calculated aswhere denotes the net cash flow in year t, r is the discount rate, and T is the length of the planning horizon. A positive NPV indicates that the discounted revenues generated by the strategy exceed its discounted costs at the selected discount rate. NPV was used as the primary criterion for comparing the economic attractiveness of the alternative reforestation strategies, as it explicitly incorporates both the magnitude and timing of cash flows and allows strategies with different temporal profiles to be compared on a common monetary basis. According to Brukas et al. (2001), two real discount rates are used in NPV calculations: 3%, typical for forest enterprise management, and 1%, used in environmental and social assessments. IRR was used as a complementary profitability indicator. It is defined as the discount rate at which the NPV of a strategy equals zero: IRR expresses the implicit rate of return generated by the investment and provides an intuitive measure of profitability that is independent of the selected discount rate. Its use complements NPV analysis by indicating the return threshold at which a strategy shifts from economically viable to non-viable. However, because forest restoration following a bark beetle calamity is largely a regulatory requirement, and because forestry investments involve long time horizons and potentially irregular cash-flow patterns, IRR was treated as a supplementary indicator. Both indicators are widely used in the economic evaluation of forestry investments and reforestation strategies, particularly where costs and revenues occur over long time horizons. Recent studies have applied discounted economic analysis, including NPV, to compare alternative forest regeneration and plantation strategies and to assess their economic attractiveness (Busch et al., 2024; Pinnschmidt et al., 2023). 3 Results 3.1 Simulated forest composition and stand development Figure 6 presents the initial and final area distribution of forest types in the simulation. The simulation results reveal differences in economic performance across forest types, driven by species-specific costs, benefits, and operational variables. Beech (5) incurs high initial costs because required operations are spread over several years. Although harvesting is assumed to occur uniformly across stands, this simplification still provides relevant insights. Sown birch (7) shows reduced interventions, primarily thinning, which are more demanding and costly than seedling reforestation. Its short harvesting period results in multiple harvests within the simulation timeframe. An increase in the area of both forest types necessitates corresponding reforestation costs. Notably, spruce forests (1) appear highly profitable per hectare due to their valuable timber. However, this does not account for the risks of planting spruce in non-native conditions, particularly in a rapidly changing climate, where increased bark beetle attacks are expected. Given the extensive spruce areas, a financial cushion from spruce-related profits can compensate for insufficient profits from other forest types, thereby promoting forest diversification and preventing delays in reforestation of forest clearings. Sown birch (7) shows relatively high NPV due to lower initial planting costs and shorter harvesting periods (Lidman et al., 2024). Other advantages of sowing over planting relate to root system development, as nursery-raised seedlings often exhibit deformed or shortened roots due to container culture or pruning (Mariotti et al., 2026). Because their root systems remain undisturbed, trees from sowing are more wind-resistant and better tolerate drought, with access to deeper groundwater. Another advantage of sowing is greater genetic diversity and selection: when planting, a limited number of individuals (thousands per hectare) are grown, whereas sowing provides tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of seeds. The high density of seedlings facilitates the natural elimination of weak genetic lines and improves responses to the local microclimate. For details, see, for example, Haupt et al. (2024) and Löf et al. (2019). Sown birch (7) yields a moderate NPV under specific scenarios; however, when sown birch is followed by beech (8), additional costs reduce overall NPV. Larch (3) offers a positive NPV at a 1% discount rate per hectare and, together with stable demand, has the potential to be a lucrative option in forestry management. Overall, the simulation shows that spruce forests have the potential to be highly profitable on a per-hectare basis. The simulation outputs are useful for assessing reforestation strategies for forest clearings; however, to evaluate actual forestry profits, it is necessary to account for cultivation risks for each forest type. This is particularly significant for spruce, despite measures that should partially eliminate risks associated with planting spruce (Fettig et al., 2007). The predominantly negative NPVs for the forest types analyzed suggest that forest restoration after a large-scale disaster may not be financially attractive when evaluated solely on market revenues from timber production. This conclusion, however, aligns with the economic reality of forest management, where reforestation is required by law and where a substantial share of forest benefits takes the form of non-market ecosystem services that conventional financial analyses do not account for. Further, calculated IRRs were generally low, reflecting the long investment horizon and the high costs of reforestation following disturbances. In the case of soil degradation, forest types (5) and (6) exhibit negative IRRs; thus, the cumulative discounted timber revenues are insufficient to cover the costs of reforestation and management even at a zero discount rate. The IRR values are consistent with the NPV calculations and suggest that forest restoration is not primarily an investment choice but rather an obligation. Thus, the forest owner is not faced with the question of “whether it is worthwhile to restore the forest” but rather with “which restoration strategy will meet current environmental requirements while remaining economically acceptable”. 3.3 External validation and consistency with observed regeneration patterns The simulation model was developed for long-term birch reforestation under post-disturbance conditions, for which direct empirical validation through cross-validation is not feasible because real-world outcomes over the simulated time horizon are not yet available. We therefore assessed model validity using several complementary approaches (see Janová et al., 2024), focusing on external validation of model assumptions and short-term model behavior. First, we compared the simulated patterns and outcomes with findings from relevant ecological and forestry studies, particularly regarding observed patterns of birch establishment, growth, and performance under post-disturbance conditions. In particular, we used the studies by Akhtaruzzaman et al. (2018), Iticha et al. (2016), Jonczak et al. (2020), Leugner et al. (2021), and Souček (2021). Second, we conducted rigorous conceptual and technical validation throughout model development to ensure that the model structure, assumptions, and implementation were consistent with established ecological knowledge and the intended modeling purpose. Third, the model underwent expert validation. We discussed its conceptual development and assumptions with forest management experts from the University Forest Enterprise Křtiny, Czech Republic, which manages over 10,000 ha of forest land and has extensive practical experience with sustainable forest management and forest regeneration. Finally, we conducted sensitivity analyses and extreme-condition tests during model development to identify influential assumptions and verify that the model produced plausible responses across a wide range of parameter values and boundary conditions. We also examined actual forest management practices in the Czech Republic in the years surrounding the bark beetle calamity. The feasibility of direct sowing as an alternative reforestation method can be assessed in light of its historical and current use in Czech forestry. According to the Czech Statistical Office, forest managers sowed more intensively during the peak of the bark beetle calamity than in previous years (see Figure 13, left graph). Direct sowing is an established forestry practice, not merely a theoretical alternative. Its marginal current use therefore does not necessarily indicate a lack of technical feasibility but may reflect the predominance of conventional planting under normal management conditions. FIGURE 13 Area of forest clearings artificially reforested by sowing (left graph) and the share of natural regeneration (right graph). Source of data: Czech Statistical Office. Although direct sowing remains a marginal practice in the Czech Republic, the area of natural forest regeneration, i.e., “natural sowing,” has increased significantly since the peak of the bark beetle crisis (Figure 13, right graph). The Czech Statistical Office explicitly links this increase to the need to restore large areas affected by forest disturbances and identifies natural regeneration as a more cost-effective alternative to artificial regeneration. Direct confirmation of the model’s assumptions comes from the growing importance of birch, which accounted for 10.3% of natural regeneration in 2021 but 15.2% in 2024, corresponding to an increase in the area of birch regeneration from approximately 938 ha to 1,888 ha. This trend is consistent with the model’s assumption that pioneer species, particularly birch, can gain importance following large-scale disturbances. Importantly, current forestry research in the Czech Republic has also identified birch and other pioneer species as suitable components of a multi-phase regeneration process (Martiník et al., 2024). These observations offer indirect evidence that the model’s key structural assumptions (including the growing importance of natural regeneration, the role of birch as a pioneer species, and the feasibility of multi-phase regeneration after large-scale disturbances) align with observed trends and current silvicultural practices in the Czech Republic. Taken together, these validation and credibility-assessment procedures indicate that the model is conceptually sound, technically correct, and ecologically plausible. 4 Discussion 4.1 Economic performance of reforestation strategies The differences in NPV and IRR among the analyzed forest types are primarily driven by the interaction between stand establishment costs, rotation length, and timber prices. The favorable economic performance of sown birch (7) can therefore be attributed to very low establishment costs and a relatively short rotation period, which allows revenues to be realized substantially earlier than for the other broadleaved species. This timing effect becomes particularly important at the 3% discount rate. In contrast, the relatively high NPV of the Norway spruce forest type (1) results from both favorable historical timber prices, reflecting strong demand for spruce timber, and the relatively early onset of revenue generation. This result, however, assumes conditions suitable for intensive spruce management, which appears increasingly unrealistic amid ongoing climate change, drought, and bark beetle outbreaks. Recent studies of birch management likewise demonstrate that economic performance depends strongly on rotation length and the timing of silvicultural interventions (Chakravorty et al., 2026). Optimal rotation age also varies substantially depending on the objective of evaluation; in particular, the financially optimal rotation may differ considerably from the optimum for carbon sequestration or broader societal objectives (O’Donoghue et al., 2024). It should be noted that the NPVs estimated in this study do not include administrative costs. This limits the assessment of profitability at the forest-enterprise level but preserves comparability among the individual forest types. The economic results for forest types (6), (7), and (8) are also likely conservative because they exclude non-timber products derived from birch, such as betulin, bark, and twigs, potentially underestimating their economic value. The economic attractiveness of birch may also be affected by future advances in wood-processing technologies. Recent research demonstrates the potential to increase the value of birch wood through densification, chemical modification, and resin impregnation. For example, densification of birch veneers has been shown to improve their suitability for high-performance engineered wood products, while combinations of delignification, densification, and resin impregnation can substantially improve the mechanical properties and dimensional stability of birch laminates (Akkurt et al., 2025; Jakob et al., 2025). Such technological developments may increase the value of birch timber and broaden its potential markets; however, these technological and market developments remain uncertain and were therefore not incorporated into the simulation. 4.2 Economic–environmental trade-offs The economic assessment of the simulated forest types should not be interpreted as a direct ranking of their overall suitability for forest management. Norway spruce provides the highest financial return under the historical price conditions used in the model, but this advantage is associated with substantial exposure to climatic and biotic risks. Birch, in contrast, generates lower timber revenues but contributes to rapid site colonization, forest diversification, and ecological stabilization. This illustrates an important trade-off between short- to medium-term financial performance and the provision of resilience and ecosystem benefits. Recent studies likewise indicate that species diversification can reduce susceptibility to bark beetle disturbance and increase forest resilience, although the economic benefits of diversification depend strongly on disturbance intensity and management objectives (López-Andújar Fustel et al., 2024; Fuchs et al., 2024; Pickering et al., 2025). An important implication is that the financial indicators used in this study do not capture several ecological benefits of birch. Rapid occupation of forest clearings may reduce the duration of exposed soil conditions and thereby limit erosion and further soil degradation, while the establishment of structurally diverse stands may contribute to greater resilience to future disturbances. Consequently, the NPV ranking should be regarded as a comparison of timber-based economic performance, rather than as a comprehensive assessment of the total social value of the alternative reforestation strategies. This distinction is particularly important for post-disturbance restoration, where reforestation provides ecosystem services that extend beyond market-based timber revenues. 4.3 Birch as a pioneer and transitional species The ecological role of birch extends beyond its function as a low-cost reforestation option. As a pioneer and nurse species, birch can form an initial canopy that modifies light availability and site microclimate. This may facilitate the subsequent establishment of target species that are more shade-tolerant or otherwise more demanding. This mechanism is particularly relevant to the two-phase strategy considered in the present study, in which birch is first sown, partially thinned after 10 years, and subsequently complemented by beech. Recent forestry research emphasizes that birch can function as a nurse stand and that thinning can regulate the availability of light, water, and nutrients for subsequent regeneration (Martiník and Matoušková, 2025). Retaining part of the birch stand after beech establishment may therefore provide both ecological continuity and a gradual transition toward a more diverse forest stand rather than simply replacing one species with another. The two-phase reforestation strategy consequently has a silvicultural rationale in addition to its economic advantages. The transitional role of birch also provides an opportunity to avoid prolonged reliance on a single pioneer species. In the strategy considered here, birch is established rapidly following disturbance, while the subsequent partial reduction of the birch stand creates growing space for beech. At the same time, the retained birch can remain part of the developing stand and contribute to its structural diversity. Such an approach may be particularly valuable under post-disturbance conditions in which immediate establishment of the desired final species is constrained by limited planting material or other resources. 4.4 Uncertainty and future disturbance risks The use of 2015 price data reflects conditions prior to the recent drought and bark beetle outbreaks, when Norway spruce was highly profitable (Janová and Hampel, 2016; Hampel and Viskotová, 2020). The risks associated with spruce planting are only implicitly mitigated in the model through a gradual reduction in the area of spruce stands. The model also does not explicitly represent early plantation failures or direct climate change effects, because sufficiently detailed empirical data describing these processes are not yet available. Although the effects of delayed reforestation on soil productivity are represented in the model through yield-reduction scenarios, the underlying soil degradation processes are not modeled dynamically. Furthermore, the model does not explicitly represent multiple species within individual stands, except for forest type (8), although mixed-forest dynamics are approximated through small spatial plots with different species compositions. The economic evaluation of the simulated forest types should therefore be interpreted in the context of substantial uncertainty regarding future disturbance regimes and timber markets. Recent European evidence indicates that natural disturbances already impose substantial economic costs on timber-oriented forestry and that these costs may increase markedly under climate change, with Central Europe identified as a major hotspot (Mohr et al., 2025). At the stand level, Farahani et al. (2026) further demonstrated that explicitly incorporating extreme disturbance events into forest-management simulations can substantially reduce NPV, particularly for Norway spruce. The high NPV of spruce obtained in the present study should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of its unconditional long-term economic superiority. Future timber prices represent an additional source of uncertainty. Econometric evidence from five European countries, including Czechia, indicates that disturbance-induced supply shocks can have contrasting effects across timber assortments: sawlog prices may rise because of shortages of high-quality timber, whereas pulpwood prices tend to decline following bark beetle infestations and windthrow (Asada et al., 2023). These asymmetric market responses suggest that future disturbance regimes may alter both the biological productivity and the economic value of individual tree species. The relative attractiveness of birch may therefore increase not only because of its ecological suitability for post-disturbance regeneration but also if future developments in timber markets and wood processing increase the value of birch timber. Although birch is generally considered a relatively drought-tolerant pioneer species, its performance depends strongly on site and stand conditions. European evidence indicates that silver birch exhibits relatively high drought tolerance compared with several late-successional species, but prolonged drought may nevertheless reduce growth and induce mortality under highly exposed or water-limited conditions (Schwenke et al., 2024; Kharuk et al., 2013). Birch mortality has been observed particularly at stand margins exposed to high solar radiation and low soil moisture, suggesting that stand structure may be important for protection under drought stress. Appropriate stand density and thinning may therefore be critical for balancing competition for water against the benefits of canopy-mediated microclimatic regulation. Warming, particularly drought, may also challenge larch and beech. Recent research indicates that thinning can improve the drought resistance of released birch trees while also influencing their wind stability and the conditions for subsequent regeneration (Martiník and Matoušková, 2025). Birch suitability also depends on maintaining an appropriate rotation length. The probability of wind damage increases with birch stand age and structural characteristics such as basal area and slenderness ratio (Kitenberga et al., 2021), while age-related decline, wood discoloration, and decay become increasingly important after approximately 60–70 years (Dubois et al., 2020). Birch is therefore well suited to the rapid reforestation of forest clearings, but its advantages should be utilized during a relatively short and appropriately managed growth phase. Appropriate stand density, timely reduction, and a suitable final rotation age are therefore essential components of a birch-based reforestation strategy. 4.5 Practical implications and limitations The simulation results reflect the economic and environmental conditions of the Czech Republic. Because Czechia is an open economy within the European interior mixed forest bioregion, the findings may generalize to many European countries, except southern regions where birch occurs primarily as a mountain relict rather than as a commercially managed species (Dyderski et al., 2018). Globally, the methodological framework provides an adaptable approach to designing simulation studies and organizing the required input data. Moreover, the use of naturally regenerated or sowed birch to restore damaged stands is consistent with actual forestry practices observed, for example, in Sweden following major windstorms (Lodin et al., 2017). Spatial timber-processing capacity and timber demand also influence the suitability of individual tree species. Although demand is partly reflected in timber prices, changes in the structure of cultivated forests may create incentives for wood processors to adapt their technologies, particularly by expanding the use of hardwood from deciduous species. Recent technological developments in the processing of birch wood further suggest that its future value may extend beyond conventional timber products. Nevertheless, such developments are uncertain and were not included in the present model. Reforestation success is also shaped by ecological and socioeconomic factors. Overabundant game populations represent a major threat, with wild boar and other ungulates causing substantial damage to seedlings (Skoták et al., 2021), thereby increasing protection costs and potentially limiting the expansion of broadleaved stands (Janová et al., 2022). In contrast, birch exhibits relatively high tolerance to browsing (Dubois et al., 2020) and establishes effectively through natural regeneration or high-density sowing (Martiník, 2014), making it particularly suitable for extensive disturbed areas. Finally, labor shortages, financial pressures associated with reduced timber revenues, and changes in forest ownership underscore the need for targeted public support and technological advances to facilitate post-disturbance restoration. While mechanization in forest harvesting can have negative effects on soil conditions (Parker et al., 2016), technological advances in nursery production, including automated grading systems based on computer vision and artificial intelligence, may offer opportunities to reduce labor requirements and improve operational efficiency. 5 Conclusion Taken together, birch deserves particular attention when planning climate-resilient reforestation strategies. As a pioneer species with rapid early growth, high ecological plasticity, good performance on degraded soils, and relatively low sensitivity to browsing, birch represents a practical and resilient option for quickly stabilizing disturbed sites. The results discussed above provide sufficient evidence to answer the research questions formulated in the Introduction. RQ1. How do different tree species affect the economic viability of reforestation under varying levels of soil degradation in forest clearings? The simulation results demonstrate that the economic performance of reforestation strategies varies substantially across tree species and is strongly influenced by soil degradation resulting from delayed reforestation. Norway spruce provides the highest expected financial returns under historical production and price conditions; however, this advantage comes with considerably higher ecological and production risks. Broadleaved species generally generate lower revenues but also require lower establishment costs and exhibit greater resilience under disturbed site conditions. Birch, in particular, performs favorably because of its low establishment costs, rapid early development, and tolerance of degraded soils. The analysis therefore indicates that increasing soil degradation shifts the relative economic attractiveness from conventional spruce-based regeneration towards pioneer-based regeneration strategies. RQ2. Is the use of birch for the reforestation of forest clearings an economically viable management strategy under post-disturbance conditions? The results provide a positive answer to this research question. Birch represents an economically viable emergency reforestation strategy, particularly when extensive forest disturbances are accompanied by shortages of seedlings, labor, and financial resources. Direct sowing of birch achieved the most favorable economic performance among the broadleaved alternatives by substantially reducing establishment costs while simultaneously limiting further soil degradation through rapid site occupation. Furthermore, the two-phase regeneration strategy, in which birch serves as an initial pioneer species before conversion to beech, approached economic break-even while supporting long-term forest diversification. These findings suggest that birch should be regarded not merely as a temporary pioneer species but as a strategic component of climate-resilient forest management under increasing disturbance pressure. The presented simulation model of reforestation in forest clearings shows good agreement with forest management practices. Although the model is based on detailed data from the University Forest Enterprise of Mendel University in Brno–reflecting conditions typical of Western Eurasia–the general approach and many of the findings are broadly applicable to reforestation contexts worldwide. Thus, forest managers should consider immediate reforestation with birch to prevent soil degradation and to capitalize on the low cost of initial stand establishment, with the option to introduce more economically viable species later to enhance long-term profitability. Future research should incorporate dynamic price developments, including volatility driven by disturbance events, to more precisely evaluate the economic risks associated with particular species such as spruce. Further work should also explore mixed-species dynamics, the long-term ecological benefits of pioneer-based regeneration pathways, and the role of birch within adaptive silvicultural strategies for rapidly changing European forest ecosystems.
