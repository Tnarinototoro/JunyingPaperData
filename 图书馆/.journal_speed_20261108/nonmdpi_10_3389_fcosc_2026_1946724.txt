Department of Livestock, Wildlife and Fisheries, Gary Magadzire School of Agriculture and Engineering, Great Zimbabwe University, Masvingo, Zimbabwe See more Article metrics View details Abstract Southern Africa holds an irreplaceable stock of locally adapted livestock breeds that persist mainly within smallholder and communal production systems. These breeds are losing genetic ground because of uncontrolled crossbreeding, disrupted land tenure, climate driven destocking, and decades of livestock policy that rewarded commercial output over the conservation of indigenous breeds. This structured narrative review, which incorporates systematic elements in its search and screening but does not follow the full protocol of a systematic or scoping review, examines how indigenous knowledge systems (IKSs) may function as a conservation mechanism for animal genetic resources (AnGR) across the region, drawing on 62 peer-reviewed and grey-literature sources published between 1995 and 2026 and retained in the final thematic synthesis. Findings synthesized across six thematic domains suggest that IKS-associated practices are linked to several dimensions of in situ conservation, although the underlying evidence is predominantly cross-sectional, observational, and ethnographic, and does not permit strong causal inference. Phenotype-based selection criteria appear, in the studies reviewed, to be associated with the persistence of some adaptive traits, though the genetic mechanisms underlying these associations are largely unconfirmed. Communal institutions such as livestock loaning (mafisa) and rotational bull-sharing are reported, mainly on ethnographic grounds, to regulate gene flow and limit inbreeding in the absence of any pedigree recording system. Customary taboos, socio-cultural governance structures, and oversight by elder councils are documented as helping to anchor indigenous genotypes within community gene pools across generations. Molecular studies of populations managed under IKS report heterozygosity, allelic richness, and between-breed differentiation that are consistent with, though not proof of effective community-level genetic management; the available evidence indicates an association between IKS-informed management and conservation-relevant genetic patterns rather than a demonstrated causal relationship. Significant gaps remain, particularly the limited genomic validation of IKS selection claims, the systematic under-documentation of women’s knowledge in small-stock management, and the near-total absence of longitudinal research linking IKS practice to genetic outcomes over time; closing these gaps is identified as the priority for establishing whether the associations described here reflect genuine conservation mechanisms. The review closes with policy-relevant recommendations for further evaluating, and where warranted embedding, IKS within national AnGR strategies and SADC livestock frameworks. 1 Introduction Southern Africa, understood here as the SADC region, is home to more than 70% of the continent’s locally adapted cattle, goat, and sheep breeds (FAO DAD-IS, 2024). Smallholder systems carry 65 to 90% of the region’s cattle herd, dominated by Sanga type breeds (Nguni, Tuli, Barotse, Tswana, Tonga, Mashona) and zebu types such as the Malawi Zebu and Angoni (Nyamushamba et al., 2016). Generations of exposure to local environmental pressures have given these animals notable resilience, including tolerance to heat, drought, and diseases such as trypanosomiasis, which makes them indispensable for maintaining food production in resource constrained, climate exposed settings (Oke et al., 2025). The region’s animal genetic resources (AnGR) face growing pressure. Indiscriminate crossbreeding with exotic stock, recurrent drought, changing land use, and policy environments biased towards commercial production have combined to produce measurable genetic erosion (Nyamushamba et al., 2016; Russo et al., 2019). An estimated 23% of SADC’s indigenous breeds are classified as at risk of extinction, and breeds such as the Hottentot, White Woolled Tswana, and Namaqua Afrikaner sheep have already disappeared entirely (Ngcobo et al., 2025). Each such loss removes not just a set of physical characteristics but an entire package of locally evolved adaptations to disease pressure, forage scarcity, and climatic extremes that took many generations of selection, both natural and human-guided, to accumulate, and that cannot be recovered once the underlying breeding population is gone. In response, international instruments including the FAO Global Plan of Action for Animal Genetic Resources and the Nagoya Protocol call for conservation approaches that recognise and incorporate the knowledge held by indigenous and local communities (Beato and Veneroso, 2023; Martyniuk and Haska, 2021). Indigenous Knowledge Systems (IKS) are widely regarded as central to in situ conservation efforts throughout the region, though, as this review will show, the strength of that role is more often asserted than demonstrated with genetic evidence. They constitute living, culturally embedded bodies of knowledge, skill, and practice built up through communities’ sustained interaction with their environment (Phaahla et al., 2025; Zidny and Eilks, 2020). This knowledge spans breeding and mating decisions, ethnoveterinary care, management of rangelands and water resources, and the cultural meaning attached to breed traits for social, ceremonial, and economic purposes (Assan, 2025; Malcom, 2024). Much of it captures livestock phenotypes long before these were formally described by science, yet its relationship to formal AnGR conservation programmes remains poorly understood and contested. IKS is frequently marginalized within policy spaces dominated by Western scientific paradigms, leaving it largely absents from national breed improvement strategies (Ncube et al., 2025; Radcliffe et al., 2021). This review maps the range and diversity of documented IKS practices; critically assesses the strength and type of evidence for their contribution to breed conservation outcomes; identifies the structural, institutional, and epistemological barriers to IKS integration; and sets out a research and policy agenda for strengthening the evidence base under conditions of intensifying climate change and genetic erosion. In doing so, it treats IKS not as a residual or transitional stage on the way to formal conservation science, but as an evolving, internally coherent management system whose conservation value merits rigorous, rather than assumed, investigation. 2 Materials and methods This study adopts a structured narrative literature review incorporating systematic search elements, following the methodological orientation set out by Snyder (2019) and the reporting principles articulated by Page et al. (2021). We use this designation deliberately rather than describing the work as a systematic or scoping review: systematic elements were applied to the search, screening, and extraction stages to improve transparency and reduce selection bias, but the review does not undertake formal risk-of-bias appraisal across all included study designs, protocol pre-registration, or quantitative effect synthesis, and its thematic organization is closer to interpretive synthesis than to the concept-mapping approach of a scoping review. A narrative approach with systematic elements was chosen for three interlinked reasons: the IKS evidence base is methodologically heterogeneous, spanning ethnographic fieldwork, participatory rural appraisal (PRA), genetic characterization studies, policy analyses, and grey literature, which rules out meaningful statistical pooling; much of the relevant knowledge is locally produced and circulates through non-indexed channels that require deliberate capture; and quantitative genetic diversity metrics and qualitative IKS documentation are not directly commensurable, calling for interpretive rather than aggregative synthesis. Rather than forcing these disparate evidence types into a single pooled effect size, the review instead traces convergences and contradictions across studies, allowing genomic findings, ethnographic accounts, and policy documents to inform one another whilst preserving the methodological integrity of each and whilst distinguishing, throughout, between documented association and demonstrated causal effect. 2.1 Search strategy A wide-ranging, multi-source search was carried out to capture both formal peer-reviewed scholarship and grey literature generated by intergovernmental, governmental, and civil society actors working in Southern African livestock and biodiversity governance. Peer-reviewed sources were drawn from Scopus, AJOL (African Journals Online), SABINET, and Google Scholar; grey literature was sourced from FAO, AU-IBAR, ILRI, SADC Secretariat documents, national ministry reports, and dissertation repositories (DART-Europe, EThOS, ProQuest, SEALS). Search strings followed a three-part Population–Concept–Context (PCC) structure combining population terms (indigenous livestock breeds and animal genetic resources), concept terms (indigenous and traditional knowledge systems and their documented practices), and context terms (Southern Africa/SADC member states), linked through Boolean AND/OR operators applied to controlled vocabulary and free-text terms. The PCC framework was adopted in place of a PICO structure because this review addresses a broad and heterogeneous concept space IKS practices, cultural governance, and community institutions rather than a discrete intervention with a defined comparator and measurable outcome, for which PICO is more naturally suited. The core search window spanned 1995–2026, capturing the post-colonial policy shift triggered by the Convention on Biological Diversity together with the major SADC land reform era, whilst the 2026 upper bound was set to capture emerging scholarship on climate-adaptive IKS and the implications of the post-2020 Global Biodiversity Framework for Southern African AnGR. Studies qualified for inclusion if they concerned SADC member states (Angola, Botswana, Comoros, DRC, Eswatini, Lesotho, Madagascar, Malawi, Mauritius, Mozambique, Namibia, Seychelles, South Africa, Tanzania, Zambia, Zimbabwe), were published between 1995 and 2026, dealt with indigenous or locally adapted livestock species (cattle, goats, sheep, chickens, pigs, donkeys), contained an explicit IKS or traditional knowledge component, and reported conservation-relevant outcomes such as breed diversity, genetic erosion, or policy integration. Studies were excluded where they focused solely on regions outside SADC, addressed exotic breeds without any IKS dimension, or amounted to opinion pieces lacking an empirical basis. Coverage extended to English, Portuguese, and French sources with accessible English abstracts. A small number of examples from outside the SADC region (East Africa, West Africa) appear later in this review for illustrative, comparative purposes only; these did not form part of the 62-source evidence base used for thematic synthesis and are explicitly flagged as comparative material wherever they are used. 2.3 Study selection and screening All records retrieved were imported into Rayyan QCRI (Ouzzani et al., 2016) for duplicate removal and blind screening, proceeding through two sequential stages. In the first stage, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against the inclusion/exclusion criteria, coding each record as Include, Exclude, or Uncertain; uncertain records moved forward to full-text review, and disagreements were settled through discussion and, where necessary, arbitration by a third reviewer. In the second stage, the same two reviewers independently assessed the full text of all included and uncertain records using a standardized exclusion coding scheme. Inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s κ) was computed at each stage against a target of κ ≥ 0.80. Of an initial pool of 4,850 database records plus 320 identified through citation chaining, 3,260 records remained after duplicate removal and were screened, of which 180 proceeded to full-text assessment. After excluding records lacking an explicit IKS component or Southern African scope, 62 sources entered the final thematic synthesis, drawing on both peer-reviewed and grey literature; this is the figure reported consistently throughout this manuscript, including the Abstract. 2.4 Data extraction A standardized data extraction form, built in Microsoft Excel, was piloted on ten randomly chosen studies before being applied across the full sample. It captured bibliographic and contextual details (authorship, year, title, source, country, study design, species, breed); IKS documentation (type of IKS, knowledge holders, evidence of erosion, documentation method); conservation outcomes (breed status against FAO DAD-IS criteria and genetic diversity metrics where reported); and policy and governance dimensions (frameworks cited, access and benefit sharing mechanisms documented, involvement of community-based organizations). For each source, reviewers additionally classified the evidence type as direct genetic/genomic, indirect population level empirical, qualitative/ethnographic, or policy/contextual, and assigned a qualitative confidence rating (low, moderate, high) reflecting study design, sample size, and whether findings had been corroborated elsewhere. Two reviewers carried out extraction, resolving discrepancies by consensus. Findings were then organised thematically across six analytical domains: IKS-based mate selection; community breeding practices; ethnoveterinary medicine; rangeland and water management; socio-cultural and governance systems; and the interaction of IKS with formal conservation frameworks. 2.5 Evidence-quality assessment Because the 62 included sources span methodologically incommensurable evidence types ethnographic and PRA studies, population-genetic and genomic analyses, policy documents, and grey literature these are not treated as interchangeable support for conservation claims. IKS supplies the criteria for mate choice, the strategies used to manage herds, the cultural taboos that constrain certain practices, and the communal institutions that together shape the genetic composition and population structure of local breeds (Nephawe, 2023). Because these criteria are transmitted orally and applied consistently across households and generations, they function less like isolated preferences and more like an informal, decentralized selection programme operating in parallel to any formal breeding scheme. Throughout this review, we distinguish three related but non-equivalent dimensions of conservation. Phenotypic conservation refers to the maintenance of recognizable breed characteristics such as coat colour, conformation, or horn status. Genetic conservation refers to the maintenance of genome-wide measures such as allelic richness, heterozygosity, effective population size, and population structure. Adaptive genetic conservation refers more narrowly to the maintenance of genetic variants underlying functional traits such as disease resistance, heat tolerance, or feed efficiency. Evidence that an IKS practice preserves a phenotype does not, by itself, establish that the practice conserves genome-wide genetic diversity, since selection for a specific visible trait can, in principle, narrow variation elsewhere in the genome even whilst maintaining the trait itself. Where the sections below describe IKS practices as contributing to “conservation,” the specific dimension intended, and the type of evidence available for it, is indicated. 3.1 IKS-based mate selection criteria Coat colour is one of the most well-documented IKS-derived selection criteria. Certain colour patterns, particularly ibona (spotted, multicoloured animals), are meaningful to Nguni cattle keepers in South Africa and Eswatini during ritual sacrifice and lobola talks (Glover, 2025). Herders use knowledge passed down through the generations to link phenotypes with temperament, fertility, and heat tolerance, illustrating the cultural weight of colour beyond appearance (Fre, 2018). This cultural preference for specific colours documented amongst the communities studied is associated with the persistence of colour polymorphism at the phenotypic level; whether it also safeguards allelic variation at pigmentation loci or nearby genomic regions is plausible but, in the absence of genomic evidence on linkage disequilibrium or hitchhiking effects at these loci, remains to be established rather than demonstrated. Identifying drought-tolerant animals is crucial for herd survival in drought-prone pastoral settings. A compact frame, a noticeable dewlap, small hooves, and the endurance required to travel great distances between grazing and water points are characteristics that Nguni keepers, in the case studies reviewed, use to identify such animals. Although these characteristics are expressed in local idiom, they closely resemble formal quantitative selection indices (Raphalalani et al., 2025). Among Tswana herders documented in the literature, a preferred breeding bull is described as “one who walks well and eats little,” a description capturing a preference for minimal maintenance energy requirements; this remains a phenotypic selection criterion whose downstream genetic consequences have not been separately measured. Maternal performance, which includes milk output, calf-rearing behaviour, and pelvic conformation, is documented as a key IKS breeding goal amongst the communities studied. Nguni women are reported to play an important role in assessing breeding females based on udder shape, milk let-down behaviour, and calf development as a proxy indicator, consistent with the larger but often underestimated role women play in livestock breeding choices throughout the region (Galiè et al., 2025). These maternal-line criteria are seldom documented in the literature, but where they are, they appear to be applied consistently, suggesting a community-validated norm rather than idiosyncratic personal taste; they plausibly influence which females are retained for future breeding, though this link has not been independently verified with pedigree or genomic data. 3.2 Community breeding practices The mafisa system of livestock loaning, practised in Botswana and Zimbabwe, functions as a documented communal institution with plausible consequences for AnGR management (Resane, 2025). Under this arrangement, better-resourced patron places cattle with a poorer household, which in turn gains access to milk, draught power, and manure, whilst the patron retains breeding rights over any offspring. In genetic terms, mafisa is reported to disperse breeding animals across many households, which could in principle spread genetic material community-wide and reduce the risk that disease or drought eliminates a concentrated gene pool; because this claim rests on ethnographic description of the institution rather than direct genetic monitoring, it should be read as a plausible hypothesis rather than a demonstrated outcome. Because loaned animals are dispersed across geographically separated homesteads experiencing somewhat different disease exposure, rainfall, and grazing conditions, the arrangement is also hypothesised to function as a risk-buffering mechanism, although this too has not been tested with longitudinal or genetic data. Within Zimbabwe’s communal areas, this practice is described as transferring genes between villages, with an inferred (not directly measured) contribution to effective population size amongst Mashona cattle herds (Mapiye et al., 2019). A related practice, bull rotation, moves breeding males between family groups either by custom or deliberate arrangement, and is documented across communal systems in Zimbabwe, Namibia, and Botswana. Among Ovambo agro pastoralists in northern Namibia, elders are reported to have traditionally organised annual bull rotations so that no single sire could dominate the community herd for more than one breeding season. In Matabeleland, Zimbabwe, reciprocal bull-borrowing agreements are reported to have a comparable effect; the available evidence suggests these practices may reduce inbreeding amongst village herds in the absence of formal pedigree records, though, as with mafisa, this pathway from custom to measured genetic outcome has not yet been tested with longitudinal genetic data (Strydom et al., 2020). Another conservation-relevant practice documented within IKS is a cultural taboo on selling breeding females. Parting with a proven breeding cow is reported to be frowned upon amongst Tonga villages in the Zambezi Valley (Bwalya et al., 2024). In Shona society, cattle management is organised around the concept of pfuma, or livestock as a form of social wealth, which places breeding females within a web of obligations passed down through generations; certain cows are designated mombe yeumai (cattle of motherhood), with their offspring reserved for ceremonial rather than commercial purposes, according to the case studies reviewed. Cattle inheritance is matrilineal amongst the Ovambo pastoralists of Namibia documented by John and Mbenzi (2025); breeding females pass down the maternal line, and selling one requires the approval of maternal relations, an institutional constraint that plausibly helps retain breeding stock within the group, though again this is documented ethnographically rather than through population-genetic monitoring. 4 IKS in health and disease management When synthetic pharmaceuticals and veterinary services are rare or prohibitively expensive, community farmers turn to locally accessible plant-based therapies to treat tick-borne disease, internal parasites, and wounds (Jaime et al., 2022). Ethnoveterinary medicine, which applies traditional beliefs, skills, and herbal therapies to animal health, is documented as widespread because it is less expensive, more accessible, and reported by users to be effective (Gobvu et al., 2023; Wendimu et al., 2024). This expertise is sometimes the only health intervention accessible to herders in geographically isolated wards, where the nearest state veterinary station may be a full day’s drive away, making ethnoveterinary competency a practical necessity rather than a cultural preference alone. A small number of the plants most commonly cited in the reviewed literature including Aloe species, Nicotiana tabacum, Cissus quadrangularis, Sclerocarya birrea, and Kigelia africana are used against ectoparasites, gut worms, ticks, septic wounds, and postpartum uterine conditions, and at least two (Aloe marlothii and Kigelia africana) have documented laboratory evidence of anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, or wound-healing activity supporting their traditional use (Nabatanzi et al., 2020; Phaahla et al., 2025). Rather than cataloguing each remedy in detail, this section focuses on the conservation-relevant pathway these practices are hypothesized to support: ethnoveterinary care (direct evidence: documented use and, for some plants, laboratory bioactivity) leads to reduced mortality and improved herd health (indirect evidence: user-reported outcomes, limited controlled data) which supports the maintenance of breeding populations (evidence largely lacking at the population level) and, in turn, the persistence of locally adapted breeds (evidence lacking) with a potential contribution to AnGR conservation (evidence lacking). Each link in this pathway currently rests on progressively weaker evidence, and the manuscript does not claim that ethnoveterinary practice has been shown to conserve genetic diversity, only that it plausibly supports the survival of the animals in which that diversity resides. This knowledge base faces genuine threats: deforestation and urban expansion are reducing access to medicinal plants, much of the underlying pharmacology remains unknown, and the informal, largely oral nature of the practice necessitates concerted documentation and the establishment of ethnobotanical gardens (Marandure, 2016). Systematic pharmacological screening of the plants most cited by herders would help distinguish remedies with genuine bioactive value from those whose apparent effectiveness reflects nursing care or the natural resolution of self-limiting conditions, strengthening the evidence base without dismissing practices that have yet to be tested. 5 IKS in rangeland and water management Rangeland-focused IKS practices documented within SADC are based on herd mobility, seasonal grazing-land rotation, and community-level management of site and water resources (Kisiaya, 2016; Saruni, 2018). These techniques are reported to create diverse landscapes, support both livestock and wildlife, and interact with state-led conservation programmes. Grazing pressure is dispersed unevenly across a mosaic of managed patches rather than concentrated on a single fixed pasture, which is hypothesised to reduce density-dependent stress associated with land degradation and disease transmission within a herd, although this remains an inference from management logic rather than a directly measured outcome. Herds are divided according to age, sex, and health condition, which is reported to lower competition for food and improve grazing efficiency, whilst traditional leaders monitor seasonal rest periods and manage pasture access to limit overgrazing. Although this review’s evidence base is limited to SADC member states, two additional examples are included here for comparative context only and did not form part of the 62-source evidence synthesis. Among Maasai pastoralists in East Africa, the olokeri system allows elder councils to temporarily close pastures for regeneration, guided by their reading of environmental conditions; a structurally similar age-set system is documented within the SADC region amongst pastoralist groups, underpinning seasonal movement of livestock between dry lowland and highland areas to secure access to better forage and water (Moti et al., 2026). In Sierra Leone, outside SADC, comparable outcomes are reported to be achieved through rotational grazing combined with crop-livestock integration (Oladele and Amara, 2024); this is presented as illustrative of a broader institutional pattern rather than as part of the Southern African evidence base. Within the SADC region, traditional weather forecasting, drawing on plant cycles, animal behaviour, and atmospheric cues, is documented as helping communities respond to shifting conditions and informing decisions on planting times and pest control. Techniques such as ponds, sand dams, and traditional irrigation help capture rainwater for household, crop, and livestock use (Behailu et al., 2016). Sacred locations, such as pools, hills, and woods, have traditionally governed water usage and cleanliness, but many of these governance systems have weakened under colonial and postcolonial policies that did not recognise their validity (Mugambiwa and Makhubele, 2021). Socio-Cultural and Governance Systems. IKS extends beyond herd management to influence the broader social and institutional environment in which animals are owned, traded, inherited, used ritually, and socially regulated (Nephawe, 2023). Customary norms, kinship-based inheritance, ceremonial responsibility, and communal sanction all influence, in the communities documented, which animal features persist and which breeding options are considered acceptable. These institutions typically operate without a written statute, but their sanctions, ranging from social disapproval to exclusion from ceremonial participation, are reported to be consistently enforced at the village level, giving them documented influence over which genetics circulate within a community; the genome wide consequences of that influence, as distinct from its effect on visible phenotypes, have generally not been measured. Bridewealth rituals (lobola, roora, and lobolo) are documented as a means of preserving preferred breed characteristics in the region. Among the Ngoni of eastern Zambia and northern Malawi, bridewealth is reported to have historically specified cattle with Angoni breed conformation, a phenotypic requirement that plausibly encouraged households to keep good indigenous breeding stock rather than sell it for fast cash (Elvis and Hummerly, 2025; Mapiye et al., 2019). In Zambia’s Eastern Province, the chuma system links family status to herd quality, and is reported to encourage households to retain breed-typical features rather than replace indigenous animals with exotic crossbreds within bridewealth herds (Bwalya et al., 2024). Inheritance practices are reported to reinforce this pattern: amongst the matrilineal Chewa of Malawi and Zambia, breeding females are said to pass along maternal lines, limiting the transfer of genetic material out of the clan and plausibly contributing to the persistence of breed-typical phenotypes, though the effect on allele frequencies specifically has not been genetically measured (Bwalya et al., 2024). Among the patrilineal Tumbuka and Ngoni, inherited bulls are expected to be of recognised indigenous type, a documented norm that creates generational selection pressure towards breed-typical phenotype; replacing an inherited bull with an exotic sire without elder approval is considered a serious breach of custom (Morudu and Maimela, 2021). Ritual requirements for specific animal types are also reported to have conservation-relevant effects the phenotypic level. Shona, Karanga, Chewa, and Tonga communities in Zimbabwe, Malawi, and Zambia are documented as favouring uniformly black goats for rainmaking ceremonies (mukwerera, mutoro, mafuwe); this preference maintains a visible phenotype at coat-colour loci such as ASIP and MC1R, and may be associated with variation at genes influencing stress-related physiology, although this specific link has not been tested in the populations concerned (Kırıkçı et al., 2016; Jacobs et al., 2016). Hornless black cattle are reported to be especially sought for sacrificial rites among Zimbabwe’s Tonga people, a preference associated with the persistence of this rare polled characteristic amongst local herds (Ncube et al., 2015). White or light grey cattle carry elevated ritual status in Nguni, Sotho-Tswana, and Lozi communities, where they are associated with ancestral purity and royal authority; social prohibitions on selling white breeding animals are reported to maintain a subpopulation of white-phenotype Nguni cattle, which represents phenotypic rather than necessarily broader genetic conservation at coat-colour loci (Motsamayi, 2020). Elder councils, clan-based livestock custodianship, and community assemblies such as the Tswana kgotla are documented as having traditionally regulated breeding decisions, controlled the introduction of new bulls, and adjudicated disputes arising from unauthorised crossbreeding, functions that parallel, at an institutional level, those performed by breed societies and community-based breeding programmes elsewhere (Morudu and Maimela, 2021). Interaction of IKS with Formal AnGR Conservation. The relationship between IKS-based conservation and formally constituted state programmes is dynamic, contested, and shaped by uneven institutional power and epistemic recognition. Where formal and indigenous systems align, meaningful conservation synergies appear possible; where they conflict, indigenous practices are reported to deteriorate and genetic erosion to accelerate; and where policy remains silent on IKS, a de facto vacuum leaf communal AnGR exposed to unmanaged loss. The two cases that follow illustrate these divergent trajectories within a single subregion and are presented here as a deliberate comparison of what the evidence suggests happens when state intervention works with, rather than against, existing community institutions. Community-based breeding programmes (CBBPs) in Zimbabwe represent one of the more directly evidenced cases of productive complementarity in this review. Emerging from the CAMPFIRE framework and supported by international agricultural development actors, CBBPs in districts such as Matabeleland South, Masvingo, and Mashonaland Central were designed to build on existing indigenous herd management structures rather than replace them (Kaumbata et al., 2021). The underlying institutional logic involves formalizing practices already embedded in IKS collective bull selection, seasonal mating management, and communal evaluation of breeding females whilst confining formal inputs to veterinary support and record-keeping infrastructure, treating external technical assistance as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, existing community decision-making. Genetic monitoring in CBBP villages in Matabeleland South documented heterozygosity levels in Nguni and Mashona herds that were higher than in unorganised communal herds, with participating households reporting improved calf survival and body condition scores at weaning (Strydom et al., 2020); this is one of the few cases in the reviewed literature where a direct genetic outcome, rather than an inferred one, is reported alongside the institutional description. The success of these CBBPs has been linked to their respect for IKS-based decision-making authority: village breed committees retained the right to veto external sire introductions inconsistent with community preferences (Ncube et al., 2015). By contrast, Botswana’s veterinary cordon fence (VCF) system, which subdivides the country’s rangeland with more than 1,500 kilometres of fencing, was implemented without input from Tswana pastoralists, making it one of the clearest documented cases of formal policy displacing IKS (Favretto et al., 2016). The fencing severed corridors that animals had historically used to move across large areas, coinciding with disruption to the bull-rotation practices Tswana herders relied on to manage inbreeding and preserve genetic variation. Genetic studies of Tswana cattle report population subdivision, smaller effective population sizes, and higher homozygosity in areas subject to extensive fencing (Strydom et al., 2020); unless the underlying genetic studies employed longitudinal or comparative designs capable of establishing causality, the more defensible reading is that the evidence is consistent with the hypothesis that fragmentation associated with fencing contributed to reduced gene flow and increased population subdivision, rather than that a direct cause-and-effect chain from fencing to specific genetic outcomes has been established. The Botswana case is instructive precisely because the policy in question was not hostile to livestock production as such; it was designed to serve a legitimate disease-control and export-market objective, yet its failure to consult the communities whose management systems it disrupted is associated with a broader genetic-conservation setback than the narrowly targeted veterinary measure intended. Read together, these two cases offer a constructive contrast rather than two unrelated examples. The CBBP case illustrates integration, in which IKS and formal breeding approaches operate complementarily, and community institutions retain effective decision-making authority; the cordon-fence case illustrates displacement, in which formal intervention disrupts established traditional management practices without engaging them, with reported unintended consequences for population structure. Taken as a pair, they suggest that the conservation outcome of a formal intervention may depend substantially on whether existing community knowledge and management institutions are incorporated or displaced a hypothesis consistent with the evidence reviewed here, though one that would benefit from testing across a larger number of comparable policy interventions. A review of national AnGR strategies across SADC countries suggests a consistent pattern: IKS is largely absent from formal policy or acknowledged only superficially without practical application. South Africa’s National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan recognises indigenous breeds as valuable but is silent on IKS as a conservation tool. Botswana’s livestock policy similarly overlooks IKS, and its veterinary governance structure, as the cordon-fence case illustrates, has in at least this instance worked against indigenous management approaches. Comparable gaps are reported in the policies of Zimbabwe, Namibia, Zambia, and Mozambique (FAO, 2015). 6 Evidence of IKS impact on AnGR diversity Molecular studies indicate an association between IKS-managed populations and several genetic patterns conventionally interpreted as favourable for conservation, although, consistent with the cautions above, this evidence is cross-sectional and does not establish that IKS management is the cause of the diversity observed rather than one of several contributing factors. Genomic analyses of South African cattle breeds report substantial expected heterozygosity and measurable genetic heterogeneity within indigenous breed populations, which the source studies interpret as consistent with sound community-level genetic management (Strydom et al., 2020). A study of Nguni ecotypes reported heterozygosity of 69–72%, an average of 6.0–6.9 alleles per locus, and a low inbreeding coefficient (FIS) of 0.01 (Sanarana et al., 2016); these figures are reported as given in the original source and should be independently verified by readers seeking to cite them precisely. Village chickens raised under traditional free-range IKS management in Malawi, Zimbabwe, and South Africa are reported to show lower linkage disequilibrium and more distinct regional genetic patterns than flocks held in ex situ conservation initiatives (Khanyile et al., 2015). Research on South African pigs reported that village populations managed under IKS partitioned genetic variance differently from commercial populations: approximately 35.5% of variance was attributed within populations and 62.4% amongst population groups in the village-managed sample, compared with approximately 76.4% within populations and 5.4% amongst groups in the commercial sample (Hlongwane et al., 2020). These figures are reported here using AMOVA-style variance partitioning rather than as a percentage-based “diversity” score: FIS, strictly, measures the departure from Hardy–Weinberg expected heterozygosity within a population and is conventionally interpreted in relation to inbreeding or heterozygote deficiency/excess, not as a direct percentage measure of within-population diversity; FCT, from AMOVA, measures the proportion of total genetic variance attributable to differentiation amongst population groups, and reflects population structure rather than “diversity” as such. The pig study indicates that village-managed populations retained substantially greater differentiation amongst groups than commercial populations, which is itself a conservation-relevant form of diversity (population structure) distinct from within-population heterozygosity, and the original source and terminology should be consulted directly before citing specific values. Genomic and gene-expression studies report that Nguni cattle show distinct expression patterns (CCL26, MZB1, MMP12) associated with immune responses to Rhipicephalus ticks, a finding consistent with, though not direct proof of, the hypothesis that IKS-guided selection for hardiness has helped preserve functionally relevant genetic variation (Marima, 2022). Similarly, Nguni sheep are reported to carry variants at genes associated with resilience, including MYH9 (energy metabolism), GNAQ (thermoregulation), and EPAS1 (low-oxygen adaptation), consistent with their documented tolerance of heat, drought, and disease (Nxumalo et al., 2024). Local pig breeds such as Mukota pigs in Zimbabwe are reported to show greater resistance to African swine fever and lower worm loads than exotic breeds, although the genetic basis for this tolerance remains unidentified (Penrith et al., 2022; Pius et al., 2023). Taken together, this body of molecular evidence is consistent with the interpretation that populations managed under IKS retain genetic diversity and functionally relevant variation at rates comparable to, or in some respects exceeding, formally managed populations; it does not, on its own, establish that IKS management is the specific cause of these patterns, and longitudinal or experimental designs would be required to move from association to a demonstrated causal pathway. 7 Challenges and threats to IKS as a tool for AnGR conservation Despite the associations described above, IKS faces multiple compounding pressures that may limit its future conservation contribution. Globalization and rural-to-urban migration have eroded oral traditions and breeding practices, weakening the continuity of cultural heritage and locally generated innovation (Chokoe, 2016). The colonial era perception of indigenous breeds as inferior to exotic stock persists in extension messaging, driving crossbreeding that threatens genetic integrity; Nyamushamba et al. (2017) report that 66.4% of herds in South Africa’s emerging sector are now crossbred. Rising drought frequency and disease outbreaks are straining traditional systems as ecosystems shift beyond their adaptive capacity. National agricultural policies continue to prioritize commercial, improved breeds and technologies, sidelining IKS (Tiambo, 2024), whilst communities rarely capture benefits from the commercialization of indigenous breeds or ethnoveterinary knowledge, producing a pattern of exploitation without recognition (Gautam, 2019). Where private companies or research bodies have drawn on community-held selection criteria or plant-based remedies to develop marketable products, the originating households have typically received little acknowledgement and even less financial return, a pattern that may discourage communities from disclosing knowledge to outside researchers in future. Much IKS remains oral and undocumented, leaving it vulnerable to distortion or disappearance, whilst deforestation continues to erode the availability of medicinal plants essential to ethnoveterinary practice. It is important to note that IKS is not uniformly conservation positive specific practices may also intensify directional selection, restrict gene flow, favour particular phenotypes at the expense of others, increase local reproductive skew, contribute to local inbreeding, or become maladaptive as environmental, socioeconomic, and production conditions change, and the cordon-fence case above shows that disruption of IKS institutions by external policy can itself become a conservation risk. The cumulative effect of these pressures is that generational knowledge, once passed almost automatically from elder to younger herder, now competes with formal schooling, urban wage labour, and extension advice that frequently contradicts or ignores indigenous practice, narrowing the window within which transmission can occur before a given cohort of knowledge holders ages out of active herd management. 8 Knowledge gaps in IKS adoption for AnGR conservation The scientific credibility and policy traction of IKS-based conservation remain constrained by three interrelated knowledge gaps, which this review regards as its most scientifically important findings and the foundation for future research. First, genomic validation of IKS selection claims is still limited. Pastoralist communities articulate nuanced selection criteria based on phenotypic indicators believed to predict adaptive performance, yet genomic research on Southern African indigenous breeds has, to date, concentrated mainly on phylogenetic characterization and population structure rather than the functional genomics of herder-identified selection targets (Strydom et al., 2020). Without genome-wide association studies, selection-sweep analyses, or functional annotation linking phenotype to genomic loci, it remains impossible to confirm whether IKS-guided selection maintains or erodes the underlying adaptive genetic architecture. Priority research should include participatory GWAS studies built around herder-defined phenotypic categories, alongside longitudinal genotyping of herds managed under documented IKS practice compared with commercially managed herds. Such studies would also help settle an open question in the literature: whether herder-identified indicators of resilience and productivity track the same underlying loci that formal quantitative genetics has identified, or whether they capture complementary adaptive information that conventional breeding programmes currently overlook. Second, women’s knowledge in small-stock management remains systematically under-documented. Women are the primary managers of goats, sheep, and indigenous poultry, playing a substantial role in selection, health management, and genetic stewardship, yet the IKS literature remains dominated by studies of cattle systems managed predominantly by men (Galiè et al., 2025). Small ruminants represent important AnGR diversity, and their genetic integrity depends heavily on decisions made by women; leaving this knowledge undocumented renders a substantial share of the IKS governing small-stock AnGR invisible to formal conservation planning. In Namibia, Ovambo women are reported to sustain distinct small-stock lineages through matrilineal inheritance rules, effectively functioning as breed guardians, yet no study has characterised the genetic consequences of this management pattern on population structure (Nghitevelekwa, 2015). Third, the near total absence of longitudinal studies linking IKS practice to genetic metrics constitutes the most structurally significant gap identified in this review. Almost all published work is cross-sectional, capturing a single point in time and relying on retrospective ethnographic accounts. Claims that bull rotation sustains heterozygosity, or that mafisa arrangements prevent inbreeding depression, are plausible and consistent with the available cross sectional evidence, but none has yet been tested against time-series genetic data. Establishing community genomics sentinel sites, with baseline genotyping paired with concurrent IKS practice mapping and repeat sampling at five-year intervals, is proposed as a means of generating longitudinal, causally interpretable evidence. Such sites would also allow researchers to observe how IKS practice itself changes over time in response to drought, market pressure, and generational turnover, and to link those shifts directly to measurable changes in heterozygosity, inbreeding coefficients, and allelic richness within the same herds, rather than inferring genetic consequences indirectly from separate cross-sectional samples. 9 Conclusion and recommendations This review indicates that IKS is a widespread and, in the near total absence of functioning formal in situ conservation programmes at community level, practically consequential set of management practices for AnGR across Southern Africa, through which the phenotypic and, in some documented cases, genetic characteristics of breeds such as the Nguni, Tswana, Mashona, Tuli, Ovambo, and Sanga appear to be sustained (FAO, 2015; Ayalew et al., 2023). We stop short of describing IKS as the primary, de facto operative conservation framework, since the evidence reviewed here is predominantly cross-sectional and associative rather than causal, and since IKS is not uniformly conservation positive, several practices documented above (directional selection for ritual or ceremonial phenotypes, disruption of rotation systems by external policy, restricted gene flow under certain inheritance rules) can, under some conditions, also narrow genetic diversity or increase local inbreeding. The genetic diversity documented in molecular surveys of communal livestock populations high heterozygosity, low inbreeding coefficients in some studies, and the presence of haplotypes not observed in commercial herds is associated with IKS informed management in the studies reviewed, but a direct causal attribution to IKS practice specifically, as opposed to other features of communal production systems, has not been established and would require the longitudinal designs described in the previous section. The adaptive traits associated with IKS managed populations tick resistance, thermotolerance, feed-conversion efficiency under low input conditions, and trypanotolerance nonetheless carry growing strategic value under climate change and rising input costs, and their loss through the displacement of indigenous breeds would represent a foreclosure of biological options for future food-system adaptation that could not easily be reversed. The most foundational recommendation arising from this review is that IKS-based practices be more systematically documented, evaluated, and, where the evidence warrants it, integrated into the national AnGR strategies of SADC member states and into the overarching SADC livestock policy framework recognising that this integration should proceed alongside, rather than instead of, the genomic validation and longitudinal monitoring identified as priority gaps above. National AnGR strategies should incorporate IKS documentation as a monitoring mechanism, recognising community defined breed descriptors and traditional selection criteria as a valuable complementary source of data for national breed inventories submitted to FAO DAD-IS, alongside rather than in place of genetic data. The SADC Livestock Development Policy Framework should be revised to include a dedicated IKS and AnGR conservation pillar, mandating member-state reporting on IKS-related conservation activity together with, wherever feasible, genetic monitoring data. SADC member states should enact or strengthen domestic legislation implementing the Nagoya Protocol specifically for AnGR, providing a legal basis for communities to establish biocultural protocols governing livestock genetic resources and the terms of research access and benefit sharing. National AnGR research institutions should establish prior informed consent frameworks that engage traditional leadership, women’s livestock management groups, and youth pastoralists as equal partners in research design, with benefit sharing mechanisms that include capacity building in genomic literacy and co-authorship of research outputs. Farmer-to-farmer knowledge transfer platforms community livestock fairs, elder youth mentorship programmes, livestock field schools, and mobile-based digital repositories in local languages represent low-cost interventions for sustaining IKS transmission that merit piloting and evaluation across the SADC region. Universities and national agricultural research institutes across the region could play a more active convening role, pairing genomics laboratories with the traditional leadership structures and women’s livestock groups that already hold relevant selection knowledge, so that future characterization studies, including the community genomics sentinel sites proposed above, are co-designed rather than conducted on communities as passive subjects of investigation. Conserving AnGR biodiversity in Southern Africa is unlikely to be achieved through formal institutional mechanisms alone; documenting, rigorously evaluating, and, where warranted, integrating IKS into national and regional policy frameworks is not an act of cultural sentimentality but a strategic opportunity for food security, climate adaptation, and biological diversity in the twenty-first century, contingent on closing the evidentiary gaps this review has identified.
