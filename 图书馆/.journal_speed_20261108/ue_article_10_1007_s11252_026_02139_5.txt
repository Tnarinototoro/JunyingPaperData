Invasive Aedes species are continuously spreading in Europe and have been increasingly reported from urban settlements. Some of these mosquito species have been associated with small artificial habitats. Therefore, their presence may also be expected in small plastic garden ponds, which are numerous in urban settings across many parts of Europe. Here, we aimed to determine whether urban waters host a high abundance of mosquitoes and to identify the potential abiotic, biotic and landscape drivers of mosquito presence and abundance in urban ponds. We sampled 93 urban ponds in the city of Budapest (Hungary), comprising 40 garden ponds and 53 other urban ponds of both natural and anthropogenic origin, across highly urbanised areas and suburban areas with a high green index. We collected data on macroinvertebrate communities, pond management, physico-chemical parameters, and pond characteristics (mainly morphology and vegetation cover) measured in situ . Two different mosquito detection techniques were used: dip-net sampling and environmental DNA (eDNA). While only one invasive species ( Aedes koreicus ) was detected, occurring in two ponds, several other species were present, including potential malaria vectors increasingly reported from urban environments: members of the Anopheles maculipennis complex, An. claviger and An. plumbeus . The presence and abundance of mosquitoes was negatively associated with fish presence, regardless of pond type. Contrary to expectations, urbanisation did not play a major role in explaining mosquito presence or abundance in the sampled ponds. These results highlight the importance of local pond characteristics, and particularly the association between fish presence and mosquito occurrence and abundance although the broader ecological effects of fish on pond communities should also be considered. Environmental Geography Biodiversity Freshwater and Marine Ecology Urban Ecology Ecology Environmental Impact Ecological Management of Mosquito Populations Introduction Urbanisation leads to changes in biodiversity mainly through land use and land cover changes, which reduce both species richness and evenness for most biotic communities (Grimm et al. 2008 ). Green and blue spaces in urban areas (e.g. parks, forests, rivers and ponds) can buffer the negative impact of urbanisation on biodiversity (Hyseni et al. 2021 ). Blue infrastructure (i.e., rivers, streams, canals and ponds) provides essential services and benefits for the urban population (Haase et al. 2014 ) as well as supporting freshwater biodiversity (Ferzoco and McCauley 2024 ). Ponds are abundant in urban landscapes in many regions, although they are rarely of natural origin (Horváth et al. 2025 ). These ecosystems often have poor water quality due to pollution and may support exotic species (Oertli and Parris 2019 ). Among other negative consequences for biodiversity, urbanisation both fragments the landscape and creates barriers to organisms’ dispersal, which is key for the assemblage of aquatic communities (Hamer and McDonnell 2008 ; Sanderson et al. 2005 ). Urban ponds are small freshwater habitats in cities, encompassing several types, such as natural or semi-natural, ornamental, industrial, stormwater retention, reservoirs, temple and fish ponds. They are commonly located on public property or owned by the state, municipality, institutions or private companies (Horváth et al. 2025 ). Urban ponds can be up to a few hectares in size and made of concrete, stone, or natural substrate, with a variable shoreline that is usually simpler and less vegetated than natural ponds (Oertli and Parris 2019 ). Garden ponds are a specific subset of urban ponds located within domestic private gardens owned and managed by a private individual (Hamer et al. 2024 ). They commonly have an ornamental function (Hill et al. 2023 ; Oertli et al. 2023 ), are stocked with fish and have a small size (in a range of 0.25–200 m 2 ) (Hamer et al. 2024 ; Hassall 2014 ; Hill et al. 2021 ). They are often lined with rubber pond liners or other artificial materials (e.g., plastic) (Hamer et al. 2024 ). Although these characteristics may reduce their contribution to sustaining urban biodiversity, they may, however, contribute to local species richness by acting as stepping stones for the movement of insects and amphibians across the urban landscape (Gledhill et al. 2008 ; Hill et al. 2021 ). Due to the private nature of garden ponds, we have little information on how they are managed and what local diversity they comprise. Thus, there is a need to further investigate their role in contributing to biodiversity, ecosystem functions, services and disservices (Horváth et al. 2025 ). Mosquitoes (Diptera, Culicidae) are commonly associated with pond ecosystems (Collier et al. 2016 ), including those situated within urban landscapes (Zhao and Sha 2025 ). The general public often associates urban wetlands with health risks linked to mosquitoes (Hanford et al. 2019 ). Urbanisation is frequently linked to higher abundance of some mosquito species, but lower overall mosquito diversity (Perrin et al. 2023a ), suggesting that high anthropogenic pressures can promote mosquito occurrence (Boerlijst et al. 2023 ). Urbanisation can provide numerous man-made larval and resting sites for mosquitoes, including flooded underground pipes and drainage systems, which some species exploit even under suboptimal conditions, as well as providing a stable water source during the dry season through irrigation and leakage (Norris 2004 ; Valdelfener et al. 2018 ; Vora 2008 ). However, urbanisation may also negatively affect mosquito dispersal by posing barriers (Perrin et al. 2023b ). In fact, a recent meta-analysis by Perrin et al. ( 2022 ) revealed an overall decline of mosquito presence in response to urbanisation, possibly linked to less availability of aquatic habitats (e.g. lower number of tree holes, ditches, vernal pools and leaf axils; Ferraguti et al. 2016 ; Gardner et al. 2014 ; Loaiza et al. 2019 ; Steiger et al. 2016 ). Furthermore, several studies have shown that urban ponds tend to host fewer mosquitoes than natural or rural ponds (see Ferraguti et al. 2016 ; Ibañez-Justicia et al. 2015 ; Versteirt et al. 2013 ). Therefore, the association between urbanisation and mosquito abundance is still not fully understood, given the inconsistent evidence in the literature. Mosquitoes are organisms of major public health concern (World Health Organization, 2002 ). Several species of mosquitoes are of particular interest because they act as vectors for severe diseases. Several Anopheles species exhibit variable susceptibility and capability to transmit Plasmodium spp., and they occur widely throughout Europe (Bertola et al. 2022 ). However, their detailed distribution patterns remain incompletely known (Tagliapietra et al. 2019 ). The most widely distributed vector in Europe belongs to the Anopheles maculipennis complex, with several species of variable susceptibility to infection by Plasmodium species. Other mosquito species, such as An. algeriensis , An. claviger s.l., An. hyrcanus , An. plumbeus and An. superpictus have historically played a minor role as secondary vectors, although their vector competence is being reevaluated (Piperaki and Daikos 2016 ). In particular, An. plumbeus was found to be susceptible to Plasmodium falciparum , thus raising suspicion of its involvement in cryptic malaria transmission in Central and Western Europe (Schaffner et al. 2012 ). Moreover, Aedes koreicus , another potential vector of mosquito-borne diseases, is also present in Europe (Brugueras et al. 2020 ). This species is already established in several European countries, including Hungary (Kurucz et al. 2016 ; Garamszegi et al. 2023 ). Although Ae. koreicus has been associated with the outbreaks of both Japanese encephalitis (Miles 1964 ) and Dirofilaria infections (Feng 1938 ), its role in the transmission of the related pathogens remains unconfirmed (Schaffner et al. 2013 ). Therefore, reliably detecting mosquitoes in urban aquatic habitats is increasingly important. A variety of techniques are used to detect mosquitoes, including both active and passive approaches. The active ones involve collecting mosquitoes in their natural habitats (Pernat et al. 2021 ), deploying traps for adult mosquitoes (Becker et al. 2010 ), and, more recently, using environmental DNA (eDNA) from water samples (Wittwer et al. 2024 ). The reliability and effectiveness of the eDNA-based approach to detect mosquitoes, including invasive species, have been shown in several studies (Schneider et al. 2016 ; Sakata et al. 2022 ; Gutiérrez-López et al. 2023 ). Several abiotic and biotic factors can affect mosquito populations in ponds. For instance, water quality parameters, such as sulphate concentration, have been associated with increased mosquito presence and abundance (Gadzama et al. 2018 ). Also, some studies found that mosquito abundance depends on habitat size, although both positive and negative relationships have been reported (Juliano 2009 ; Sunahara et al. 2002 ). Due to their proximity to anthropogenic activities and urban infrastructure, urban ponds tend to accumulate pollutants from surface runoff (Hassall 2014 ; Oertli and Parris 2019 ) and often have low water quality (Power et al. 2018 ). The presence of such water pollutants has been linked to biodiversity loss (Villalobos-Jiménez et al. 2016 ). In particular, aquatic invertebrates are sensitive to heavy metals, as demonstrated by both field and laboratory studies (Malaj et al. 2012 ). In addition to the unintentional input of contaminants, management practices in garden ponds, including the regular use of pesticides and/or algaecides (Hamer et al. 2024 ; Loram et al. 2011 ; Márton et al. 2025 ), may also negatively affect the biota. Among the taxa that could be potentially affected, several macroinvertebrate taxa are known predators of mosquito larvae (e.g. Culler and Lamp 2009 ; Knight et al. 2003 ; Banerjee et al. 2010 ). Some amphibians can prey on mosquito larvae (Rubbo et al. 2011 ) and sometimes compete with them for the use of similar resources (Blaustein and Margalit 1996 ). Moreover, the presence of fish, often inhabiting in garden ponds, negatively influences the abundance of several macroinvertebrate taxa (Thornhill 2013 ). Thus, fish may influence mosquito populations through two pathways: direct predation on mosquito larvae and indirect effects mediated by predation on aquatic invertebrate predators of mosquitoes (Blaustein and Chase 2007 ; Becker et al. 2010 ; Horváth et al. 2025 ). Given the frequent assumption that urban waters provide breeding habitats for mosquitoes, we aimed to assess mosquito presence and abundance in garden ponds and other urban ponds in a Central European city, including invasive species or potential vectors of public health concern. We also aimed to identify the most important environmental and landscape-level predictors of mosquito presence and abundance in these garden and urban ponds, and compare results based on dip-net and eDNA samples. To this end, we sampled 93 ponds across the Budapest metropolitan area (Hungary). Methods Pond selection Here, we differentiated between garden ponds and all other urban ponds, the latter hereafter referred to as urban ponds. We selected garden ponds from the “MyPond” project online survey (Hamer et al. 2024 ; Márton et al. 2025 ), which gathered information on garden ponds across Hungary. From this dataset we first selected ponds located within a 36 km radius of the centre of Budapest, covering a substantially larger area than the city, and the level of urbanisation was calculated for each of these ponds (see ‘Landscape-level data’ below). Then, ponds were categorised into 10 equal-width categories according to their level of urbanisation, as measured by the percentage of urban cover. From each category, a random set of six ponds was selected, and their owners were contacted. The final selection from this set of ponds depended on owner consent, which resulted in 40 garden ponds that we could sample. Garden ponds had a mean area of 18 m 2 . Two ponds recorded as garden ponds (GW23 and GW24) were in fact agricultural rather than ornamental (used for irrigation or as a drinking source for livestock). Thus, they were not fully representative of garden pond category, but we kept them in the analysis to provide more complete data on urban mosquito occurrences. Natural, semi-natural or artificial ponds located within public areas (mainly city parks), with a range of functions and usually larger compared to garden ponds (Hassall 2014 ; Horváth et al. 2025 ) constituted the urban pond type. For the urban ponds, we aimed to sample all the publicly accessible ponds located within the Budapest metropolitan area, resulting in 53 urban ponds (mean area: 2023 m 2 ), which represent a close to extensive set of all such ponds in the city. Therefore, our final dataset comprised 93 ponds (Fig. 1 ). The average geographic distance between ponds was 12,345 m, whereas the minimum and maximum distances were 6.59 and 56,736 m, respectively. Fig. 1 Full size image Map of the distribution of the 53 urban (red) and 40 garden (black) ponds that were sampled across the Budapest metropolitan area (Hungary). Land cover types as defined by ‘The Ecosystem Map of Hungary’ are shown Sampling Ponds were sampled between 4 th April and 17 th May 2022. Aquatic macroinvertebrate samples were collected by performing dip-net sweeps, where sampling effort (number of sweeps) was adjusted to pond size (Table S1 and S2 ). Sweeps were equally distributed across the different microhabitats visually identified in each pond before sampling, including vegetated areas, different substrate types, open water, etc. Sampling was done from the shoreline in order to avoid damaging planted vegetation and pond liners in garden ponds, while sampling in larger urban ponds was restricted to the shoreline due to pond depth. Samples were collected using an aquarium net (15 cm x 12 cm, mesh size: 500 μm) in the case of garden ponds, and a rectangular-framed hand net (25 cm x 20 cm, mesh size: 500 μm) in the case of urban ponds. Macroinvertebrate samples were preserved in 70% alcohol. In the laboratory, individuals were sorted, counted and identified to species level whenever possible, or to the lowest taxonomic category. The main identification keys used were Tachet et al. ( 2003 ), Nilsson ( 1996 ) and Becker et al. ( 2020 ) (see Supplementary material for additional references). Mosquito larvae were identified morphologically, prioritising individuals at more advanced larval stages when multiple specimens from the same sample were available. Immature specimens that could not be reliably identified to species level were assigned to species only when a single species of the corresponding genus was detected in the same sample. To account for differences in sampling volume among samples, macroinvertebrate abundance was standardised by dividing the number of individuals recorded in each sample by the corresponding volume of water sampled and was expressed as individuals per litre (individuals L⁻¹) (see Table S3 ). At the same time, eDNA from water samples was collected in the same ponds. Water was collected from ten different points in the ponds using a sterile ladle. Once homogenised, water was filtered using Sterivex filters (0.45 μm) and a sterile syringe. The samples were dried, preserved in 99% ethanol and kept at -20 °C until processing. DNA was extracted with the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kits (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol, with an additional ZYMO PCR Inhibitor removal step (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Library preparation and sequencing were conducted by EnviroDNA (Australia). Mosquito DNA was amplified using a Culicidae-specific cytochrome c oxidase I (COI) metabarcoding assay (Krol et al. 2019 ). Libraries were prepared using a two-step PCR protocol and sequenced on Illumina platforms (iSeq 100 and NextSeq 2000). Sequence data were quality-filtered and clustered into amplicon sequence variants using VSEARCH (Rognes et al. 2016 ). Taxonomic assignment was performed against a curated reference database derived from NCBI using a 95% similarity threshold. Negative extraction and PCR controls were included throughout library preparation to monitor contamination. Pond features At each pond, surface area (m 2 ), open water (visual estimate of the proportion of open water not covered by emergent or floating vegetation) and pond age were recorded (based on information from pond owners or managers). The area of urban ponds was calculated using either the Google Maps measuring tool or measured in situ . For garden ponds, we approximated the pond surface area based on the lengths and widths (that pond owners provided in the “MyPond” online survey; Márton et al. 2025 ), by multiplying these measurements. Regarding pond age, in the case of garden ponds, if owners did not provide a specific year of installation but stated that they inherited the pond from a previous owner and provided the year they moved in, then pond age was calculated from that year (Hamer et al. 2024 ). For urban ponds, we used the ‘Historical Imagery’ from Google Earth Pro to establish when they appeared. Conductivity, pH value and chlorophyll- a were measured in situ with portable meters (HANNA (HI 9142 N), WTW (3620 IDS) and AquaPen AP 110-C, Photon System Instruments). Concentrations of total phosphorus (µg/L), total nitrogen (mg/L), total organic carbon (mg/L), and heavy metals (Al, As, Ba, Cr, Cu, Fe, Li, Mn, Ni and Zn, in µg/L) as potential pollutants in urban settings were measured from water samples following the protocols from Eaton et al. ( 2005 ). The type of pond (garden or urban) was considered as a factor. This set of variables was considered as local abiotic predictors in the subsequent analyses. The presence of fish (larvae and adults) and amphibians (from eggs to adults) in each pond was also recorded. These two binary variables (1, presence; 0, absence) were considered as local biotic predictors of mosquito presence and abundance in the subsequent analyses. We estimated the abundance of the predatory life stages of macroinvertebrate taxa that can potentially prey on mosquito larvae (for instance, Dytiscidae, Chaoboridae, Heteropterans, Odonates, etc.) and considered it as a local biotic variable (hereafter, abundance of predatory macroinvertebrates). Landscape-level data To characterise the level of urbanisation surrounding the ponds, we used the Ecosystem Map of Hungary (project KEHOP-430-VEKOP-15-2016-00001, Ministry of Agriculture, 2019). The number of raster pixels (20 m x 20 m) was counted within a 1 km radius around each focal pond using the Zonal Histogram tool. The classification of land cover was used as defined by The Ecosystem Map of Hungary and then these were grouped together into larger categories of ‘Forest’, ‘Grassland’, ’Agricultural land’, ‘Standing and flowing waters’ and ‘Urban’ land cover types. The urban category contained pixels of tall and short buildings, sealed and dirt roads, railways and other artificial surfaces. Urbanisation was calculated as the proportion of total pixels within the 1 km radius covered by the ‘Urban’ land cover type (Oertli and Parris 2019 ). The overall spatial analysis was carried out in QGIS 3.28.1 (QGIS Development Team, 2022 ). We calculated pairwise geographic distances between all ponds using ‘DistGeo’ function from the “geosphere” R package (Hijmans et al. 2022 ). For the analysis of spatial community structure, we used Moran’s Eigenvector Maps (MEMs) based on the spatial position of the ponds, using the “adespatial” R package (Dray et al. 2025 ). We only kept the positive MEM eigenvectors for the subsequent analyses since these represent positively autocorrelated spatial structures across a range of scales, and their ordering typically runs from broad to progressively finer structure as Moran’s I/eigenvalue declines. Ecologically, those structures can reflect dispersal limitation or spatially structured environmental drivers (Dray 2011 ). Statistical analysis To reduce dimensionality and summarise the variation in heavy metal concentrations, we performed a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on log-transformed data. The first principal component explaining the highest variance (40.4%) was retained for further analysis and used as an explanatory variable in subsequent analysis (Figure S1 in the Supplementary material). Hereafter, we will refer to this PC1 as ‘metalsPC1’. Stepwise selection of positive MEMs was performed using a redundancy analysis (RDA) framework on the detrended mosquito community data set (Borcard et al. 2018 ). The selection process, based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and using both forward and backward stepwise search, identified the positive MEMs most strongly related to mosquito community composition (i.e., MEM2). We conducted a variation partitioning analysis to divide the variation of mosquito community composition among three sets of predictors: local abiotic, local biotic and landscape. Mosquito community data were Hellinger transformed. The local abiotic matrix was composed of pond features that were selected with a stepwise model selection with the ‘ordistep’ function in the R package vegan from the complete set of local abiotic variables (i.e., open water, metalsPC1, TN and pond area). The local biotic matrix was composed of both amphibians and fish presence, and the abundance of predatory macroinvertebrates. Finally, urbanisation and the MEM2 constituted the landscape matrix. Variation partitioning was carried out using the ‘varpart’ function, and the significance of unique contributions of each explanatory matrix to variation in mosquito community composition was tested using the ‘anova’ function, while controlling for the remaining explanatory matrices; both functions were implemented in the R package vegan (Oksanen et al. 2025 ). To analyse which predictors had the strongest effect on mosquito abundance, a linear model (LM) was used. Prior to this, a stepwise model selection was performed to select significant local abiotic variables to be included in the LM (‘stepAIC’ function, direction= “both”). The stepwise model selection identified pond area and metalsPC1 as the local abiotic variables best explaining mosquito abundance. Using the same procedure, the positive MEMs with a significant effect on mosquito abundance were selected (i.e., MEM2 and MEM5) and then included in the LM. The set of local biotic variables (amphibians and fish presence, and abundance of predatory macroinvertebrates) was also included. Similarly, to assess which predictors most strongly influenced mosquito presence based on dip-net samples, we fitted a generalised linear model (GLM) with a binomial distribution and logit link function. Prior to this, local abiotic variables and positive MEMs, separately, were selected using the ‘stepAIC’ function, which resulted in keeping metalsPC1 and open water, and MEM2, respectively. An analogous GLM was also fitted to the mosquito presence data derived from eDNA samples in order to compare the results obtained with the other sampling technique. Again, a prior selection of the positive MEMs and of the local abiotic variables was done, which resulted in keeping MEM17 and open water, respectively, for the GLM. The LM on mosquito abundance, the GLM on mosquito presence (dip-net) and the GLM on mosquito presence (eDNA) were repeated, this time including an interaction term between fish presence and abundance of predatory macroinvertebrates, to test a possible effect of the biotic interaction between the mosquito predators. Results From the 93 ponds sampled, a total of 21 ponds hosted mosquitoes, of which nine were garden ponds, and twelve were urban ponds based on dip-net sampling (see Fig. 2 ). Based on eDNA sampling, eight garden and six urban ponds had mosquitoes. In eleven cases, eDNA detected Culicidae in samples where dip-net samples did not. Conversely, in 18 ponds, Culicidae were collected with dip-net sampling, but not detected by eDNA. Dip-net samples resulted in a higher total number of species detected, while the eDNA-based species were a subset of these (Fig. 3 ). Dip-net samples detected one taxon shared between the garden and urban ponds, with eight taxa unique to garden ponds and seven taxa unique to urban ponds (Fig. 3 a). eDNA samples detected two taxa shared between the garden and urban ponds, with two taxa unique to garden ponds and only one taxon unique to urban ponds (Fig. 3 b). Both techniques detected mosquitoes in the same ponds on only three occasions (Fig. 2 a and b). This suggests that the two sampling methods are complementary. We identified 14 taxa from four genera. In ponds where mosquitoes were present, their species numbers ranged between one and four (not shown). Both detection techniques detected the same taxa on three occasions (Fig. 2 c). Thirteen taxa were exclusively detected by dip-netting whereas only two taxa were exclusively detected by eDNA method (Fig. 2 c). Among the three most invasive Aedes species in Europe, we only found Aedes koreicus , occurring in two urban ponds (Table 1 ). Regarding potential malaria-vectors, Anopheles maculipennis s.l. was detected in five urban ponds and one garden pond, while two other Anopheles taxa with lower risk of transmission, An. claviger s.l. and An. plumbeus , were each found in three ponds. eDNA samples detected the same four genera that were detected by dip-netting (Table 1 ). Fig. 4 Full size image Venn diagram of the variation partitioning of the mosquito community explained by local abiotic, local biotic and landscape predictors Discussion Here, we assessed whether urban and garden ponds function as larval habitats for mosquitoes during spring in a Central European city, and examined the effects of potential drivers of mosquito presence and abundance. Overall, mosquitoes were detected in relatively few ponds - and generally in low abundance - regardless of the pond type or detection method. Fish presence showed the strongest negative association with both mosquito presence and abundance. Despite using two complementary detection techniques, mosquitoes were detected only rarely. Individuals of the genus Culex were found mostly in garden ponds, with Culex hortensis being the only Culex species detected in urban ponds using eDNA. Culiseta longiareolata was recorded only in two garden ponds and we did not detect its presence in urban ponds, even though this species is typically associated with artificial breeding habitats in Central Europe (Seidel et al. 2013 ; Becker and Hoffmann 2011 ). In contrast, Aedes species were mostly found in urban ponds. The observed differences among species may therefore reflect environmental differences between the two pond types, rather than intrinsic species preferences. Because garden and urban ponds differ in several ecological characteristics, the associations observed here should be interpreted as habitat-specific occurrence patterns rather than evidence of active habitat selection. Increasing levels of urban pollution can reduce the quality of larval habitats, thereby promoting the dominance of relatively tolerant species, some of them belonging to the genus Culex , known for their adaptability to temporary and degraded aquatic habitats (Ishii and Sohn 1987 ; Noori et al. 2015 ). In our study, Culex hortensis was the most frequently detected species, a finding consistent with previous evidence showing that this species is positively influenced by landscape anthropisation (Perrin et al. 2023b ) and prefers artificial habitats over natural ponds (Martínez-Barciela et al. 2025 ). We found Aedes koreicus only in two of the sampled urban ponds. Aedes vexans was recorded in one urban pond, and was the only species exclusively detected by eDNA. Both Aedes koreicus and Ae. vexans are negatively influenced by landscape anthropisation (Perrin et al. 2022 , 2023b ). In European urban settlements, Ae. koreicus generally shows habitat preference towards puddles, basin of fountains, catch basins (Montarsi et al. 2013 ) or vases in cemeteries (Versteirt et al. 2012 ), habitats that were not included in our survey. In contrast, Ae. vexans prefers temporary floodwaters for larval development (Pothmann et al. 2025 ; Schäfer and Lundström 2006 ). The strong preference of these species for habitats other than those we sampled could explain the very low detection in our data. We found Anopheles plumbeus which is considered a potential malaria vector (Schaffner et al. 2012 ), with notes on its vector role for West Nile virus and dirofilariasis (Vermeil et al. 1960 ; Schaffner et al. 2001 ), in two garden ponds. A study performed in Belgium (Dekoninck et al. 2011 ) concluded that the strong population expansion of An. plumbeus was related to a larval habitat shift from tree-holes in natural ecosystems to anthropogenic influenced habitats, posing a public health concern with the expansion of this species into human-dominated environments. Thus, its low occurrence in our dataset suggests that these ponds are unlikely to represent important larval habitats but are consistent with the habitat expansion of this species that has been documented in several European settings (Bueno-Marí and Jiménez-Peydró 2011 ; Townroe and Callaghan 2014 ). Another potential malaria vector is An. claviger s.l., which we recorded in one of the garden ponds. This species has long been considered as a minor malaria vector due to its strong zoophilic tendency (Becker et al. 2010 ), but it has been increasingly detected in urban or peri-urban areas (Marí et al. 2014 ; Medlock and Vaux 2014 ; Ruiz-Arrondo et al. 2023 ; Wegner 2009 ) and has been associated with malaria outbreaks reported in Eastern Mediterranean countries (Coluzzi et al. 1964 ; Gramiccia 1956 ). The few records of the species in the ponds examined could be due to its greater affinity for margins of ditches, canals, scrapes or small rocky puddles (Becker et al. 2010 ; Boukraa et al. 2016 ). The An. maculipennis complex was detected in urban ponds with both sampling methods and in garden ponds using eDNA. The species of this complex are typically associated with agricultural landscapes and are positively influenced by landscape anthropisation (Perrin et al. 2023b ). High abundances of the An. maculipennis complex have been reported from extensive areas for larval development, typically rice fields, and areas with farms but also urban areas (Gilioli et al. 2024 ). In other parts of Europe, this species has usually been collected in ephemeral and anthropogenic habitats such as ditches, irrigation channels and river bed pools (Calzolari et al. 2021 ; Kavran et al. 2018 ). Consistent with Evans et al. ( 2019 ), we did not find a clear urbanisation effect on mosquito abundance. However, previous studies have reported differences in mosquito abundances across an urban gradient (e.g. Johnson et al. 2008 ; Li et al. 2014 ). Although we found no statistically significant effects of spatial drivers in our study, the observed pattern was consistent with a higher frequency of mosquitoes in ponds situated in suburban areas as opposed to those located in the city centre. Similar findings have been reported elsewhere, with Goselle et al. ( 2017 ) observing the highest abundance of several species in suburban areas. Furthermore, the meta-analysis of Perrin and collaborators ( 2022 ) found a general negative effect of urbanisation on mosquito abundance but also noted an increase in the abundance of mosquito species that are of global concern in human-modified landscapes. This suggests that urbanisation may favour a small subset of species which are adapted to anthropogenic landscapes. We did not observe a significant effect of heavy metals on mosquito community composition, presence or abundance. The most abundant heavy metals in our ponds were manganese, aluminium, and iron. However, because heavy metals were analysed collectively rather than individually, any species-specific or metal-specific effects may have been masked, and our results should not be interpreted as evidence that individual heavy metals have no influence on mosquito communities. In agreement with our results, Mireji et al. ( 2008 ) did not find correlations between manganese or iron ion concentrations and mosquito species in urban aquatic habitats, while they found that copper was positively related to certain mosquito species. Notably, the average ion concentrations of these three heavy metals in our urban ponds were approximately two orders of magnitude lower than those in Mireji et al. ( 2008 ). Moreover, Ndenga et al. ( 2012 ) observed a negative relationship between late-instar anopheline larvae abundance and iron concentration, but the iron ion concentrations in their study were much higher than those observed in our ponds. Regarding other water parameters, we did not observe a substantial effect of physico-chemical parameters on overall mosquito abundance, consistent with Beketov et al. ( 2010 ). Nevertheless, some species seem to have strong preferences for waters with high salinity and phosphate concentrations (Beketov et al. 2010 ), conductivity, dissolved oxygen, nutrients or pH value (Avramov et al. 2024 ; Yadav et al. 2012 ). In addition, a recent meta-analysis by Avramov et al. ( 2024 ) concluded that urban vector species are the most adaptable to a wider range of values for water quality properties. Although variance partitioning revealed a relationship between the abundance of predatory macroinvertebrates and the mosquito community, we did not find a clear relationship between the abundance of mosquitoes and predatory macroinvertebrates in the regression analysis. Previous studies reporting a similar inconsistency have suggested that the presence of alternative prey may reduce predation on mosquito larvae, especially as the larvae grow and become harder to capture (Kumar et al. 2008 ). Complex submerged vegetation might also reduce overall predatory efficiency (Sunahara et al. 2002 ). However, we did not observe such a pattern. Possibly, in our ponds, macroinvertebrate predators’ abundance or diversity was not large enough to have a significant negative effect on the mosquito community. This is similar to findings from several studies that reported less diverse macroinvertebrate assemblages in artificial habitats, with a generally lower potential importance in limiting mosquito populations than natural habitats (Srivastava 2006 ; Yanoviak 2001 ). In contrast to macroinvertebrate predators, fish presence showed a clear negative association with both mosquito presence and abundance, whereas amphibian presence had no such relationship, even though they can also act as natural control agents (Perrin et al. 2023a ). Fish introductions are common in urban ponds, often to control mosquitoes (Oertli and Parris 2019 ). Fish may influence the numbers of mosquitoes both directly through predation and indirectly by reducing the number of aquatic insects, which would otherwise be effective predators of mosquito larvae (Becker et al. 2010 ). Although some studies have reported a combined predatory effect of fish and macroinvertebrates on mosquito larvae (e.g. Hurlbert et al. 1972 ; Peck and Walton 2008 ), this interaction was not statistically significant in the present study. Nevertheless, the observed association with fish cannot necessarily be attributed to fish predation alone. Fish presence may covary with other pond characteristics or management practices, such as water circulation, especially in garden ponds (Hamer et al. 2024 ), which may also affect habitat suitability for mosquito breeding through water surface disturbance (Jackson et al. 2009 , Horváth et al. 2025 ). Moreover, because garden and urban ponds were analysed together, the overall association may partly reflect patterns driven by one of the two pond types. Therefore, the negative association between fish and mosquitoes should not be interpreted as evidence for fish stocking being an ecologically beneficial management solution, because fish can reduce amphibian populations, alter invertebrate assemblages, and shift pond ecosystems towards a turbid, phytoplankton-dominated state by reducing zooplankton biomass and the extent of macrophyte beds (Scheffer et al. 1993 ; Oertli and Parris 2019 ; Bucciarelli et al. 2019 ; Hamer et al. 2002 ). Moreover, mosquito occurrence was relatively low across ponds overall, indicating that these habitats do not appear to function as major sites for larval development of the recorded mosquito species in the studied urban landscape during the spring sampling period. Therefore, the ecological costs of fish introductions should be carefully considered in these systems. Pond area did not have an effect on either mosquito presence or abundance in our study. This contrasts with previous empirical and experimental work reporting positive relationships between habitat size and mosquito abundance (Harlan and Paradise 2006 ; Zhao et al. 2020 ). Other studies including both natural and artificial habitats (e.g. Westby and Juliano 2017 ; Bradshaw and Holzapfel 1988 ; Bevins 2007 ; Sunahara et al. 2002 ) have found species-specific habitat size preferences. For instance, several studies reported that Aedes japonicus and Culex larvae preferred larger containers (Sunahara et al. 2002 ; Westby and Juliano 2017 ), while Ae. koreicus preferred smaller habitats (Montarsi et al. 2013 ). While habitat size did not strongly influence total mosquito abundance in our ponds, it was one of the significant drivers of species composition, which is similar to findings by e.g. Talaga et al. ( 2017 ), where a significant fraction of the variation in community composition was explained by the size of the habitat, particularly at small spatial scales. We observed differences between mosquito detection techniques related to pond type. In urban ponds, dip-netting performed better than eDNA, whereas in garden ponds, the detection rate was similar between techniques. Thus, dip-netting became overall much more efficient than eDNA since it allowed us to collect a larger number of taxa, and in a larger number of ponds. Moreover, eDNA surveys still need to be improved as the number of culicid species in the available databases is still somewhat limited, with only three genera ( Aedes , Anopheles and Culex ) accounting for 78% of the occurrences (Gutiérrez-López et al. 2023 ) and the databases are skewed in geographical and taxonomic average (Chapman et al. 2024 ). Despite these issues, it is a truly non-invasive method that does not harm species or habitats, and sometimes can have higher detection probabilities than traditional survey methods (Schneider et al. 2016 ). Given its comparable performance to dip-netting in garden ponds, eDNA could furthermore provide a useful tool for mosquito monitoring in citizen science programs. In our study, the lower eDNA detection rates in urban ponds may reflect dilution effects in the generally larger water bodies (Hansen et al. 2018 ; Tréguier et al. 2014 ). Caveats and perspectives While mosquito species composition was diverse across the ponds and the two pond types, their overall occurrence was relatively low, as mosquitoes were detected in only 32 of the 93 sampled ponds (34.4%), and usually in low abundance. Nevertheless, some of the results should be interpreted with caution, because of the relatively low number of mosquito detections and the single-round sampling. Although the early sampling season (spring), and the highly seasonal nature of mosquito assemblages in temperate regions might have contributed to the overall low occurrence and abundance of this group during our survey (Hanford et al. 2019 ; Rettich et al. 2007 ; Schäfer et al. 2004 ), in Central Europe, mosquito larval activity typically commences in early spring (March-April). Thus, increasing temperatures and day length terminate diapause or stimulate egg hatching, depending on the species (Meuti 2026 ; Rydzanicz et al. 2016 ). Sampling in spring may also have favoured the early detection of invasive mosquito species, as early seasonal larval activity has been reported for some invasive taxa adapted to temperate climates (Kaufman and Fonseca 2014 ). While dip-net sampling captures only immature mosquitoes, which do not yet cause nuisance or spread diseases, their density can be used to predict the number of adult mosquitoes (Zhao et al. 2020 ). Although this aspect was beyond the scope of the present study, these data could support mosquito control at municipal level. At present, Hungary has a mosquito control programme to which municipalities may apply to participate. This programme targets native nuisance mosquito species in general rather than individual vector species, although a species-specific approach focusing on medically important vectors could improve the efficiency of mosquito control (Fadel et al. 2026 ). In addition to ponds, other artificial water bodies, such as small plastic containers or flowerpots in gardens, are also known to serve as sites for larval development of mosquitoes (European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control 2012 ; Ibañez-Justicia 2020 ). Although these habitats were not included in our study, eDNA-based citizen science surveys could complement conventional larval surveys by enabling large numbers of small, widely distributed larval habitats to be sampled using simple water collection protocols. Future comparative studies across different cities, climates, and mosquito habitats, and incorporating seasonality whenever possible, would provide a broader understanding of mosquito communities in urban ecosystems. Such knowledge is particularly important under ongoing climate change, as shifts in temperature, humidity, and precipitation can strongly influence mosquito distributions and the dynamics of mosquito-borne diseases (Brugueras et al. 2020 ; Boerlijst et al. 2023 ). In view of all the above, we believe that this study may be of interest to a wide range of disciplines, such as public health, epidemiology, or urban planning.
