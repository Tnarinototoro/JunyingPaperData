Urban sustainability planning and urban outcomes in a rapidly growing city in Mogadishu, Somalia

  • AA

    Abshir Abdulle Amei 1

  • AA

    Ali Abdi Yusuf 2*

  • 1. Faculty of Social Science, Department of Social Work and Social Administration, SIMAD University, Mogadishu, Somalia

  • 2. Faculty of Social Science, Department of Public Administration, SIMAD University, Mogadishu, Somalia

Article metrics

View details

Abstract

Rapid urbanization in fragile and post-conflict cities creates complex challenges related to environmental degradation, informal settlement expansion, infrastructure limitations, weak governance systems, and unequal access to urban services. This study examined the relationships between Environmental Sustainability Planning, Social Sustainability Planning, Institutional and Governance Capacity, and Urban Outcomes in Mogadishu, Somalia. The study targeted a population of 933 individuals, including residents, NGO representatives, and CSO representatives engaged in urban development, governance, community development, and service delivery. A quantitative, descriptive, and correlational research design was adopted, and data were collected from 280 respondents using structured questionnaires. The measurement assessment demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity, with all constructs achieving acceptable internal consistency. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Relative Importance Index (RII), Pearson correlation, and multiple regression analysis. The findings revealed that Environmental Sustainability Planning, Social Sustainability Planning, and Institutional and Governance Capacity were positively and significantly associated with Urban Outcomes. Environmental Sustainability Planning demonstrated the strongest association with Urban Outcomes, followed by Social Sustainability Planning and Institutional and Governance Capacity. The study concludes that sustainable urban development in Mogadishu requires integrated approaches combining environmental management, inclusive planning, effective governance, and coordinated institutional action. The findings contribute empirical evidence from a rapidly urbanizing and institutionally fragile post-conflict city context.

1 Introduction

Urbanization is one of the most important processes that have changed settlement patterns, economic activities, and social interactions, and have shaped global development. Urban growth provides increased opportunities for employment, innovation, and economic growth. However, rapid urbanization has introduced challenges to the sustainability of cities, such as strain on infrastructure, environmental pollution, housing scarcity, and skewed access to urban facilities (Seto et al., 2017). Sustainable Urban Development (SUD) has become a focus of attention in fast-changing cities because of the rising demand for planning methods that consider sustainable development, social inclusion, and effective urban governance. Urban sustainability research reveals that cities are complex social, ecological, and institutional systems characterized by the high interdependence of their components, which require integrated governance and planning (Webb et al., 2018). Similarly, adaptive capacity and coordination and collaboration among institutions are central themes in urban resilience scholarship as tools for responding to complex urban problems (Meerow et al., 2016).

This is especially true in developing countries, where urbanization often occurs in the absence of sufficient investment in infrastructure, housing systems, and institutional capacity. Slums are common in cities in the Global South, which are characterized by a lack of formal planning, weak land security, and inadequate service delivery, among other factors (Visagie and Turok, 2020). Evidence from African cities demonstrates that achieving sustainable urban development outcomes is not simply a consequence of urban growth; rather, it depends on the interaction between infrastructure provision, governance capacity, and effective spatial planning systems (Haou et al., 2025). Furthermore, informal urban development reflects broader institutional challenges, including limited regulatory enforcement, insecure tenure arrangements and weak coordination between urban authorities and communities.

The Horn of Africa is an area of rapid urbanization in challenging socioeconomic, environmental, and governance contexts. Demographic pressure due to population growth, rural-to-urban migration, displacement, and shifting economic opportunities is growing among urban centers in the region. This has been associated with increased demand for housing, infrastructure, transportation, sanitation, and public services, placing additional pressure on urban governance systems. Mitlin and Bartlett (2020) highlight that informal urbanization in African cities often occurs where planning institutions and housing systems are unable to accommodate increasing urban demand, resulting in fragmented urban development and unequal access to essential services. Furthermore, for sustainable urban development, institutional frameworks that can coordinate investment in infrastructure, land management, and inclusive planning processes are needed (Turok and Mcgranahan, 2020).

The Somali context is relevant for sustainable urban planning because urban development has occurred in the context of conflict, displacement, and institutional change. Urbanization in fragile and conflict-prone contexts tends to occur outside formal planning systems because of a lack of capacity in formal planning systems, land management institutions, and regulatory mechanisms to effectively guide urbanization. Fragile city research shows that conflict and fragile governance conditions are associated with changes in urban development processes, including variations in infrastructure provision, spatial organization, and institutional coordination (Beall et al., 2013). Moreover, displacement and conflict-related population movements are associated with rapid urban transformation in Somalia, including increased pressure on housing, urban services, and local governance arrangements, particularly in cities where formal planning and regulatory institutions are constrained (Bakonyi et al., 2019).

Mogadishu, the capital city of Somalia, is one of the most important cities in the world to consider when it comes to sustainable urban planning challenges in fragile and fast-growing cities. The city has experienced significant spatial growth, infrastructure requirements, and pressure on public services. However, in fragile situations, urban development is influenced by informal land regimes, enforcement of regulations, conflict histories, displacement dynamics, and post-conflict reconstruction issues, in addition to population growth. Evidence from African informal settlements demonstrates that insecure tenure arrangements, limited institutional coordination, and inadequate planning frameworks are associated with fragmented urban development and difficulties in achieving equitable service provision (Kovacic et al., 2019). Thus, it is important to examine the interplay of environmental sustainability planning and social inclusion with the capacity for institutional governance to understand urban outcomes in Mogadishu.

The theoretical framework of this study combines Urban Growth Theory, Sustainable Development Theory, and urban resilience perspectives. Urban Growth Theory focuses on evolving urban spatial patterns in terms of population growth, economic functions, and land use and infrastructure changes (Brueckner, 2000). However, traditional views of urban growth typically assume that a formal land market, effective planning, and strong regulatory institutions exist to guide urban growth. In fragile and post-conflict situations, where urban development is often determined by informal land tenure systems, poor regulatory enforcement, conflict legacies, and displacement dynamics, these assumptions are less relevant. Settlement patterns reflect not only population expansion and economic conditions, but also institutional gaps, humanitarian pressures, and poor governance arrangements (Watson, 2009). Conflict is often associated with disruptions in planning processes and systems, while displacement is frequently linked with rapid urban growth that may exceed the capacity of services and regulations. Thus, to understand urban growth in cities like Mogadishu, it is necessary to develop perspectives on urban growth beyond the conventional ones to include informal governance structures and post-conflict reconstruction processes.

This is complemented by Sustainable Development Theory, which emphasizes that achieving sustainability requires balancing economic development, environmental protection, and social equity to address the needs of present and future generations (Mensah, 2019). An urban resilience lens further reinforces the framework by emphasizing the need for adaptive governance and institutional capacity, as well as for urban systems to be responsive to complex social, environmental, and political challenges (Kong et al., 2022).

The state of the literature on urban sustainability issues, such as informal settlements, infrastructure constraints, environmental pressures, and governance constraints in cities of developing countries, has made a tremendous leap. However, these dimensions have been studied separately in the past, with an emphasis on specific issues such as housing, land management, environmental risks, or governance processes, without incorporating them into a holistic sustainability planning approach (Fatti, 2022). However, few empirical studies have focused on the relationship between environmental and social sustainability planning, institutional and governance capacity, and urban outcomes in fragile and rapidly urbanizing cities. This is significant because sustainability issues in these contexts are interdependent and demand integrated approaches to analysis.

Therefore, this study focuses on how environmental and social sustainability planning are related to institutional and governance capacities and urban outcomes in Mogadishu, Somalia. This study incorporates these dimensions into a single analytical framework, providing empirical data from a fragile and rapidly urbanizing context. The results provide policy practitioners, city managers, urban planners, development agencies, and scholars with insights into inclusive, resilient, and sustainable approaches to urban development.

2 Literature review

2.1 Sustainability planning and urban outcomes

The acceleration of urbanization is one of the defining characteristics of contemporary development, transforming patterns of economic activity, human settlements, and social relations across the globe (Sakketa, 2025). While urbanization can create opportunities for employment, innovation, and economic advancement, unmanaged urban growth can generate significant sustainability challenges, including environmental degradation, infrastructure pressure, housing shortages, and unequal access to urban services (Li et al., 2024). This rapid growth has led to a significant shift in land-use patterns and increased sustainability pressures, especially in fast-growing cities, where population growth and spatial changes challenge planning systems (Schiavina et al., 2022). Urban sustainability research studies cities as socio-ecological and institutional structures that are interrelated and in which environmental conditions, social structures, infrastructure systems, and governance structures interact with one another and are associated with variations in urban outcomes (Childers et al., 2014).

These problems are especially intense in cities in developing countries, where fast rates of population growth are often combined with inadequate infrastructure, unplanned urban expansion (informal settlements), insecure access to land, and low levels of governance. Informal settlements are usually the outcome of the lack of capacity of formal planning institutions to meet the rising urban demand for shelter, causing a lack of services, environmental exposure, and social marginalization (Haley et al., 2021). Furthermore, urban development alone is insufficient to achieve sustainable development. In African cities, evidence shows that positive outcomes are shaped by the interplay between spatial planning systems, spatial infrastructure investments, and institutional capacities (Turok and Mcgranahan, 2013). Thus, a single understanding of urban transformation needs to consider the interdependencies between Environmental Sustainability Planning, Social Sustainability Planning, and Institutional and Governance Capacity in fragile urban contexts.

2.2 Environmental sustainability planning and urban outcomes

Environmental Sustainability Planning (ESP) incorporates ecological concerns in urban development by integrating land-use planning, environmental protection, waste management, and planning for climate-responsive infrastructure. Good environmental planning is needed because unplanned urban growth can result in fragmentation, inefficient infrastructure, and higher ecological stress (Enyew et al., 2026). Thus, land-use control is one of the most important instruments that cities have at their disposal for controlling spatial expansion, environmental resources, and the development of sustainable cities. Evidence from urban studies around the world shows that poorly managed urban growth contributes to higher demand for land and environmental issues through encroachments into ecologically sensitive areas (Seto et al., 2011).

Environmental sustainability planning is also related to urban infrastructure, such as drainage, sanitation, waste management, and green infrastructure. These systems are important for environmental quality, public health, and residents’ quality of life. Abubakar et al. (2022) emphasize that waste management is a vital element of sustainable urban planning because ineffective solid waste management is associated with environmental pollution, public health risks, and reduced quality of life in cities, particularly in rapidly growing urban areas where municipal service capacity is limited. Moreover, informal settlement studies have identified institutional constraints, poor coordination, and limited-service providers as challenges to the provision of urban sanitation and waste-related services (Dickson-gomez et al., 2023).

Purposeful ecological planning and adaptive environmental management are prominent themes in urban resilience research because they enhance cities’ ability to withstand environmental pressures and continue to function in the face of environmental change (Meerow and Stults, 2016). However, in fragile and fast-growing cities, the absence of institutional capacity, poor implementation of environmental laws, conflicting land-use interests, and underdeveloped infrastructure capacities make it difficult to implement planning for environmental sustainability. Hence, there is a close relationship between environmental sustainability and the capacity for governance and coordinated planning approaches (Alblooshi et al., 2026).

Based on this, this study frames Environmental Sustainability Planning in four dimensions. These dimensions are the most important aspects of ESP operations, as they reflect ecological protection, sustainable use of resources, mitigation of uncontrolled urban growth, and development of environmentally sensitive urban infrastructure.

  • Environmental protection

  • Waste management

  • Urban sprawl management

  • Green infrastructure

These dimensions are explored through the lens of urban outcomes: environmental quality, infrastructure conditions, and urban resilience.

2.3 Social sustainability planning and urban outcomes

Social Sustainability Planning (SSP) refers to the embedding of social inclusion, community participation, housing security, and equitable access to basic services in urban development processes (Dempsey et al., 2011). Social sustainability is not only about increasing physical infrastructure but also about whether urban transformation responds to inequalities and the needs of the vulnerable and whether it promotes inclusive decision-making. Social sustainability is closely linked to the capacity of cities to offer safe living conditions and equitable access to urban resources (Bramley, 2009). Informal settlement studies have shown that insecure tenure, inadequate housing, and limited access to basic services are significant issues in the path toward socially sustainable urban development (Seeliger and Turok, 2013). These challenges are especially relevant in cities in developing countries, where the poor and vulnerable typically face exclusion from formal planning systems and limited access to urban opportunities.

Social sustainability planning also has vulnerable group inclusion as one of its key aspects, as urban development processes often involve low-income groups, displaced populations, and socially marginalized groups. Inclusive urban planning addresses the needs of disadvantaged groups and provides access to urban opportunities, urban decision-making, and equitable distribution of development benefits (Dempsey et al., 2011). Likewise, community participation is vital because participatory planning processes provide opportunities for communities to share indigenous knowledge, voice priorities, and become part of decision-making processes in the city. Informal settlement upgrading programs have demonstrated that involvement is linked to higher levels of local ownership, social cohesion, and urban interventions being accepted within the community (Wolff et al., 2021).

Social sustainability is particularly significant in fragile and displacement-affected cities, as population movements tend to strain housing systems, public service provision, and local governance structures. These conditions are relevant to Mogadishu, which faces rapid urbanization, displacement pressures, and institutional constraints. Thus, social sustainability planning offers insights into social relations of inclusion and participation, housing conditions and service use, and access to social outcomes in cities.

In this context, this study presents four dimensions to conceptualize Social Sustainability Planning. These dimensions provide a holistic framework that reflects the key social processes that foster urban inclusion, diminish inequalities, and build community participation and access to urban resources and services.

  • Vulnerable-group inclusion

  • Community participation

  • Housing conditions

  • Equitable service accessibility

These dimensions are explored in terms of the urban outcomes they relate to: social inclusion, accessibility, and sustainable urban development.

2.4 Institutional and governance capacity and urban outcomes

Institutional and Governance Capacity (IGC) is the extent to which urban institutions can develop policies, coordinate stakeholders, enforce regulations, mobilize resources, and implement sustainable urban development strategies. Good governance capacity is critical for effective sustainability planning, which requires institutions that can ensure that policies are turned into coordinated actions and that competing urban demands are managed. Cities’ capacity to respond to complex environmental and social issues is shaped by governance arrangements, institutional coordination, and administrative capacity (Deshpande, 2025). Institutional readiness is a measure of the ability of urban authorities to set up a planning system, formulate policies, and react to urban developments. However, the limited technical capacity and administrative systems of weak institutional structures are frequently barriers to planning implementation (Turok and Mcgranahan, 2020).

Enforcement capacity and coordination among stakeholders represent important dimensions of urban governance capacity. The ability of institutions to manage land use, regulate urban expansion, and coordinate diverse urban development actors is an important component of sustainable urban development (Details, 2023). Informal urban development is often associated with weak regulatory systems, uncontrolled expansion, insecure planning processes, and persistent challenges in infrastructure and service provision (Ewnetu and Seo, 2025). Evidence from African informal settlements demonstrates that limited government coordination and weak institutional arrangements remain significant barriers to effective sustainable urban management (Baye, 2025). Furthermore, fragmented stakeholder relationships and competing interests among urban actors may reduce the effectiveness of urban planning interventions by limiting collaboration, policy implementation, and coordinated decision-making (Hamdan et al., 2021).

Transparency, accountability, and resource availability are all critical components of governance capacity, as they enhance institutional effectiveness, public trust, and sustainable urban management. Transparent and accountable institutions make decision-making more legitimate, increase stakeholder confidence, and strengthen governance in urban management (Ortega-rodr and Licer, 2020). In addition, institutional resources, such as financial capacity, technical expertise, and administrative personnel, are required to implement sustainable urban strategies and maintain essential services (Alasiri et al., 2026). Resources and accountability may be limited in regulatory enforcement, infrastructure maintenance, and public service provision. These governance issues are particularly crucial in Mogadishu, where conflict legacies, displacement pressures, informal land systems, and low municipal capacity impact urban governance processes. Therefore, examining governance capacity is essential for understanding variations in urban outcomes within fragile and rapidly changing cities (Lancaster, 2014).

In this study, Institutional and Governance Capacity is conceptualized as having five dimensions. These dimensions reflect the institutional mechanisms needed for good urban governance, such as policy capacity, the ability to implement regulations, transparency of decisions, the capacity to mobilize resources, and coordination with other relevant actors at the urban level.

  • Institutional readiness

  • Transparency

  • Accountability

  • Resource availability

  • Stakeholder coordination

The dimensions are analyzed about urban outcomes as they relate to planning effectiveness, infrastructure, accessibility of services, and sustainable urban development.

2.5 Theoretical foundation

This study uses three interrelated theories: Urban Growth Theory, Sustainable Development Theory and Urban Resilience Theory. These theories are drawn upon to understand the relationship between sustainability dimensions in urban development and the role of institutional capacities in adaptation in a rapidly changing and fragile urban environment.

2.5.1 Urban growth theory

Urban Growth Theory provides an understanding of the spatial evolution of cities through demographic growth, economic activities, land-use change, and infrastructure development. Classical theories of urban growth focus on the close relationship between urbanization and population growth, job creation, and land-use transformation (Wheeler, 2003). However, conventional models of urbanization rely on the assumption that the land market is relatively efficient and that urban regulatory institutions and formal planning exist to direct urban sprawl. In developing and fragile urban settings, particularly where informal settlements, limited institutional planning capacity, and informal land development processes shape urban transformation, conventional models of formal urban planning may have limited applicability (Roy, 2007).

Urban development is not always a formal process in many Global South cities. Informal urbanization, however, emerges as an answer to limited institutional capacities, housing constraints, and unequal access to land. According to (Mcfarlane and Mcfarlane, 2012), informality should not be understood simply as the absence of planning but as a complex process through which urban spaces are produced, negotiated, and governed through social, political, and economic relations. Therefore, applying Urban Growth Theory to fragile cities requires moving beyond traditional assumptions of formal urban expansion by considering informal land tenure systems, weak regulatory enforcement, displacement pressures, and fragmented governance arrangements. This perspective is particularly relevant to Mogadishu, where urban transformation has occurred within a context characterized by conflict legacies, displacement pressures, and ongoing institutional change (Menkhaus, 2014).

2.5.2 Sustainable development theory

Sustainable Development Theory provides a foundation for understanding the relationship between environmental protection, social equity, and long-term development. Sustainable development emphasizes the need to balance economic progress, environmental conservation, and social wellbeing while ensuring that development processes remain viable for current and future generations (Mensah, 2019). In urban environments, achieving sustainability requires balancing economic development, environmental protection, and social inclusion. Urban sustainability scholars argue that cities must navigate the interconnected challenges of economic growth, ecological preservation, and social equity to achieve sustainable urban development (Campbell, 1996).

This theory is directly relevant to the incorporation of environmental and social sustainability planning in this study. Environmental planning is a response to the requirements of managing ecological resources, minimizing environmental risk, and ensuring sustainable land use. Social sustainability is based on equity, participation, housing security, and access to essential services (Dempsey et al., 2011). Therefore, Sustainable Development Theory offers a theoretical account for analyzing the correlation between the environmental and social planning dimensions and urban outcomes.

2.5.3 Urban resilience theory

Urban Resilience Theory explains how cities react to and adjust to complex environmental, social, economic, and institutional pressures. Resilience thinking focuses on the ability of urban systems to absorb shocks, adjust to conditions, and continue functioning in times of uncertainty (Paulo and Gomes, 2019). Resilience theory is based on adaptive governance, flexibility, learning, and institutional coordination, whereas traditional planning theories are more interested in controlling urban changes. However, urban resilience is not only based on built infrastructure but also on governance structures that can coordinate responses and deal with uncertainty (Meerow et al., 2016).

This is especially important in fragile cities facing ongoing pressures from conflict, displacement, the environment, and institutional fragility. Research on fragile and conflict-affected cities has shown that governance capacity and institutional adaptation play important roles in managing urban transformation in contexts of uncertainty (Muggah, 2014). With this in mind, institutional and Governance Capacity is relevant in relation to sustainable urban outcomes and is therefore an important dimension of Urban Resilience Theory.

2.5.4 Theoretical integration

Despite their different research backgrounds, these theories can be integrated to provide a holistic understanding of sustainable urban development in fragile and rapidly changing urban contexts by examining the interaction between spatial growth, environmental sustainability, social considerations, and governance capacity. Urban Growth Theory explains the spatial processes underlying urban expansion, land-use change, and the organization of economic activities within cities (Coblentz, 1966). Urban Resilience Theory explains how cities develop the capacity to withstand, adapt to, and recover from complex social, environmental, and institutional pressures through effective governance, adaptive capacity, and coordinated responses (Sharifi, 2018). These views collectively coalesce into the position that these urban outcomes are linked to the interplay between Environmental Sustainability Planning, Social Sustainability Planning, and Institutional and Governance Capacity, within rapidly urbanizing and fragile urban contexts.

2.6 Research gap

Many studies have contributed to the understanding of urban sustainability issues, such as informal settlements, environmental constraints, housing insecurity, and governance challenges. However, previous research has often examined these dimensions separately and has provided limited integrated explanations of how they collectively shape urban outcomes. First, the extensive body of research on urban growth has primarily focused on demographic expansion, spatial transformation, and land-use change, while giving less attention to how sustainability planning and governance capacities are associated with variations in urban outcomes, particularly in fragile urban contexts (Xin, 2025).

Second, sustainability research has focused on environmental protection and social inclusion, but there is less research that has investigated the interplay between environmental and social planning dimensions and institutional capacity in urban settings affected by conflict (Hsu et al., 2020). Third, while resilience studies emphasize the importance of adaptive governance and institutional capacity, there is limited empirical evidence of how governance capacity relates to outcomes in sustainability planning in rapidly urbanizing fragile cities (Almulhim, 2025).

This is especially relevant in Mogadishu, Somalia, where rapid urban growth occurs in the context of displacement, informal land development, weak regulatory enforcement, and institutional barriers. Practically, existing urban sustainability frameworks created primarily in stable institutional contexts may not adequately reflect the situation of fragile cities, which are characterized by the interplay of formal and informal systems. Therefore, this study aims to fill this void by creating an integrated framework that examines the relationship between Environmental Sustainability Planning, Social Sustainability Planning, Institutional and Governance Capacity and Urban Outcomes in Mogadishu, Somalia.

3 Methods and materials

3.1 Study design

This study adopted a quantitative, descriptive, and correlational approach to elucidate the relationship between urban sustainability planning and urban outcomes in Mogadishu, Somalia. A descriptive design was employed to describe the respondents’ perceptions of Environmental Sustainability Planning, Social Sustainability Planning, Institutional and Governance Capacity and Urban Outcomes. A correlational design was used to explore the nature and direction of the relationship between the study variables, whereas regression analysis was used to determine the extent to which the dimensions of sustainability planning relate to the differences in urban outcomes. To produce measurable and statistically testable evidence of the relationship between the dimensions of sustainability planning and urban development outcomes, a quantitative approach was deemed suitable. Mogadishu was chosen as the study area because of its rapid growth as a post-conflict city and its increasing problems of informal settlements, stress on infrastructure, environmental degradation, lack of provision of services, and institutional coordination issues.

3.2 Study area

The study was conducted in Mogadishu, Somalia, the largest city and the administrative, economic, and political hub of the country. Mogadishu has experienced rapid urban growth due to population growth, rural–urban migration, post-conflict reconstruction, and growing needs for housing, infrastructure, transportation, sanitation, waste management, public services, and a high rate of internal displacement. These conditions render the city a pertinent example of the relationship between sustainability planning and urban outcomes in fragile, fast-growing urban environments.

3.3 Study population

The target population consisted of the residents of Mogadishu, CSO members, NGO representatives, and stakeholders involved in urban development and service delivery in the city. Residents were included as they are the end users and face the outcomes of urban planning: the availability of services, sufficiency of infrastructure, quality of the environment, social inclusion, and livability. Representatives from NGOs and CSOs were involved because of their involvement in community development, urban service delivery, environmental management, settlement support, and CSOs were activities.

3.4 Sampling technique and sample size

The target population of this study comprised 933 individuals, including residents of Mogadishu, representatives of NGO and CSO, and urban sustainability, governance, community development, and service delivery. The sample size was calculated using Yamane’s (1967) formula with a 5% margin of error, yielding 280 respondents. The final sample consisted of 230 residents, 20 NGO representatives, and 30 CSO representatives. The districts chosen were Hodan, Wadajir, Daynile, Kahda, and Karaan and the residents were selected using stratified random sampling technique. The districts were chosen to reflect a variety of urban contexts, that is, formal settlements, informal settlements, and emerging peri-urban areas. The residents were classified into three strata according to settlement characteristics, and proportional allocation was used. Simple random sampling was used for the selection of respondents within each stratum to guarantee fair representation.

NGO and CSO representatives were selected using the purposive sampling method and were selected based on their experience and knowledge in the areas of urban sustainability, governance, community development, and service delivery for Mogadishu. Questionnaires were distributed to 290, but 10 were not completed, giving a 96.6% return rate of completed questionnaires that were analyzed. The following were included as participants: adults aged 18 years and above, residents of the selected districts, and representatives of NGOs and CSOs with relevant experience. Respondents who did not meet the criteria or who left out some sections of the questionnaire were excluded. This sampling method enabled the representation of various urban contexts and facilitated a reliable analysis of sustainability planning and urban outcomes.

The larger proportion of residents was justified because residents represent the primary beneficiaries of urban services and directly experience urban sustainability challenges, while NGO and CSO representatives were included as key informants to provide institutional and stakeholder perspectives on urban planning, governance, and service delivery.

3.5 Data collection instrument

A structured questionnaire was used to collect primary data. The questionnaire focused on measuring the perceptions of the respondents regarding urban outcomes and urban sustainability planning. It was mostly composed of items with fixed response options on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The questionnaire was designed to assess four main constructs: environmental sustainability planning, social sustainability planning, institutional and governance capacity, and urban outcomes. A structured questionnaire was used to gather standardized responses that were adequate for quantitative analysis.

3.6 Measurement of variables

Four constructs were explored: environmental sustainability planning, social sustainability planning, institutional and governance capacity, and urban outcomes. Four items were used to measure each construct (16 items overall), which were adapted to the context of Mogadishu. All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree (Table 1).

3.8 Data collection procedure

Information about the purpose of the study and the rights of the respondents as participants was provided before data collection. Questionnaires were administered to selected residents, NGO representatives, and members of CSOs in Mogadishu. The researchers provided clarification of the questions and ensured that the response was voluntary. The questionnaires were checked for completeness, consistency, and accuracy before data coding and analysis. Multiple data collection procedures were used to reduce common method bias. To minimize concerns about the evaluation, respondents were assured of their anonymity and confidentiality. The questionnaire items were designed in a neutral way, and the measurement items for different constructs were separated to minimize consistent bias. Respondents were also told that there were no “right” or “wrong” answers and that they should provide their true feelings. These procedures minimized the risk of systematic effects of the data collection technique on responses.

3.9 Development and administration of questionnaires

A questionnaire was created from the existing literature related to urban sustainability planning, governance capacity, and urban outcomes, from which measurement scales had already been validated. The measurement items were modified to reflect the context of rapid urban growth in Mogadishu, Somalia, while still retaining their theoretical meaning. The questionnaire was written in English and translated into Somali for easy accessibility and understanding by the respondents. A back-translation process was used to ensure consistency between the two versions. The instrument was reviewed by academic experts, and a pilot test was conducted with persons not included in the target sample to check for clarity, relevance, and comprehensibility prior to the actual survey.

Data collection was performed by trained enumerators who used questionnaires and administered them directly to the selected residents and NGO/CSO representatives. Many informal settlements in Mogadishu do not have formal addresses or population registration, which led to the use of field-based probability sampling. The selected districts were broken down into settlement clusters according to administrative boundaries and characteristics of local settlements. In each cluster, households were chosen by systematic sampling using predetermined routes and fixed interval selection of households by enumerators. A single adult respondent was selected from each household as eligible. The enumerators clarified when needed and remained neutral. All participation was voluntary, anonymous to the collector for academic use only, and confidentiality was maintained.

5 Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that planning for environmental sustainability, social sustainability, and institutional and governance capacity is significantly related to urban outcomes in the city of Mogadishu, Somalia. These results align with previous studies that have highlighted the need to address the challenges of sustainable urban development in an integrated manner, focusing on environmental management, social inclusion, infrastructure planning, and institutional capacity, rather than tackling them individually (Webb et al., 2018). Other studies propose that cities with high rates of urbanization require coordinated planning systems to confront environmental pressures, social inequalities, and governance issues (Seto et al., 2017). However, this study builds on the existing literature by offering empirical evidence from a fragile, post-conflict urban context grappling with rapid growth, displacement pressure, and institutional constraints in Mogadishu.

Urban outcomes had the highest correlation with Environmental Sustainability Planning. This is consistent with previous studies showing that unmanaged urban expansion contributes to inefficient land consumption, infrastructure pressures, environmental degradation, and increased challenges for sustainable urban development (Angel et al., 2011). Moreover, studies have shown that effective environmental planning, including land-use planning, sustainable resource management, and climate-responsive infrastructure, is essential for improving environmental sustainability outcomes in cities (Webb et al., 2018). In Mogadishu, with its high rate of urban growth and low infrastructure capacity, stronger environmental planning mechanisms represent an important dimension of sustainable urban development.

This study found that environmental sustainability planning, social sustainability planning, and institutional and governance capacity have significant relationships with urban outcomes in Mogadishu, Somalia. These findings are consistent with earlier research that has highlighted the need for an integrated approach to sustainable urban development that goes beyond the separate management of environmental, social, infrastructure, and institutional aspects (Amin, 2022). Additionally, the relative lack of community participation mechanisms found in this study indicates that participatory planning processes are still under-institutionalized in Mogadishu. This result is aligned with earlier studies that have stressed the importance of meaningful community involvement and engagement associated with stronger social sustainability, social cohesion, and the ability to respond to community needs through urban interventions (Fridman et al., 2021).

Institutional and Governance Capacity also played a significant role in urban outcomes, but was the least developed sustainability dimension. This finding is consistent with earlier research, which has shown how weak governance systems, limited institutional coordination, inadequate resources, and poor regulatory enforcement hinder the sustainable management of cities, especially in fragile contexts (Salvador and Sancho, 2025). The study of Mogadishu also points to the problems of land governance, informal settlements, weak capacity of the municipality, and a disjointed urban governance framework (Hassan et al., 2023). Therefore, strengthening transparency, accountability, stakeholder coordination, and institutional capabilities represents an important consideration for urban sustainability planning.

The correlation and regression results also indicate that urban sustainability challenges are multidimensional and require integrated planning responses. Urban resilience perspectives emphasize that cities require adaptive capacity and coordinated governance approaches to address complex environmental, social, and institutional pressures (Wang et al., 2025). The present study contributes to the literature by demonstrating the interplay of these sustainability elements in a precarious urban context. Unlike previous studies, which often focus on environmental, social, or governance issues separately, the present study offers a holistic empirical view of the interconnections of sustainability planning dimensions and urban outcomes in Mogadishu.

The results suggest that sustainable urban development in Mogadishu requires attention to land-use coordination, environmental management, inclusive service provision, institutional coordination, transparent governance, and meaningful community participation, to establish transparent governance, and involve the community meaningfully. The results are relevant to policymakers, municipalities, and development partners interested in enhancing the sustainability of Somalia’s cities and other rapidly urbanizing fragile cities.

5.1 Practical and policy implications

The findings of this study indicate that sustainable urban development in Mogadishu requires integrated environmental planning, inclusive service delivery, and stronger institutional coordination. The study further suggests that improving governance transparency, accountability, and implementation capacity is essential for enhancing urban resilience and sustainability in rapidly growing post-conflict cities.

5.2 Limitations of the study

This study was conducted in one city only, Mogadishu, Somalia, with findings that may not apply to other urban settings. The study also used a quantitative questionnaire based and self-reported, and this approach may not have been comprehensive enough to encompass deeper qualitative views on urban sustainability issues.

6 Conclusion

This study investigated the link between environmental sustainability planning, social sustainability planning, institutional and governance capacity, and urban outcomes in Mogadishu, Somalia. The results suggest that all three sustainability dimensions have significant links to urban outcomes, but that environmental and social sustainability planning were found to have stronger associations, and institutional and governance capacity remains an important but relatively limited sustainability dimension. The findings indicate that waste management, urban congestion, governance coordination, transparency and accountability, and community participation remain key challenges associated with sustainable urban development in Mogadishu.

The results demonstrate that the sustainability planning dimensions collectively provide strong explanatory power for variations in urban outcomes, including environmental quality, infrastructure conditions, social inclusion, resilience, and service accessibility. This research adds to the literature on sustainability in cities by proposing an integrated framework that considers sustainability challenges in fragile and post-conflict urban contexts. The results highlight the importance of coordinated land-use planning, climate-responsive infrastructure, better governance mechanisms, institutional collaboration, and meaningful community engagement within sustainable urban development in Mogadishu. These learnings can be relevant for other African cities and fragile cities facing similar sustainability and governance pressures.

7 Recommendations

The waste management system in Mogadishu should be institutionalized with the establishment of waste management programs in each district with public–private partnerships (PPPs), upgrading the urban drainage system, and enhancing land use regulations to control uncontrolled urban development. Community-based monitoring mechanisms need to be established to support community accountability and strengthen service delivery capacity. District administrations should create inclusive participation platforms that include vulnerable groups, such as women, youth, and people with disabilities, to encourage the participation of these groups in decision-making on housing, infrastructure, and public services as part of broader social sustainability efforts.

In light of the observed institutional and governance capacity issues, municipal authorities should build urban sustainability coordination mechanisms between government institutions, NGOs, CSOs, and the community to support policy implementation, transparency, and resource coordination. Development partners should invest in institutional capacity building and support investments in urban infrastructure that is resilient to climate change. In the future, research on economic sustainability, climate resilience, and smart urban governance in fragile and rapidly urbanizing cities is necessary.
