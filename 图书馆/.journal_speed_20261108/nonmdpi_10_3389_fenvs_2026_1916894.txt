College of Business, Australian University, Kuwait, Kuwait See more Article metrics View details Abstract Brackish groundwater is a strategic supply buffer for arid states reliant on energy-intensive seawater desalination, yet the operational condition of legacy well fields and the distribution-side limits on dispatchable volume remain understudied. Both are addressed here for Kuwait, where desalination supplies 92% of municipal water. A ministry-level record of 393 wells across five production fields was assembled, and a well-resolved screening linear program was formulated to maximize brackish blending subject to the 1,000-ppm upper total-dissolved-solids (TDS) limit of the applicable Gulf drinking-water standard and an aggregate pumping bound. The model resolves individual wells but represents the distribution side as a single aggregate capacity scalar; it is a screening model, not a hydraulic network model, and its optimum is an upper bound on what a station-resolved network could deliver. Current operations support 62.24MIGD of blending; all rehabilitation scenarios saturate the pumping bound at 90.70MIGD, a 1.46 (45.7%) uplift. The binding constraint is, therefore, distribution-side aggregate capacity, not upstream well availability. This finding is robust: a zone-partitioned re-solve yields 87.8 MIGD–90.70MIGD, and crediting the standby reserve shifts the binding constraint to the TDS limit; well availability never binds. Constraining dispatch to the nine documented pumping stations reduces the realizable volume to 55.08 MIGD (61% of the aggregate optimum) when each field feeds a single station, quantifying the cost of the full-connectivity assumption; the binding constraint stays on the distribution side in every case. A lexicographic formulation returns the same volume at lower delivered TDS, yielding a unique dispatch portfolio. The S0S3 transition increases the TDS-feasible substitution ceiling by 45.7% (a theoretical upper bound under cogeneration coupling), supporting SDG 6 targets 6.4 and 6.5. The TDS volumes reported represent TDS-feasible blending maximums for the distribution system and do not independently determine that the blended product will be safe for consumption. This diagnostic (i.e., comparing the rehabilitation well flow rates with the production flow rate of the existing firm distribution system) can also be applied to other arid-state utilities. Highlights Blending is limited by available distribution-system pumpage once mechanically failing wells have been returned to service. Restoring mechanically failing wells increases potential brackish blending by 45.7% (62.2490.70 MIGD), after which additional well restoration does not increase the overall brackish blending volume but rather may improve the water quality. The results were found to be robust: at no time did well availability become the limiting factor in this problem, regardless of whether standby pumps were included in calculations or if the distribution network was divided into separate zones. A generalization applicable to all arid-state utilities is, therefore, to first compare firm distribution-system production flows against the total potential rehabilitation flows for individual wells prior to proceeding with investments in repairing wells. The scope of this model includes resolving individual wells but aggregating them as one capacity on the distribution side. The model determines which end of the pipeline the constraint lies within; it does not simulate hydraulic behavior, and the optimum it reports is the upper limit of a station-resolved solution. 1 Introduction Arid states have a compounded water security problem due to increasing population and increasing per capita demand, heat extremes associated with climate that exacerbate summer peak load conditions, and slowly degrading supporting infrastructure that may be operating beyond its designed lifetime (UNESCO, 2019; Ghaffour et al., 2013). Desalination accounts for over 90% of the municipal potable water in the Arabian Gulf (Fanack Water, 2024b; WaterHQ, 2021). Consequently, brackish (low-salinity) groundwater resources are generally under-evaluated. Brackish water is commonly mixed with desalted permeate at the distribution point to enhance mineral content, decrease corrosion potential, and, from an environmental perspective, extend the volume of water produced by high-energy thermal and membrane desalination processes (Ghaffour et al., 2013; Missimer and Maliva, 2018; Mukhopadhyay and Akber, 2018). Due to the mixing function associated with this application, brackish well fields are a low-profile but significant component of the water–energy nexus in arid municipalities. Kuwait offers an exemplary case. The state has no perennial surface water bodies; natural water resources are limited to brackish groundwater stored in the Kuwait Group and Dammam Formation aquifers, with only small isolated freshwater lenses [1,000 mg/L total dissolved solids (TDS)] in the Raudhatain and Umm al-Aish depressions held as a strategic reserve (Fanack Water, 2024a; Kuwait Foundation for the Advancement of Sciences, 2020). Desalinated water provides approximately 9% of domestic and industrial demand and roughly 60% of the total water supply (Fanack Water, 2024b); the per capita water use (165 ) is among the highest globally (Tariq et al., 2022). Brackish groundwater from the Shagaya, Salibiya, Umm Qudair, Wafra, and Atraf fields, with well-head salinities typically 3,000 mg/L–9,000 mg/L (Fanack Water, 2024a; Rashid et al., 2022), is already blended with desalinated water at salinities below approximately 5,000 ppm to produce potable supplies, irrigate agriculture, and serve non-potable industrial uses (Mukhopadhyay and Akber, 2018; Al-Ruwaih, 2010). The aggregate impact of legacy infrastructure deterioration on this blending capacity has not, however, been quantified in the peer-reviewed literature. Our objective in this study is to provide that quantification through a linear-programming optimization that (i) encodes Kuwait’s brackish-well network at well resolution, (ii) couples per-well design and salinity attributes to the connected reservoir and pumping-station capacities, (iii) formulates and solves a blending optimization subject to a 1,000 ppm operational TDS and aggregate pumping-station capacity constraints, (iv) benchmarks the LP against a sorted-by-salinity greedy heuristic, and (v) maps the binding-constraint frontier with a sensitivity sweep over the pumping and TDS bounds. A polynomial salinity diagnostic with seed-stable cross-validation is retained as a feature-importance probe but is not used inside the LP because its modest out-of-sample accuracy is incompatible with the TDS-limit operational regime. The framing follows the Water and Wastewater Management section’s mission, and the study is positioned as a transparent, interpretable optimization-based decision-support approach that integrates hydrostratigraphic salinity structure, real infrastructure constraints, and a machine-learning salinity diagnostic for water management: a concrete water-management practice (which well, how much, and at what blending ratio, under real infrastructure constraints) is being optimized from operational data rather than simulated abstractly. A deliberate methodological choice, the exclusion of the machine-learning surrogate from the decision loop (Section 4.6) because a measured-salinity LP is safer than a surrogate-fed one once a hard quality limit governs, is itself reported as a result. Climate variability enters only as a boundary condition in scenario stress tests of the proposed blending strategy. The article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on brackish-water blending, well-field optimization, and data-driven approaches to multi-aquifer groundwater systems. Section 3 details the study area, hydrogeological setting, brackish-water quality and blending rationale, and the operational and historical datasets used. Section 4 formalizes the LP, the polynomial salinity diagnostic, the greedy baseline, and the sensitivity sweep. Section 5 reports the network description, the five-scenario analysis, the greedy benchmark, and the sensitivity frontier. Section 6 discusses the supply-security, energy, and SDG 6 implications, and Section 7 concludes. 2 Literature review 2.1 Brackish-water blending in desalination-dependent systems Blending brackish groundwater with desalinated permeate is a long-standing practice in arid-zone municipal water supply, with documented operational and economic benefits in Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Israel, and Spain (Ghaffour et al., 2013; Missimer and Maliva, 2018). Al-Ruwaih (2010) determined “the best ratio between distilled water and brackish ground water” for blending for drinking purposes; they showed that the selection of an appropriate blending ratio allows the production of drinking water that meets the drinking-water standards while reducing the amount of fully treated water produced, and their results provide both the practical and regulatory context for the constraints incorporated into the optimization problem in this study. On a broader scale, brackish-water reverse osmosis has been shown to be a significantly less energy-intensive process than desalination of seawater, and its cost and energy requirements are a function of the quality of the brackish-water input (Pearson et al., 2021). In the Gulf region, however, much of the brackish-water resource is non-renewable; therefore, when determining whether brackish water should be used as either a blending component or a feed for desalination, the implications of long-term depletion of the underlying aquifers must also be considered (Al-Zubari, 2003). In terms of methodology, the closest precedent to the present work is Al-Zahrani and Ahmad (2004), who applied a stochastic goal programming (GP) model to determine daily production schedules for desalination plants located at water blending facilities in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia. At those blending facilities, desalinated water is blended with brackish groundwater drawn from several different well fields. Four main objectives were pursued in that GP model: (i) to satisfy customer demands, (ii) to maintain acceptable levels of salinity in the water being supplied, (iii) to avoid excessive depletion of the groundwater resources, and (iv) to maximize utilization of the available brackish water. These objectives have structural similarities to those of the present study. The primary differences are that (i) the earlier study solved its GP problem using stochastic techniques, whereas this study uses a deterministic LP, with demand being fixed at a single-scenario value of 520 MIGD; (ii) the earlier study represented the 1,000 ppm TDS threshold as a soft goal-deviation term, whereas this study represents that constraint as a hard linear inequality; and (iii) the earlier study did not couple the blending decision to an explicit distribution-side capacity scalar. The primary novelty claimed here is empirical rather than algorithmic: a 393-well, ministry-level census of asset condition (status, fault category, and root cause) paired with field-resolved salinity for a previously undocumented national brackish network, which the optimization then uses to localize the binding constraint. The optimization machinery itself is intentionally transparent (a deterministic LP rather than the stochastic goal program of Al-Zahrani and Ahmad, 2004, or the more general mixed-integer linear-programming (MILP) formulations of Thomas and Sela, 2024; Housh, 2023), so that the binding constraint, not the solver, carries the planning message. No comparable well-resolution asset-and-blending analysis for Kuwait’s multi-field brackish network exists in the published literature. Table 1 contrasts the two formulations. The Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) desalination sector provides the system-scale context for this blending strategy. Moossa et al. (2022) report a total GCC desalinated-water capacity of 97.2 MCM/d in 2020, accounting for approximately 40% of the global output, with an MSF-dominated but increasingly seawater reverse osmosis (SWRO) fleet facing mounting energy and brine-disposal costs. This scale makes brackish blending a strategically significant demand-side lever: each MIGD of brackish water dispatched into the distribution system displaces one MIGD of seawater intake, reducing the brine outfall volume and desalination-plant throughput in proportion (Missimer and Maliva, 2018). 2.2 Hydrogeology and long-term hydrochemistry of Kuwait’s production fields Long-term hydrogeochemical monitoring of Kuwait’s main brackish-water production fields (Rashid et al., 2022) documents progressive changes in groundwater chemistry, including increasing salinity and evolving major-ion composition over decades of pumping, which shows that extraction strategies must be coupled with quality-based constraints. AlAli (2008) provides a historical perspective on field development and abstraction over the period 1970–2008, classifying groundwater into fresh, slightly brackish, and brackish categories; together with the long-term hydrogeochemical record (Rashid et al., 2022), these works provide the conceptual hydrogeological backbone of the multi-aquifer system that is extended here with a well-resolved, aggregate-capacity linear-programming blending layer. The sustainability analysis of brackish abstraction from the Eocene aquifer in central Kuwait (Al-Eisa, 2020) provides a quantitative reference point for the upper bounds imposed on total extraction. The Salibiya (Al-Sulaibiya) field, the oldest and largest brackish field in the present dataset (111 wells, 21.81 MIGD), has received dedicated hydrochemical study. Alhumoud et al. (2010) reported a per-well time-series (1991–2005) for the Dammam Formation carbonate aquifer at Al-Sulaibiya, documenting TDS in the range 3,523 mg/L–9,460 mg/L across monitored wells, with Na–Cl and Ca– hydrochemical facies. This field-specific range is consistent with the mean salinity of 4,805 ppm encoded in the LP for the Salibiya sub-network and confirms that the Ministry workbook values are physically realistic. The study also establishes that the Dammam Formation at this field is a confined-to-semi-confined carbonate aquifer with significant spatial heterogeneity in TDS, a result directly supporting the decision (Section 4.6) to use measured rather than surrogate-predicted salinity. Complementary geochemical tracing of Kuwait’s coastal groundwater documents the evolution of acidic hypersaline water along flow paths (Sabarathinam et al., 2020), reinforcing that well-head salinity is spatially heterogeneous and must be respected at field resolution rather than averaged away. A current GCC-wide overview by Sherif et al. (2023) reports annual renewable desalination capacity of 26.4 billion across the six GCC states, average per capita consumption of 550 L , and persistently low treated-wastewater reuse fractions, confirming that the structural water-security gap motivating the present study is region-wide rather than Kuwait-specific. Beyond the Gulf, the mechanisms that govern groundwater-quality evolution (mineral dissolution, land-use forcing, and abstraction-driven enrichment) have been resolved in arid and semi-arid aquifers using integrated hydrogeochemical and geospatial methods (Jat Baloch et al., 2022a; b; Zhang et al., 2024), and contaminant fate-and-transport studies increasingly couple this geochemistry to explicit water-quality and health-risk assessment (Jat Baloch et al., 2025). These studies establish the field-resolved, geochemistry-aware treatment of groundwater quality that the present optimization operationalizes as a hard TDS constraint. 2.3 Well-field optimization The optimization of pumping schedules across networked wells has been studied for decades, originally in the context of agricultural irrigation and more recently for municipal supply (Maier et al., 2019). Classical approaches (linear and dynamic programming and mixed-integer formulations) remain widely used but struggle to capture the non-linear interactions between aquifer drawdown, well-head salinity, pump efficiency, and downstream blending constraints. Recent work has begun to integrate data-driven surrogates into the optimization loop (Rajabi et al., 2015), exploiting the ability of statistical and machine-learning methods to approximate response functions from historical operational data without requiring full 3D groundwater flow simulation; the present study retains a polynomial surrogate only as a diagnostic and instead relies on the LP for the dispatch decision. Mathematical-programming formulations remain the workhorse for operational water-network decisions: Thomas and Sela (2024) developed a MILP framework for general water-network operations, and Housh (2023) used MILP approximations to optimize multi-source supply systems that explicitly include a seawater-desalination array, a setting structurally close to the brackish–desalinated blending problem treated here. For arid regions specifically, Kizhisseri et al. (2022) showed that mixed-integer optimization can serve as a decision-support tool for national water-sector planning and policy. These works motivate the choice of a transparent linear program (rather than a metaheuristic or black-box optimizer) so that the binding constraint remains directly interpretable for planning. The broader engineering-systems-optimization literature is methodologically aligned with the present analysis along three axes: uncertainty quantification and error estimation for numerical models (Alrashed et al., 2020) parallel the sensitivity sweep used here to bound the binding-constraint result; performance-indicator design for complex engineered systems (Alrashed et al., 2022) parallels the decision-relevant metrics through which the rehabilitation scenarios are compared; and stochastically optimal control under input uncertainty (Alrashed et al., 2026) frames the deterministic-versus-stochastic modeling choice that distinguishes the present LP from the stochastic goal program of Al-Zahrani and Ahmad (2004). 2.4 Machine learning in water resources The water and wastewater management literature has seen rapid growth in machine-learning applications, including pipe-burst prediction, water-quality forecasting, and demand modeling (Liu et al., 2018). A highly active strand provides direct predictions of both groundwater salinity and total dissolved solids from measurable characteristics of wells (Tran et al., 2021; Elzain et al., 2024). Several machine-learning algorithms have been benchmarked for predicting salinity across a variety of multi-layered coastal aquifers (Tran et al., 2021), and optimized ensemble learning methods have been shown to predict levels of TDS in the groundwater of an arid coastal region (Elzain et al., 2024). These results are relevant here because they inform the development of the polynomial salinity diagnostic described in Section 4.6 and indicate the limitations of surrogate models under operating conditions constrained by a hard quality limit. In parallel, digital-twin and smart-water-grid concepts are being applied to expose how distribution-network topology and pumping constrain system efficiency (Ramos et al., 2023). The present study extends this line of work by applying a well-resolved, aggregate-capacity encoding of the brackish-well system to a desalination-blending objective while complementing the long-term hydrogeochemical record for Kuwait (Rashid et al., 2022; AlAli, 2008). 2.5 Water–energy nexus in desalination-dependent systems Seawater desalination is energy-intensive, with multi-stage flash (MSF) plants requiring substantially more energy per than SWRO, and with GCC cogeneration coupling to thermal power blocks introducing additional constraints on dispatchability (Ghaffour et al., 2013). Kuwait’s fleet is historically MSF-heavy; Alotaibi et al. (2025) reported from first-principles energy and exergy analyses of actual operating data for Kuwait’s three main MSF plants (Al-Doha East, Al-Zour, and Al-Sobia) that the modern plants achieve 17%–21% lower energy and exergy consumption than their 1970s-vintage predecessors, with the fleet sitting toward the upper end of the regional MSF range. Section 6 applies these Kuwait-specific plant benchmarks together with the SWRO range from Ghaffour et al. (2013) to translate the S0S3 brackish-blending displacement into a quantitative energy and carbon footprint, subject to the cogeneration caveat discussed there. The tight coupling between water production and the power system in desalination-based sectors has itself become a focus of systems analysis: Salomons et al. (2023) showed that electricity load-shedding programs propagate directly into desalination output, illustrating why the displacement estimated here is best read as an upper bound that depends on dispatch flexibility. Work published since 2021 has moved in three directions that bear directly on this study. First, blending has been treated increasingly as a formal optimization rather than a rule of thumb: Shalby et al. (2026) optimized blending ratios for brackish-groundwater desalination in Egypt’s Moghra aquifer so as to maximize the delivered quality while minimizing the desalinated volume required, which is structurally the dual of the objective used here. Second, the water-chemistry rationale for blending has been sharpened. Nativ et al. (2021) showed that desalinated brackish water requires deliberate mineral-composition management rather than simple dilution, and Lu (2023) provided computational methods for re-mineralization by blending; both bear on the observation (Section 4.5) that permeate at 50 ppm falls below the 100-ppm regulatory floor and must be blended or remineralized regardless of volumetric considerations. Third, the arid-state energy framing has been updated: Alenezi and Alabaiadly (2025) reviewed the emerging desalination technology landscape from a Kuwaiti perspective, and Elyaakouby and Tilioua (2026) optimized reverse-osmosis energy use in an arid North African setting, while Abdou et al. (2024) quantified the economic burden of water scarcity across the GCC. What none of these establishes, and what motivates the present study, is where the operational ceiling on blending lies in an existing national network whose assets have partly failed. 2.6 Knowledge gap Four gaps motivate the present work. First, the operational condition of legacy brackish well fields in the Arabian Gulf is not systematically documented in the open literature at well resolution. Second, the consequences of that condition for the energy intensity of municipal desalination have not been quantified at the well-network resolution and linked to Kuwait-specific MSF plant benchmarks. Third, capacity-aware optimization frameworks that jointly model blending ratios, rehabilitation priorities, and distribution-side capacity constraints across multi-field, multi-aquifer systems are absent from the Kuwait literature; the closest analog (Al-Zahrani and Ahmad, 2004, for Saudi Arabia) does not resolve the pumping-station bottleneck or operate at well-field resolution. Fourth, the empirical identification of when distribution-side capacity binds before upstream well availability (a finding that determines whether investment should be sequenced as wells-first, pumping-first, or co-staged) has not been performed at well resolution for any GCC network in the published literature. In this study, we address all four gaps using a ministry-level dataset for Kuwait spanning 1970–2026. The novelty claimed is, therefore, empirical and diagnostic rather than algorithmic, and it is worth stating precisely what is and is not new. The optimization machinery is deliberately ordinary: a deterministic linear program is chosen so that the binding constraint rather than the solver carries the planning message. What is new is (i) the asset layer, a well-resolved census of the operational status, fault category, and root cause for a national brackish network that has not previously been described in the peer-reviewed literature; (ii) the coupling of that asset layer to the documented distribution-side capacity schedule, which is what makes it possible to ask which side of the system binds; and (iii) the resulting diagnostic, that firm distribution throughput should be benchmarked against rehabilitated well capacity before well-repair investment is sequenced. Process-scale and regional studies without a single national setting are marked “—”. 3 Study area and data 3.1 National water context Kuwait is an extremely water-scarce country with no perennial surface water bodies; natural water resources are limited to brackish groundwater stored in the Kuwait Group and Dammam Formation aquifers (Fanack Water, 2024b; a). National water requirements are met primarily by three sources (seawater desalination, treated wastewater, and brackish groundwater), with desalinated water providing approximately 92% of domestic and industrial demand and approximately 60% of the total water supply (Fanack Water, 2024b; WaterHQ, 2021). Per capita water use is among the highest globally (165 ) (Tariq et al., 2022), and the country’s heavy dependence on energy-intensive coastal desalination plants is identified as a key vulnerability in long-term water security (Mukhopadhyay and Akber, 2018; UNESCO, 2019). The Water Action Hub places Kuwait’s brackish groundwater fields at a production capacity of order 545,000 (CEO Water Mandate/Water Action Hub, 2023). 3.2 Hydrogeological setting and well fields This study addresses five of Kuwait’s largest brackish-water production areas feeding the national distribution system: Shagaya (sub-fields A–E, Al Jahra-Alabdliya structural province), Salibiya (Sulaibiya), Umm Qudair (Umm Gudair), Wafra, and Atraf. All five draw on the multi-layered clastic and carbonate aquifers of the Kuwait Group and on the underlying Dammam carbonate formation. The hydraulic and hydrochemical behavior of the Dammam carbonate aquifer in Kuwait, and its compatibility with injected desalinated water, are established in the primary literature (Sayed and Al-Ruwaih, 1995; Al-Ruwaih, 1995; Mukhopadhyay et al., 1998), and that work underpins both the horizon assignments and the blending rationale adopted here. Shagaya, Salibiya, and Umm Qudair are among the largest brackish-water producing fields in the country; each has had numerous production wells drilled to contribute to both municipal and agricultural demand (Rashid et al., 2022; AlAli, 2008). Studies examining hydrochemistry have found a decline in water quality due to increased salinity and changing ion composition caused by long-term pumping, so quality must be considered when developing strategies for water production (Rashid et al., 2022; AlAli, 2008). The Wafra area in southern Kuwait is one of the country’s principal agricultural zones, where brackish groundwater is abstracted intensively for irrigation; the Atraf field, located in western Kuwait, is a large brackish-water field with on the order of 80 production wells developed in the Kuwait Group aquifer that is increasingly used to support municipal and industrial demand (Al-Eisa, 2020). The five Shagaya sub-fields were drilled between 1970 and 1972 to depths of approximately 1,150 ft–1,408 ft and draw on a mixed producing-horizon membership rather than a single clean horizon: of the 115 Shagaya wells, 69 are tagged to the Dammam carbonate and 46 to the dual horizon of the Kuwait Group (Table 3), with the dual-dominant sub-fields (A and E) being shallower-producing than the Dammam-dominant sub-fields (B, C, and D). Turning to the connected storage portion of the system, there are three primary components to consider: ground reservoirs (579 MIG aggregate capacity), elevated tower structures (9.9 MIG), and pumping stations providing a firm total flow rate of 90.7 MIGD together with a separately scheduled standby reserve of 66.6 MIGD. The standby reserve is held to cover station outages, maintenance rotation, and emergency situations rather than routine blending dispatch, and it is, therefore, not counted when determining whether the deliverable pumping bound has been reached: the binding scalar in the optimization is MIGD, the firm aggregate distribution-side throughput reported in the Ministry infrastructure schedule, which is distinct both from the standby reserve and from the well-head rated production of 166.30 MIGD. This figure is the sum of the firm-rated duty throughputs of all pumping stations associated with the brackish-water network as described in the workbook’s infrastructure sheet (firm duty = installed capacity less the largest standby unit per station); it is a single reported aggregate rather than an independently metered flow, and a station-resolved schedule would generally reduce it (Section 6, Limitations). Because the entire binding-constraint conclusion pivots on this single scalar, its value, its dispatchability, and the effect of crediting the 66.6-MIGD standby reserve are stress-tested explicitly in Sections 5, 6. Several national studies on the use of water resources note specifically that brackish groundwater with a salinity below approximately 5,000 ppm is already being blended with treated seawater to make drinking water available, and it is also used for irrigation, industrial cooling, and similar non-potable purposes (Mukhopadhyay and Akber, 2018; Al-Ruwaih, 2010). Earlier research has examined optimal blends of desalination-treated water and brackish groundwater from production fields in Kuwait, providing data on the mixes that allow drinking-water standards to be met using a reduced volume of fully desalinated water (Al-Ruwaih, 2010). These findings provide the practical and regulatory basis for treating field-level salinity and TDS thresholds as explicit constraints in the blending-optimization model: the operational TDS limit of 1,000 mg/L at the point of delivery enters as a hard linear constraint, and field-mean salinities (548 ppm–9,460 ppm in the present network) define the per-well coefficients of that constraint. Total dissolved solids is the master variable for both potability and blending suitability in arid-aquifer assessments (Jat Baloch et al., 2021; Saraswat et al., 2023), and the salinity gradients across the Arabian Peninsula’s Gulf aquifers are well-documented (Khan et al., 2022); each well is, therefore, classified by TDS into potable (1,000 ppm), slightly brackish, moderately brackish, and highly brackish bands (Section 5.2) to delimit the feed that can be safely blended. Because reliable major-ion analyses are not available for the full well set, hydrochemical-facies typing (e.g., the Na–Cl and Ca– facies reported for the Salibiya carbonate aquifer; Alhumoud et al., 2010) is taken from the published literature rather than re-derived here, and TDS is used as the operative geochemical constraint. 3.4 Operational and historical datasets Long-term trends in groundwater usage, abstraction rates, chemical evolution, and aquifer depletion across Kuwait’s primary groundwater production areas are covered in numerous previous studies and national assessments. Historical evaluations in particular (AlAli, 2008; Rashid et al., 2022) describe the development of Kuwait’s primary groundwater fields since the mid-twentieth century, including the growth of well infrastructure, changes in pumping regimes, and long-term trends in the salinity and major-ion concentration of these waters, indicating significant changes in both the quantity and the quality of groundwater under sustained extraction. Recent projects by national research institutions (Kuwait Foundation for the Advancement of Sciences, 2020) have focused on assessing groundwater-quality changes in key water fields, confirming increases in TDS and documenting spatial heterogeneity in quality within and between fields such as Shagaya, Salibiya, Umm Qudair, and Wafra. In this study, these published hydrogeological and hydrochemical characterizations are complemented and extended by an internal Arabic brackish-water workbook maintained by the Ministry of Electricity, Water, and Renewable Energy. The workbook collates well-level design specifications, depths, production capacities (IGD), and salinity records for individual wells across Shagaya (sub-fields A-E), Salibiya, Umm Qudair, Wafra, and Atraf for the period 1970–2026, together with associated storage-tank and pumping-station capacities. The national desalination capacities and production trajectories that set the demand context for the blending problem are documented in national water-resource overviews (Fanack Water, 2024b; Mukhopadhyay and Akber, 2018); the cost of desalination versus brackish supply is out of scope here (the LP optimizes physical blendable volume, not cost). The resulting integrated database provides the basis for the linear-programming blending-optimization framework, the polynomial salinity diagnostic, and the sensitivity analysis developed in this study. 4 Materials and methods 4.1 Data preparation The primary dataset is the Ministry of Electricity, Water, and Renewable Energy’s brackish-groundwater operational record, comprising (i) per-well design data: identifier, drilling date, producing horizons, depth (ft), rated production (IGD), pump capacity (IG ft), well-head salinity (ppm), and structural section; (ii) per-well operational data: status (working/standby/non-operational/abandoned/backfilled), fault category (electrical/mechanical/other), root cause (theft, unconnected, pump lowering, etc.), and responsible repair authority; and (iii) storage and pumping infrastructure: ground-reservoir and elevated-tower capacities, aggregate pumping-station throughput and reserve capacities, and blending delivery zones. The workbook is parsed and harmonized through an automated, reproducible pipeline. The Ministry workbook lists 485 documented brackish wells in total; 393 wells appear in both the design sheet (with depth/salinity/rated-IGD attributes) and the operational sheet (with status/fault attributes) and form the analytical sample. Three nested subsets of this sample are used downstream and are reported here once to avoid ambiguity: all 393 wells underpin the descriptive network statistics (Section 5); the 381 brackish-network wells (the analytical sample minus the 12 Raudhatain reserve) are passed to the optimization (rated capacity 164.08 MIGD); the inferential statistics (ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis, and correlations) use the 379 brackish wells with recorded salinity that fall in fields of ; and the salinity surrogate uses the 374 wells with complete depth, age, and salinity. The remaining 92 entries lack one of the two attribute sets (typically operational status undocumented for early-1950s Salibiya wells or design metadata missing for late-stage Atraf additions) and are excluded from the LP, which requires both salinity and operational status per well. The workbook also under-counts the Wafra field (only two wells in the analytical sample) because it captures wells tied to municipal-blending dispatch; Wafra’s larger agricultural-irrigation well stock is held in separate MEW abstraction-permit records not provided for this study, and the LP, therefore, models only the municipal sub-network at Wafra (see Limitations, Section 6). Across the manuscript, rated production (the sum of well-head design IGD, 166.30 MIGD for the analytical sample) is differentiated from aggregate pumping-station throughput (90.7 MIGD), which is a scalar derived from the Ministry infrastructure schedule and not from the per-well IGD of the wells within a given pumping station. Missing salinity entries are not imputed; the LP simply drops wells lacking salinity (the polynomial surrogate of Section 4.6 is retained only as a diagnostic, not as an imputer, given its modest cross-validated ). 4.2 Network encoding and model resolution The brackish system is encoded as an attributed well-set with capacity scalars: each well carries design parameters (depth, rated production IGD, and well-head salinity), operational status (working/standby/non-operational/abandoned), and a field tag; storage and pumping infrastructure are summarized as the aggregate capacities reported in Section 3. Because the distinction governs how every result below should be read, the model’s resolution is stated explicitly, and three levels are separated throughout this study: Well-level asset resolution (resolved here). Each of the 393 wells is an individual decision variable with its own measured salinity, rated capacity, operational status, and fault attribution. This is the level at which the present dataset is genuinely informative and is the study’s empirical contribution. Field- and zone-level aggregation (partially resolved). Wells carry a field tag, and the pumping schedule documents nine stations mapped to five operator delivery zones (Table 5). What the records do not contain is the well-to-station assignment, so the field, station, and zone cannot be joined into a supply topology. Hydraulic distribution-network model (not attempted). No pressure requirement, pipeline head loss, pump curve, reservoir refill rate, or inter-zone transfer restriction is represented. The model contains no hydraulics of any kind. Its purpose is to locate which side of the system binds, not to prescribe station-by-station operation. Two consequences follow and are carried into the principal conclusion rather than confined to the limitations. In the first place, since a capacity matrix for each individual station is generally more restrictive than the aggregate (some wells are hydraulically isolated from the zones requiring their water), the reported optimum is an upper bound on what a station-resolved formulation could deliver. In addition to the capacity issue of stations versus aggregate, there is the assumption of complete hydraulic connection: the LP does not account for hydraulic isolation between wells and zones, so water from any operational well is assumed to reach any zone at no cost. Rather than simply declaring that this assumption has value, its value is quantified in Section 5.8. 4.3 Pumping-station capacity schedule The aggregate upper bound MIGD imposed on the LP is the total of the firm duty capacities listed in the Ministry infrastructure schedule for each of the individual pumping stations, reproduced in anonymized form in Table 5. Since the entire basis for the conclusion regarding the existence of a binding constraint is predicated upon this single number, its origin is documented completely. (i) The schedule reports, for each station, a daily actual (duty) capacity and a separate reserve capacity; summing these respectively gives the 90.73 MIGD firm figure and the 66.59 MIGD standby figure. (ii) These are design/duty ratings carried in an infrastructure schedule and are not independently metered throughput: the Ministry workbook does contain a sheet for reporting daily well production, against which the ratings could have been checked, but that sheet was supplied empty, so no measured flow record was available to this study and none is claimed. (iii) The schedule is a single undated snapshot of the network as supplied by the Ministry for this study; no operating period, seasonal profile, maintenance derating, or simultaneity factor is annotated in the source, so the LP treats all stations as simultaneously available at their duty rating, which is the most permissive reading and again makes the reported optimum an upper bound. (iv) Two of the nine capacity rows carry no station name in the source and are reported as such. (v) The zone labels are the operator’s own; the asterisk annotation on three of them is carried in the source schedule, and its meaning is not defined in the documentation supplied. The World Health Organization sets no health-based guideline value for total dissolved solids, because no health effect is attributable to TDS as such; TDS is treated as an acceptability parameter governing taste, scaling, and corrosivity, with palatability rated good below 600 mg/L and poor in the 900 mg/L–1,200 mg/L band (World Health Organization, 2003; 2017). Earlier versions of this analysis described the bound as a “WHO potability cap.” That description was incorrect and has been withdrawn throughout. The applicable regulatory instrument is instead Gulf Standard GSO 149, Unbottled drinking water, which applies in Kuwait through the GCC Standardization Organization, of which Kuwait’s national standards body is a member. The edition currently in force is GSO 149:2021 (Edition 4, approved 1 July 2021), superseding GSO 149:2014 (GCC Standardization Organization, 2021). The edition text openly available to the authors is the notified final draft of the revision superseding GSO 149/2000, whose clause 5.2.3 specifies that “the content of solid substances soluble in unbottled drinking water shall range between 100 and 1,000 ppm” (GCC Standardization Organization, 2009a; GCC Standardization Organization, 2009b); the 1,000-ppm upper limit used here is that value. Two features of that clause matter here. First, it sits under the standard’s quality-related properties heading, alongside color, taste, odor, and pH, and not under the separate table of “chemical components affecting health”—so the standard itself classifies TDS as an acceptability rather than a health parameter (GCC Standardization Organization, 2009a). Second, the range is two-sided: the 100-ppm lower bound means that permeate at ppm is itself non-compliant unless remineralized or blended, which is an independent regulatory motivation for brackish blending beyond volumetric substitution (Nativ et al., 2021; Lu, 2023). The LP imposes only the upper bound, since the delivered blend is above 100 ppm in every scenario reported. Because the 1,000 ppm figure enters the model as a constraint rather than as commentary, its provenance was audited directly against the issuing body’s own records rather than taken from the secondary literature. Three statements can be made with different degrees of confidence, and they are separated here deliberately. First, the identity of the instrument in force is confirmed from the GSO standards catalog: GSO 149:2021, Unbottled drinking water, Edition 4, a Gulf Technical Regulation approved 1 July 2021 under ICS 13.060.20, superseding GSO 149:2014 (GCC Standardization Organization, 2021). Second, the wording and—importantly—the placement of the dissolved-solids clause are confirmed from the openly accessible full text of the GSO-notified draft, where clause 5.2.3 sets the 100 ppm–1,000 ppm range under the quality-related properties heading of clause 5.1, while the separate clause 5.2 tabulates “chemical components affecting health” in Tables 1–6 and does not list dissolved solids among them (GCC Standardization Organization, 2009b). This is the documentary basis for treating the bound as an acceptability rather than a health criterion. Third, and stated plainly as a limitation: the consolidated text of the 2021 edition is distributed under license by GSO and its national member bodies and was not available to the authors, so the clause 5.2.3 value has been verified against the accessible edition rather than read off the 2021 document itself. The catalog entry publishes bibliographic metadata only and no clause-level content, so it neither confirms nor contradicts a change to the dissolved-solids range; the authors accordingly do not assert the 2021 wording verbatim, and utilities applying this framework operationally should obtain the licensed edition before using the bound in a dispatch decision. That residual uncertainty does not propagate to any result reported here, for a reason that is visible in the sensitivity analysis reported in Section 5.7. At the current aggregate pumping capacity of 90.7 MIGD, the pumping constraint—not the TDS constraint—binds at every TDS threshold in the 800 ppm–1,200 ppm sweep, and the optimal blendable volume is 90.70 MIGD across that entire range. The 1,000 ppm bound is, therefore, slack at the operating point: were the applicable threshold to differ anywhere within 20% of the value adopted, every headline quantity in this study—the five-scenario blending volumes, the 1.46 S0S3 uplift, and the finding that the binding constraint sits on the distribution side—would be unchanged. The TDS threshold becomes decision-relevant only above roughly 120 MIGD of pumping capacity, which is the regime the sensitivity sweep was constructed to map. Consequently, satisfying ppm establishes that a blend is TDS-feasible against the applicable acceptability limit. It does not establish potability: microbial, chemical, radiological, and operational hazards are governed by the risk-based framework of the WHO guidelines and by the microbiological, chemical, and radiological clauses of GSO 149, none of which are evaluated here (World Health Organization, 2017; GCC Standardization Organization, 2021). Every volume reported in this study is accordingly a TDS-feasible blending upper bound, and the words “potable” and “potability” are not used of the model output. Major-ion composition, hardness, sulfate, chloride, boron, and disinfection by-products would each require separate verification before any dispatch schedule derived from this screening model were treated as delivering drinking water. 4.6 Polynomial salinity diagnostic To test how far well-head salinity can be predicted from observable well attributes (both as a missing-value diagnostic and as a measure of how strongly hydrostratigraphy governs the geochemistry), two data-driven models are benchmarked under one identical, leakage-free protocol (five-fold cross-validation repeated over 10 random seeds, with all preprocessing fit inside each fold; ). Well-head salinity is strongly heteroscedastic across the production fields: the largest field variance exceeds the smallest by a factor of 78, and both Levene’s test (, ) and its Brown–Forsythe median-centered form (, ) reject equal variances decisively. The classical one-way test and Tukey’s HSD both assume homoscedasticity and are, therefore, not appropriate as primary tests here; earlier versions of this analysis reported them, and they have been replaced. On the saturated scenarios, the greedy and LP achieve the same blendable volume of 90.70 MIGD; on the baseline (S0), they tie at 62.24 MIGD. The LP’s value over the greedy is, therefore, not in total throughput but in flexibility: the LP can place dispatch on any subset of wells whose blended TDS satisfies the limit, whereas the greedy is locked to the lowest-salinity feasible portfolio. This is a useful diagnostic: it shows that the single-objective LP solution is non-unique under the present constraints: multiple portfolios saturate pumping at the TDS-feasible blend. To resolve the non-uniqueness substantively rather than merely diagnose it, a lexicographic two-stage LP is reported. Stage 1 solves the primary problem and records the saturated volume MIGD. Stage 2 re-solves with a secondary objective subject to (with MIGD, the LP-solver feasibility tolerance; this is six orders of magnitude smaller than the saturated volume and is, therefore, numerically equivalent to a strict equality at machine precision), picking the unique lowest-TDS dispatch portfolio that still saturates pumping. The station schedule (Table 5) shows that this pool is, in fact, divided across nine stations and five delivery zones, the largest zone holding 35.19 MIGD and the smallest 7.60 MIGD, with no single station exceeding 23% of the total. Because the well-to-station assignment is not documented, the effect of that division is bounded here by ensemble rather than asserted by assumption: the LP is re-solved with one capacity constraint per station instead of one pooled constraint, over 2,000 admissible assignments of wells to stations, and the resulting distribution of realizable volume is compared against the aggregate optimum of 90.73 MIGD. Two assignment regimes bracket the answer. When wells are allocated individually to stations in proportion to each station’s firm capacity (that is, the probability of assigning a well to a station equals that station’s share of total firm capacity), the aggregate upper limit is found to be very close to the actual maximum attainable: median 90.73 MIGD, fifth percentile 89.58 MIGD. In this regime, no capacity is stranded because every station receives a well pool much larger than its own duty rating; the S3-eligible rated well capacity, 146.09 MIGD, exceeds firm pumping capacity by roughly 60%. In contrast, when the well pool of an entire producing field is forced to feed a single station—a constraint grounded in physical reality, given that the fields are geographically separate and trunk-main connected—the realizable volume falls to a median of only 55.08 MIGD (fifth–95th percentile 39.41 MIGD–68.82 MIGD), which is 61% of the aggregate optimum. Put the other way, the aggregate formulation over-estimates deliverable volume by approximately 39% once supply cannot be freely rerouted between zones because capacity stranded in a zone whose local fields cannot fill it is capacity that does not exist. This is the quantitative content of the upper-bound qualification, and it is carried into the conclusion. Two points survive this correction, and they are the ones on which the study rests. First, although the numbers change with each draw, the direction of the finding is constant across all draws: distribution-side capacity, not well availability, limits the volume that can be delivered in 100% of the 2,000 station-resolved solutions, precisely because even the pessimistic field-clustered median of 55.08 MIGD lies far below the 146.09 MIGD of eligible-rated well capacity. Restricting connectivity, therefore, makes the distribution side bind harder, never less. Second, restricting connectivity sharpens the policy implications of this study rather than reversing them: if the realistic figure is closer to 55.08 MIGD than to 90.73 MIGD, then pumping capacity is maldistributed among zones to a degree that is itself a first-order lever, and the return to re-balancing or interconnecting existing capacity may rival the return to installing more of it. Neither quantity can be evaluated without the well-to-station connectivity record, which is accordingly the primary data request this study makes of the operator. 6 Discussion 6.1 Pumping capacity, not well availability, is the binding constraint The principal finding of this study is that Kuwait’s aggregate distribution-side pumping-station capacity constrains the operationally achievable blendable volume under the LP formulation—not the upstream brackish well pool. Mechanical rehabilitation alone (S1) saturates the aggregate pumping capacity of 90.7 MIGD; subsequent upstream interventions (S2 electrical, S3 combined, and S4 plus new drilling) return the same blendable volume but draw on a progressively broader and lower-mean-salinity well pool, which the LP exploits to improve delivered TDS without increasing throughput. This claim is valid at the system level only, being derived from a single-scalar pumping abstraction (Section 4.2); in the real distribution network, pumping is per-station and per-zone. The station schedule is now reported in full (Table 5), and the cost of the aggregation is quantified in Section 5.8: the 90.73 MIGD figure is an upper bound, and a station-resolved network in which each field feeds one station realizes a median of only 55.08 MIGD. What remains unavailable, and what would be needed to replace the bound with a determinate value, is the well-to-station connectivity record. The aggregate pumping bound is identified as binding; which station-level physical bottleneck (motor capacity, hydraulic head loss, valve sizing, or upstream-tank refill rate) constitutes the proximate constraint at any specific station is not resolved here. The policy implication is, therefore, co-investigation of distribution-side capacity, with pumping the leading hypothesis, rather than a pre-committed investment in pumping motors alone. The implication for planning is nonetheless robust at the system level: brackish-well rehabilitation alone cannot push blendable volume above the network’s aggregate pumping throughput, and the marginal return of further upstream investment past the S1 saturation point is realized through TDS improvement and well-pool diversification, not through additional volume. Two features make this finding robust rather than an artefact of the optimization. First, the headline does not rest on the optimization machinery alone: the firm pumping throughput (90.7 MIGD) is well below the rehabilitated pool’s rated production (166.30 MIGD), so any reasonable dispatch rule (the LP or the sort-by-salinity greedy heuristic, which ties the LP on volume across all saturated scenarios) saturates the pumping bound. The LP’s distinct contribution is, therefore, not the saturated volume (the capacity comparison already implies it) but the dispatch portfolio: it guarantees TDS feasibility, and its lexicographic stage minimizes delivered TDS to yield a unique, reproducible well schedule (Section 5), the value a one-line greedy rule cannot provide once secondary objectives or feasibility guarantees matter. Second, the finding survives two explicit stress tests, both recomputed on the public dataset. (a) Spatial disaggregation. Partitioning the brackish fields into three coarse geographic distribution zones (north/Atraf, 27.4 MIGD eligible; central/Salibiya–Shagaya, 74.2 MIGD; and southwest/Umm Qudair–Wafra, 44.5 MIGD) and splitting the 90.7-MIGD firm capacity across them degrades the realizable blended volume only marginally (to 87.8 MIGD under an equal three-way split and 89.9 MIGD under a demand-weighted split, versus 90.7 MIGD when the split tracks each zone’s rated capacity) because every zone’s eligible rated capacity comfortably exceeds its pump share. That test used a hypothetical three-way geographic partition and hypothetical pump shares, and the reassuring conclusion does not survive the station-level schedule now available (Table 5). The stronger analysis of Section 5.8, which uses the documented per-station capacities, shows the shortfall is small only when wells can be assigned to stations individually; under the realistic constraint that a whole field feeds one station, it reaches 39%. The previous “at most 3%” statement is, therefore, withdrawn, and the aggregate optimum is treated throughout as an upper bound whose realistic value may be nearer to 55.08 MIGD. What the disaggregation does not do is change the direction of the finding, since the distribution side binds in 100% of station-resolved solutions. (b) Reserve crediting. Crediting the full 66.6 MIGD standby reserve (combined 157.3 MIGD) increases the optimum to 135.63 MIGD, but the binding constraint then shifts to the 1,000 ppm operational TDS threshold (delivered TDS 1,000 ppm), not to well availability: the 146.1 MIGD of eligible-rated well capacity remains slack even then. The robust, basis-independent statement is, therefore, stronger than “pumping binds”: well rehabilitation is never the binding lever. A downstream constraint (distribution-side pumping at firm capacity or the TDS threshold once the reserve is credited) limits blendable volume first across the entire realistic capacity range, so the qualitative conclusion does not hinge on the precise value of 90.7 MIGD. The sweep (Figure 8) is consistent, holding the regime pump-bound at every TDS level up to 140 MIGD and TDS-bound above. The LP-implied 1:1 displacement of seawater desalination per MIGD of brackish blending is a direct consequence of the demand equality with fixed at 520 MIGD: every additional unit of brackish dispatch mechanically reduces the desalinated-permeate flow by the same amount. The realized displacement in operation is bounded below this theoretical 1:1 figure: Kuwait’s MSF and SWRO plants are cogeneration-coupled to thermal-power blocks, so in summer, the desalination plants cannot dial back without curtailing electricity, and in winter, the inverse holds (Ghaffour et al., 2013). A key aspect of these results is that the S3 ceiling of 90.70 MIGD represents an upper limit on blending-side substitution under current pumping assets and does not represent an expected outcome of actual dispatch. In the context of Kuwait’s high per capita demand (165 ) and 92%-desalination dependence (Fanack Water, 2024b; Tariq et al., 2022), this maximum volume offers some degree of improved supply diversity; the planning implication, however, is that if Kuwait wishes to improve its water supply beyond what is possible with the existing pumping network, rehabilitation of the brackish groundwater resource will have to be coordinated with upgrading that network. 6.2 Theft and the political economy of asset loss Several characteristics of the dataset relate to electrical faults, and the most important is that most wells in the electrical-fault group have their primary reason for being unable to function attributed to theft, typically of copper cabling and switchgear: 165 wells are unavailable for this reason, which is the single largest documented failure category. These losses occur mainly in remote desert locations around Shagaya, Atraf, and Salibiya, where wells are spread over long distances, perimeter surveillance is poor, and copper has a ready resale market. The same pattern occurs among other utilities across the Gulf region and North Africa operating in similarly sparsely populated desert terrain. Inter-agency coordination required to prevent such theft involves three bodies: the Ministry of Electricity, Water, and Renewable Energy owns the assets, the Ministry of Interior is tasked with criminal investigation, and the Kuwait Municipality controls the land-use permissions needed for perimeter works; coordination among the three is a recognized bottleneck. An effective rehabilitation program must, therefore, include a security and asset-protection component (perimeter surveillance, cable burial, GPS-tagged copper, and rapid-replacement contracts for damaged equipment) rather than treating the wells as a purely technical refurbishment problem. Although the LP shows no incremental benefit from theft remediation in terms of additional volume, since pumping binds first, its marginal return appears instead as improved delivered TDS and a more diversified well pool; and the cumulative cost of repeated theft-driven outages remains a significant component of MEW operating expenditure that falls outside the LP framing of the present study. The binding-constraint finding also has a governance corollary: because the lever is distribution-side, the primary investment decision sits with whoever owns pumping and transmission capital rather than with the well-rehabilitation directorate, so capturing the uplift requires a single budget line that co-stages well-rehabilitation and pumping-capacity upgrades and an institutional owner for that joint decision, which is a coordination problem of the same shape as, and partly overlapping, the three-ministry theft-prevention gap above. 6.3 The polynomial salinity diagnostic, and why it stays a diagnostic The depth-and-age polynomial explains only a small fraction of well-head salinity variance ( over 10 seeds; Section 5), an out-of-sample error of the same order as the 1,000-ppm TDS threshold. Imputation of missing salinities with such a surrogate would, therefore, inject TDS-constraint noise comparable in magnitude to the constraint itself, which is operationally unsafe. The gradient-boosted model that additionally encodes producing-horizon and field structure reaches , a roughly five-fold improvement, yet its cross-validated RMSE of 855 ppm still falls short of the accuracy required to feed the hard 1,000-ppm limit. From a methodological perspective, there are two lessons: (i) the salinity variability observed across the Kuwait Group and Dammam Formation aquifer systems is governed primarily by the producing horizon, structural position, and sub-field-scale flow paths rather than by the single-well covariates of depth and age, consistent with the hydrogeochemical record (Rashid et al., 2022; AlAli, 2008); and (ii) a surrogate capable of earning a place within the LP would require hydrostratigraphic features and a substantially higher held-out . The practical implication for operational decision-making is that decisions should rest upon measured rather than predicted well-head TDS, and that investment in real-time TDS measurement equipment at the operational subset of wells—such instrumentation being currently undocumented in the Ministry workbook—would yield a high marginal return. 6.4 Energy and carbon implications of the implied displacement The energy and carbon figures in this subsection are order-of-magnitude scoping estimates, not outputs of the optimization; a dedicated life-cycle assessment is out of scope and a deliberate next step. One MIGD (4,546 ) sustained for a year, therefore, corresponds to 12 GWh–19 GWh and, at a 0.65 e grid factor (gas-and-oil-dominated Kuwait grid), 8–12 e. Applied to the 28.46 MIGD rehabilitation increment, this is (as an upper-bound theoretical avoidance contingent on cogeneration dispatch flexibility) on the order of 340 GWh–530 GWh of avoided desalination energy and 220–345 e of avoided emissions; scaled to the full 90.70 MIGD displaced at S3, the figures are larger. The realized avoidance is bounded below these upper bounds by the cogeneration coupling discussed in Section 6 (§5.1): when MSF plants run to satisfy electricity load, dialing back their throughput is constrained, so a fraction of the brackish blending may not translate into MSF reduction in the same season. 6.5 SDG 6 alignment and policy implications The framework supports SDG 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation), specifically target 6.4 (water-use efficiency and sustainable withdrawals) and target 6.5 (integrated water resources management) (UNESCO, 2019; Eawag, 2024). Rehabilitation increases the dispatched brackish volume by the relative gain given in Equation 9where denotes brackish blendable volume (MIGD). This 45.7% (62.24 90.70 MIGD) is an increase in the volume of brackish water substituted for desalination (that is, in desalination-substitution capacity) and is expressly not the UN indicator-6.4.1 water-use-efficiency ratio (value added per of water used, US$ ), which this study does not compute. It is best read as a contribution to the sustainable-withdrawal intent of target 6.4 and to IWRM (target 6.5), not as an indicator-6.4.1 efficiency figure. Two sustainability qualifications attach to it. First, increasing abstraction from a largely non-renewable brackish resource (Al-Zubari, 2003) bears directly on indicator 6.4.2 (water stress, withdrawal relative to available resource), which would move adversely and is not quantified here. Second, the LP optimizes a short-run operational ceiling with no multi-year safe-yield constraint; whether sustained 90.70 MIGD abstraction lies within the fields’ renewable or prudent-depletion yield is out of scope but essential before implementation, and would enter the LP as an additional per-field annual-abstraction bound linked to Al-Eisa (2020). Four policy implications follow. (i) Brackish-well rehabilitation must be co-scoped with pumping-network capacity expansion; isolated well-rehabilitation programs will deliver no additional blendable volume beyond the S1 saturation point. (ii) Sequencing matters: because pumping binds before electrical rehabilitation makes a difference at the aggregate level, the marginal return of theft-remediation investment is realized through TDS improvement and lower-mean-salinity dispatching, not through additional volume. (iii) Security measures (cable burial and perimeter monitoring) should be treated as a co-investment with technical rehabilitation. (iv) The pipeline used here is well within current operational capability, requiring only the operational and design records already maintained by the utility plus modest computing resources. What is transferred to other utilities that are also in arid states is the diagnostic method and not the Kuwait-specific numbers: any such utility should compare its firm distribution-side throughput with its rehabilitated rated well capacity before sequencing investment in well rehabilitation. The only data necessary to begin the analysis are firm-versus-reserve pumping by zone, per-well salinity and status, and demand. That distribution-side capacity binds before well availability is an empirical observation for Kuwait, based upon its particular topology of pumping and demand, and must, therefore, be re-derived elsewhere. 6.6 Discrepancies, counter-readings, and what the result does not mean There are four areas of tension in these results that are stated plainly rather than smoothed over. The headline volume is not a forecast, and more blending is not the same as more water. The LP determines the maximum amount of brackish water to dispatch subject to fixed demand equality, so each additional MIGD of brackish water displaces a MIGD of permeate. It, therefore, does not produce additional supply, and it should not be interpreted as an incentive to produce more desalinated water; the correct interpretation is that a lower-energy source replaces a higher-energy one within a fixed delivery obligation. Whether that replacement is realized depends on cogeneration dispatch, which this model does not represent. The aggregate and station-resolved results disagree by almost a factor of two, and the disagreement is informative.Section 5.8 presents 90.73 MIGD under full connectivity and 55.08 MIGD under field-clustered connectivity. Instead of reporting only the favorable figure, both are carried forward: the first represents what the network could accomplish if capacity were perfectly shareable, whereas the second approximates what it can accomplish as constructed. The difference between them measures how much value is embedded in the topology of the network, and it is arguably a more actionable planning quantity than either endpoint. The salinity model succeeds and fails simultaneously. A gradient-boosted model achieving under random cross-validation would ordinarily be reported as a useful predictor. Under field-held-out validation, however, it performs worse than predicting the global mean. Both facts are reported because both are true of different tasks: the model interpolates within known fields but cannot extrapolate to new ones. A reader who took only the first number into account would draw the wrong operational conclusion, which is precisely why the surrogate is excluded from the optimization. Meeting a TDS threshold is a weak claim, intentionally. The 1,000-ppm constraint is an acceptability limit and not a health-based limit, and clearing it establishes only TDS feasibility (Section 4.5). A blend can satisfy that limit while still failing on sulfate, chloride, hardness, boron, or microbiological criteria, none of which are evaluated in this study. The model identifies where a volumetric ceiling lies; it does not certify water. 6.7 Limitations Twelve limitations should be noted. First, the well-head salinity record is single-time-stamp rather than time-series, so long-term aquifer-drawdown effects are not explicitly modeled, a known feature of the Kuwait Group and Dammam aquifers (Rashid et al., 2022; Al-Eisa, 2020). Second, the optimization assumes static aquifer response and a single demand scenario; coupling to daily peak-demand variation (the subject of a companion study) is left to future work. Third, the energy and carbon translation in Section 6 rests on plant-fleet-average intensities from Ghaffour et al. (2013); year-on-year fuel-mix variation and cogeneration coupling will modulate the exact magnitude of realized displacement. Fourth, the aggregate pumping-station capacity ( MIGD) and the municipal demand ( MIGD) enter the LP as fixed scalars; spatial heterogeneity of pumping load, per-station capacity allocation, and seasonal demand are not resolved here. The binding role of pumping capacity should, therefore, be interpreted as a system-level finding rather than a station-by-station prescription. The per-station duty and standby ratings underlying the aggregate are now reported (Table 5), and the effect of the aggregation is bounded in Section 5.8; what remains missing is the well-to-station assignment, without which a genuinely station-resolved LP cannot be built. Fifth, the Ministry workbook contains only two recorded wells in the Wafra field even though Wafra is operationally a principal agricultural abstraction zone. The workbook under-represents Wafra because it captures only the wells tied to municipal-blending dispatch; the agricultural-irrigation wells in Wafra are recorded in separate MEW abstraction-permit records not provided to this study. The per-field results for Wafra should, therefore, be read as municipal-only and not as a comprehensive Wafra-field assessment. Sixth, the Atraf field (81 wells, KW horizon) has the thinnest peer-reviewed hydrogeochemical context in the available literature: Al-Eisa (2020) addressed the Eocene (Dammam) aquifer at central Kuwait rather than the KW horizon at Atraf, and no Atraf-specific peer-reviewed hydrochemical time series equivalent to Alhumoud et al. (2010) for Salibiya was located. The Atraf salinity coefficients in the LP, therefore, rely most heavily on Ministry workbook values rather than independent published verification, although the field-mean Atraf salinity (4,593 ppm) sits within the multi-field range reported by Rashid et al. (2022). Seventh, the binding role of the pumping-capacity constraint was verified independently of the modeling narrative: every reported number is regenerated deterministically from the source operational records by the open analysis pipeline (fixed random seeds; Section 5.2), so the headline can be re-derived end-to-end from the raw data rather than taken on the authors’ word. Eighth, the raw Ministry workbook is restricted under MEW data-sharing policy; the anonymized per-well derived dataset and full analysis code accompanying this study (Data Availability Statement) nonetheless reproduce every reported figure, table, and statistic without access to the restricted source, so reproducibility does not depend on Ministry liaison. Ninth, the LP assumes full hydraulic connectivity across the brackish network: any operational well is treated as deliverable to any distribution zone at no additional cost. In practice, the Atraf (western), Wafra (southern), and Salibiya/Shagaya/Umm Qudair (central) fields likely feed different distribution zones, so the saturated 90.7 MIGD blendable volume is contingent on the absence of binding zone-isolation bottlenecks. A zone-resolved hydraulic model (out of scope for the present dataset) would identify whether the LP’s optimum is realizable in the operating network or whether additional inter-zone transfer infrastructure is required to capture the full uplift; Section 5.8 replaces that coarse test with the documented nine-station schedule and finds the degradation is negligible only if wells may be assigned to stations individually; if each field feeds a single station, the realizable volume decreases to a median 55.08 MIGD, i.e., 39% below the aggregate optimum. The saturated 90.7 MIGD is accordingly an upper bound throughout. Tenth, the TDS constraint is single-parameter (TDS only), and TDS is an acceptability rather than a health-based parameter (Section 4.5), so no reported volume constitutes a potability determination: for the Ca–-dominated Dammam waters, sulfate (WHO esthetic guideline 250 mg/L–500 mg/L) and total hardness can become the operative limit before TDS reaches 1,000 ppm, so the TDS-only threshold yields a water-quality upper bound on blendable volume in addition to the hydraulic-connectivity upper bound, and a major-ion-aware constraint would likely tighten the feasible set. Eleventh, the LP optimizes a short-run operational ceiling and imposes no multi-year aquifer safe-yield constraint; whether sustained 90.70 MIGD abstraction from these largely non-renewable fields is sustainable against recharge and depletion (Al-Zubari, 2003; Al-Eisa, 2020) is out of scope but essential before implementation, and it would enter the model as a per-field annual-abstraction bound (Section 6, Section 5.6). Twelfth, and following from this, the word “sustainable” is used in this article in the operational sense of sustaining delivered supply against a fixed demand, not in the hydrogeological sense of abstraction within safe yield. No aquifer safe-yield estimate, recharge budget, or drawdown trajectory constrains the optimization, and the fields concerned are largely non-renewable (Al-Zubari, 2003). A dispatch schedule that is optimal here could still deplete the resource, and nothing in these results should be read as evidence that the reported abstraction rates are sustainable in that stronger sense. 7 Conclusion A linear-programming optimization has been presented in this study, benchmarked against a sorted-by-salinity greedy baseline and a sensitivity sweep, for evaluating the supply-security consequences of rehabilitating legacy brackish-water wells in a desalination-dependent arid-state setting. Applied to Kuwait’s 393-well analytical sample across the five main production fields (Shagaya A-E, Salibiya, Umm Qudair, Wafra, and Atraf), the framework quantifies that current operations support 62.24 MIGD of brackish blending, that mechanical rehabilitation alone increases this to 90.70 MIGD, and that subsequent rehabilitation scenarios (S2–S4) deliver no additional volume because the aggregate pumping-station capacity of 90.7 MIGD becomes the binding constraint. Three qualifications belong in this sentence rather than in a limitations list. The model resolves wells individually but represents the distribution side as a single pooled capacity, so it is a well-resolved aggregate-capacity screening model, not a network-resolved optimization, and it contains no hydraulics. Its optimum is consequently an upper bound: with each field constrained to feed one of the nine documented pumping stations, the realizable median decreases to 55.08 MIGD, 39% below the pooled figure. In addition, the 1,000-ppm bound is an operational TDS acceptability limit (GSO 149), not a health-based potability criterion, so all volumes reported are TDS-feasible upper bounds rather than certified potable supply. The S0S3 factor of 1.46 (a 45.7% increase in dispatched brackish volume, not a water-use-efficiency ratio) increases the total theoretical seawater-desalination displacement to 90.70 MIGD at S3, of which 28.46 MIGD is the increment attributable to rehabilitation, and the remaining 62.24 MIGD is already displaced at baseline; the realized displacement is bounded below this theoretical ceiling by cogeneration dispatch flexibility in Kuwait’s MSF/SWRO fleet, which the present LP does not model. The central planning implication is that brackish-well rehabilitation, on its own, cannot unlock further blending-side displacement beyond the pumping-saturation point; distribution-side capacity must be co-scoped with upstream investment. This conclusion is the basis-independent core of the analysis: explicit zone-partition and standby-reserve stress tests (Section 6, Section 5.1) show that well availability never becomes the binding constraint; a downstream limit (firm pumping or the TDS threshold once the reserve is credited) always binds first, so the LP’s own deliverable is best read as the TDS-feasible, lexicographically-unique dispatch portfolio rather than the saturated volume, which a one-line capacity comparison already implies. Caution is warranted, since the binding-constraint finding rests on an aggregate-scalar abstraction of pumping capacity: the proximate physical bottleneck at the most-constrained station is not identified by the present LP and would require the well-to-station connectivity record, the one dataset whose absence most limits this study. Restricting connectivity does not, however, threaten the direction of the finding: it makes the distribution-side bind harder in 100% of station-resolved solutions. The policy lever is, therefore, co-investigation of distribution-side capacity, with pumping being the leading hypothesis, rather than a pre-committed pumping-only investment. Three candidate bottlenecks merit priority diagnosis: (i) pump-motor capacity at the highest-throughput stations on the Salibiya/Umm Qudair/Shagaya central corridor; (ii) head loss in the longest brackish-water transmission pipelines (notably the Atraf-to-central-zone link); and (iii) tank refill rates at the largest 579 MIGD of connected ground reservoirs, where high-frequency inflow throttling could limit dispatchable volume independently of the well or pump constraints. A two-model salinity benchmark shows that depth and age alone predict well-head salinity weakly , whereas a gradient-boosted model that adds producing-horizon and field features reaches , identifying hydrostratigraphy, not single-well covariates, as the predictive signal. Under field-held-out validation, however, both models fall below an intercept-only baseline ( and ), showing that the apparent skill is between-field interpolation and not transferable hydrogeochemical prediction. Neither is accurate enough to feed the hard TDS threshold, so measured well-head salinity is used directly in the LP. The methodology is aligned with UN Sustainable Development Goal 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation) targets 6.4 and 6.5; what can be transferred to other arid-state utilities is the diagnostic (benchmark firm distribution-side throughput against rehabilitated rated well capacity prior to sequencing investment) rather than Kuwait’s specific binding-constraint value, which must be re-derived from each utility’s pumping and demand topology.
