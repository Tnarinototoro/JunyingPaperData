The Education University of Hong Kong, Tai Po, Hong Kong SAR, China See more Article metrics View details Abstract Sustainable spatial planning is critical for reconciling rapid urbanization with low-carbon transitions, yet the spatial differentiation of transport carbon emissions (TCE) and its moderating factors remain insufficiently integrated into planning frameworks. This study examines how built environment factors influence TCE under the 5Ds framework in Guangzhou, China, using mobile phone signaling data, multi-source spatial data, and the GeoDetector model. We reveal three key findings. First, TCE exhibits a polycentric agglomeration pattern, reflecting finger-like urban expansion. Second, development intensity (FAR) is the strongest explanatory factor (q = 0.309), with the FAR–employment accessibility interaction showing the strongest synergistic effect (q = 0.323), validated as genuine nonlinear enhancement, indicating that density regulation and jobs-housing coordination must be planned jointly. Third, building height and housing price stratifications reveal monotonic gradient effects—FAR’s explanatory power increases from low-rise to high-rise areas and from low-price to high-price zones—demonstrating that uniform policies may exacerbate spatial inequities. Land surface temperature shows an inverted U-shaped moderating effect, highlighting the need for climate-adaptive planning. These findings support a differentiated, context-sensitive planning framework that integrates density regulation, jobs-housing coordination, and climate-adaptive design, moving beyond the conventional assumption that uniform-built environment interventions yield uniform carbon outcomes. 1 Introduction Global warming and climate change have positioned low-carbon transition, climate resilience, and sustainable development as core objectives for mitigating environmental degradation and promoting high-quality urban development. The transport sector ac-counts for 23% of global CO2 emissions, ranking second only to the industrial sector in 2019. Enhancing energy efficiency and optimizing the transport modal structure are direct pathways toward achieving urban CO2 emission reductions. As the world’s largest CO2 emitter, China’s total CO2 emissions reached 11.13 billion tones in 2023 (accounting for 32.08% of the global total), of which the transport sector contributed 1.048 billion tones (9.42%). Concurrent with rapid urbanization, urban challenges such as unplanned urban sprawl, inefficient land development, and car-oriented urban planning have gradually emerged (Shi et al., 2018). Therefore, reducing urban transport carbon emissions holds significant and far-reaching implications for building low-carbon cities and mitigating global climate change. Addressing this challenge is fundamentally a spatial planning question: how urban land is developed, how activities are distributed across space, and how these spatial configurations shape travel behavior and carbon emissions—all of which fall within the purview of sustainable spatial planning (Creutzig et al., 2015; Geels, 2012). The built environment therefore provides an important spatial entry point for understanding and reducing transport carbon emissions, with empirical research indicating that improvements in built-environment conditions can contribute to lower travel-related emissions, although their effects may differ across urban spatial forms and socioeconomic contexts (Geng et al., 2026). The influencing factors of urban transport carbon emissions primarily include spatial and non-spatial factors. As a critical component for deconstructing urban spatial structure, the built environment has attracted considerable scholarly attention regarding its effects on transport carbon emissions. Cervero and Kockelman (1997) first proposed the classic 3Ds analytical framework, examining the impacts of the built environment on travel behavior from three dimensions: Density, Diversity, and Design. With the advancement of research, scholars have extended the 3Ds framework by incorporating two additional factors—travel distance and destination accessibility—resulting in the well-established 5Ds analytical model of the built environment (Ewing and Cervero, 2010). This framework has since become a fundamental theoretical basis for studying urban travel behavior. The 5Ds framework is adopted in this study because it provides a systematic and comprehensive set of dimensions—Density, Diversity, Design, Destination Accessibility, and Distance to Transit—that have been validated in numerous empirical studies as key built environment characteristics influencing travel behavior and transport carbon emissions. Its structured organization of multiple built environment variables enables a coherent comparison of their relative explanatory power, which is essential for identifying which dimensions matter most for TCE spatial differentiation. Moreover, its five dimensions cover the built environment attributes most consistently linked to travel demand and mode choice, making it particularly suitable for investigating transport carbon emissions in a complex megacity context. Despite the widespread application of the 5Ds framework, a critical gap remains in translating its insights into spatial planning practice. The 5Ds describe what built environment characteristics matter, but they do not address how these characteristics vary across different spatial contexts—such as central versus peril-urban areas, or high-income versus low-income neighborhoods—nor do they account for how local climatic conditions, particularly outdoor thermal environments, may systematically alter the effectiveness of built environment interventions. These contextual dimensions—spatial heterogeneity, socioeconomic stratification, and climatic adaptation—are precisely the core concerns of sustainable spatial planning, yet they have largely remained implicit in existing 5Ds studies. The scale-dependent and nonlinear relationships identified in existing empirical research further suggest that the effects of individual 5Ds indicators cannot necessarily be assumed to be spatially uniform. For instance, the influence of land-use mix has been found to vary with proximity to subway stations and city centers, indicating that the same built-environment characteristic may generate different carbon outcomes under different spatial conditions (Zhang and Zhou, 2025). Within the density indicator system, population density is the most commonly used metric to reflect the degree of spatial concentration. However, divergent views persist regarding its impact on urban transport energy consumption or travel behavior. Proponents of the compact city argue that population density exhibits a significant negative correlation with per capita urban transport carbon emissions (Newman and Kenworthy, 1989; Jarboui and Bouzouina, 2024). Conversely, other scholars contend that population density has a limited effect on urban transport energy consumption (Schimek, 1996; Gordon and Richardson, 1997; Karathodorou et al., 2010). In the Chinese context, Hong et al. (2022) found that the relationship between population density and urban transport carbon emissions is not directly negative, but rather exhibits a threshold effect at approximately one million population: when urban population falls below this threshold, population density is positively correlated with transport carbon emissions. This discrepancy is attributed to differences in population density and urbanization levels between China and other countries. Some researchers have employed residential density as a proxy indicator. Norman et al. (2006) argued that neighborhoods developed at low density generate higher urban transport carbon emissions compared to those at high density. In contrast, Shen et al. (2026) suggested that the impact of residential density on transport carbon emissions varies across regions with different levels of economic development. Among the indicators of land use diversity, the land use mix index has received the most attention. In urban land use systems, a single land use structure tends to increase urban travel demand (Kockelman, 1997; Brown et al., 2009), whereas higher land use diversity is generally conducive to reducing urban transport carbon emissions (Zhang et al., 2018). Through its type, intensity, and spatial configuration, the land use system interacts deeply with the transport system, thereby exerting direct or indirect effects on transport carbon emissions (Zhao et al., 2026). The relationship between land-use diversity and transport carbon emissions may also depend on the surrounding accessibility conditions. Zhang and Zhou (2025) found that the effects of land-use mix varied with distance to subway stations and city centers, suggesting that the carbon-reduction potential of mixed land use may differ across urban locations. Quantitative indicators of the design are intended to distinguish between pedestrian-oriented and automobile-oriented environments. These include neighborhood design metrics such as street grid patterns and entrance spacing, road network design metrics such as metro station density, intersection density, road network density, and proximal intersection density, as well as pedestrian street design metrics such as average street width, proportion of roads with pedestrian facilities, and green coverage rate. Among these, changes in road network morphology represent a defining feature of urban expansion, and road network density—as the most frequently used indicator by scholars—significantly influences automobile-oriented urban travel (Ewing and Cervero, 2010; Mohajeri et al., 2015; Hu and Wang, 2025). Higher road network connectivity is conducive to public transport, whereas widespread cul-de-sac and gated communities tend to encourage private car use (Marshall et al., 2014; Barrington-Leigh and Millard-Ball, 2017). A well-designed road network morphology is therefore of great significance for reducing road transport carbon emissions. With respect to destination accessibility, the distance between employment and residential land uses has received considerable attention. Lu et al. (2026) argued that promoting jobs-housing balance can effectively reduce travel demand and thereby lower urban transport energy consumption. Tong and Ma (2012) also emphasized that shortening the com-mute distance through the establishment of efficient public transport systems is critical for reducing transport carbon emissions. Cellular signaling data have also enabled the relationship between accessibility, land-use configuration, and emissions to be examined at finer spatial scales. Using more than 11 million cellular signaling records in Shanghai, Li B. et al. (2025) estimated commuting CO2 emissions and found that balanced job-housing distribution and moderate land-use mixing were associated with lower commuting emissions, while the effects of transit-related variables differed between residential and workplace contexts. Distance to transit has been consistently confirmed by existing studies to have a significant impact on carbon emissions (Ma and Chen, 2024). Ao et al. (2019) even suggested that only destination accessibility exerts a significant direct effect on transport carbon emissions, while other built environment indicators influence emissions indirectly through mediating variables such as travel distance and trip frequency. Regarding the estimation or simulation methods for urban transport carbon emissions, panel data are widely used at the macro-scale (Gordon and Richardson, 1997; Lee and Lee, 2014; Muñiz and Sánchez, 2018). This approach offers ad-vantages such as data availability, strong policy relevance, and rapid, intuitive analysis; however, it suffers from limitations, including low accuracy and poor timeliness. At the micro-scale, travel diary surveys conducted over fixed time periods are commonly employed to obtain private vehicle energy consumption for different trip purposes (Liu and Shen, 2011; Ma et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2017). Nevertheless, this method requires large sample sizes, and studies are generally confined to neighborhood-level analyses or rely on community samples to estimate city-wide data, resulting in low acquisition efficiency and limited data resolution. With the advent of the intelligent era, mobile phone signaling data—as a product of the rapid development of mobile communication technologies—have offered new opportunities for urban space and travel behavior research, owing to their advantages of large sample sizes, high spatial coverage, and low collection costs (Wang et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2019). These data have become one of the most widely used types of transport big data in the 21st century (Yue et al., 2014). Their application has subsequently expanded from describing population movement and travel behavior to estimating transport-related carbon emissions. Gao and Zhao (2024) reviewed applications of mobile phone signaling data in jobs-housing relationships, travel behavior, and transport carbon emissions, highlighting their broad spatial-temporal coverage and large sample size while also noting the importance of methodological refinement and integration with other data sources. Li B. et al. (2025) demonstrated the feasibility of using cellular signaling data to estimate fine-grained commuting CO2 emissions and examine built-environment effects, while Yang et al. (2025) used high-precision signaling data to identify commuting characteristics, including travel modes, distances, and durations, and aggregate these characteristics at the parcel level. Mobile phone signaling data have also been used to identify collective activity spaces and their spatial heterogeneity, providing a basis for proximity-oriented urban planning (Li J. et al., 2025). The reliability of signaling-based mobility measures depends, however, on the characteristics of the data and the spatial and temporal scale of analysis. A comparison with household travel surveys showed that mobile signaling data can reproduce per-capita trip counts and origin–destination distributions relatively well at scales above the street level, but are less reliable for travel-time distributions and trip purposes because of incomplete location records and the influence of identification thresholds (Wang D. et al., 2024). Accordingly, combining mobile signaling data with complementary transport statistics and calibration procedures can improve their applicability to fine-scale transport analysis. In summary, research on 5Ds-based urban transport carbon emissions has been growing both domestically and internationally. Existing studies generally agree that the built environment indicators of the 5Ds framework influence residents’ travel behavior, thereby indirectly affecting urban transport carbon emissions. However, from the perspective of sustainable spatial planning, three critical research gaps remain. First, spatial heterogeneity is often overlooked in 5Ds applications. While the 5Ds indicators are widely studied, their explanatory power on transport carbon emissions is often assumed to be spatially uniform—yet urban areas differ substantially in development density and socioeconomic conditions. Whether and how these spatial contexts moderate the effectiveness of 5Ds-based interventions remains unclear, limiting the design of context-sensitive spatial planning strategies. Although mobile signaling and other human-mobility data have made it possible to examine travel and carbon emissions at increasingly fine spatial scales, existing applications have mainly focused on commuting behavior, individual travel characteristics, or specific residential and workplace contexts (Li J. et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). How these mobility data can be used to characterize the spatial differentiation of total intra-urban transport carbon emissions and its relationship with the full set of 5Ds indicators across an entire megacity remains less explored.Second, socioeconomic stratification—proxied by housing price—may shape travel behavior and carbon responsiveness, but existing studies have rarely examined whether this influence operates directly or indirectly through built environment fac-tors. This has critical implications for spatial equity in land governance, as uniform policies may inadvertently benefit higher-income groups more than lower-income ones—a concern that sustainable spatial planning must address. Third, outdoor thermal environments, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions, may systematically alter residents’ travel comfort and mode choice, yet this dimension has been entirely absent from the 5Ds literature. As climate change intensifies urban heat stress, understanding this moderating effect is essential for climate-adaptive spatial planning—a dimension that is central to the sustainable spatial planning agenda. To address these governance-relevant gaps, this study adopts a three-step analytical strategy within the 5Ds framework. Step 1 characterizes the spatial distribution and agglomeration patterns of intra-urban transport carbon emissions (TCE) using global spatial autocorrelation and hot spot analysis, establishing the dependent variable’s spatial structure. Step 2 identifies the dominant built environment factors driving TCE spatial differentiation at the full-sample level via the GeoDetector factor detector, and further examines synergistic interactions among factors—with the FAR–DEA (development intensity–employment accessibility) pair cross-validated by Generalized Additive Models (GAM) to ensure robustness of nonlinear detection. Step 3 moves beyond uniform-effect assumptions by stratifying the full sample along three contextual dimensions—building height (proxying urban location and travel time cost), housing price (proxying socioeconomic status and travel economic cost), and land surface temperature (proxying outdoor thermal com-fort)—to reveal how the explanatory power of built environment factors varies across spatial and climatic contexts. This sequential logic—from spatial pattern, to baseline drivers, to contextual moderation—directly maps onto the Results structure in Sections 3.1–3.3 and the analytical pipeline detailed in Methods Section 2.1. The contributions of this study are threefold. From a data perspective, we demonstrate the feasibility of integrating mobile phone signaling data with multi-source spatial data—including land use, housing prices, and land surface temperature—at a 500 m × 500 m grid resolution to map intra-urban transport carbon emission (TCE) patterns in a subtropical megacity. This data integration provides a replicable data processing workflow for other rapidly urbanizing cities. From an analytical/methodological perspective, we extend the conventional 5Ds framework by introducing a three-step analytical pipeline—spatial pattern characterization, baseline driver identification, and contextual moderation analysis—that systematically incorporates stratification by building height (proxying travel time cost), housing price (proxying travel economic cost), and land surface temperature (proxying outdoor thermal comfort). This design offers a replicable analytical template for examining context-dependent built environment–TCE relationships in other urban contexts. From a planning-policy perspective, our empirical findings collectively suggest that the effectiveness of built environment interventions is not spatially uniform but is systematically moderated by vertical development intensity, socioeconomic conditions, and thermal environment. These patterns point toward a differentiated, context-sensitive planning framework that integrates density regulation, jobs-housing coordination, and climate-adaptive design—moving beyond the conventional assumption that uniform built environment interventions yield uniform carbon outcomes. The findings of this study are expected to contribute to sustainable spatial planning in developing regions by providing empirical evidence for differentiated, context-sensitive, and climate-adaptive planning strategies—moving beyond the conventional assumption that uniform built environment interventions yield uniform carbon outcomes. Step 1 characterizes the spatial distribution and agglomeration patterns of TCE using global spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I) and hot spot analysis (Getis-Ord Gi*), establishing the dependent variable’s spatial structure. Step 2 identifies the dominant built environment factors driving TCE spatial differentiation at the full-sample level, employing correlation analysis, multicollinearity diagnosis, and the GeoDetector factor detector, supplemented by Generalized Additive Models (GAM) to cross-validate nonlinear interaction effects. Step 3 moves beyond uniform-effect assumptions to examine how contextual conditions—urban location (building height), economic development level (housing price), and outdoor thermal environment (land surface temperature, LST)—moderate the explanatory power of built environment factors. This stratification strategy directly addresses the spatial heterogeneity and climate-adaptation gaps identified in the introduction, enabling the derivation of context-sensitive planning implications. All spatial analyses are implemented at a 500 m × 500 m grid resolution, with a 1 km × 1 km robustness test to verify scale independence of the findings. 2.2 Study area and data sources 2.2.1 Study area Guangzhou is located in southern China and serves as an important city in the GBA. Research on urban transport CO2 emissions in Guangzhou is highly representative of megacities in East Asia (Figure 2). FIGURE 2 Study Area (Guangzhou) and its location in China. The city covers a total area of 7,434.4 square kilometers. By the end of 2024, Guangzhou had a permanent resident population of 18.978 million. It features a huge intra-urban commuting demand, with 4.154 million motor vehicles in total and 185 passenger cars per 1,000 people. The city’s total daily commuting population is approximately 11.56 million, with an average commuting distance of 8.9 km; 53.2% of commutes are within 5 km. In the central urban area, the shares of walking and cycling (including bicycles and electric bicycles), and private motorized travel (passenger cars, taxis, etc.), rail transit, and conventional buses are 54%, 25%, and 21%, respectively. The overall green travel proportion reaches 74.2%. 2.2.2 Data sources and processing Mobile phone signaling data were collected on a typical weekday in 2021; all data were anonymized and stripped of all personally identifiable information (PII) prior to analysis. This study complies with local data protection regulations. Data on the built environment, metro station entrances, bus stops, and road network distribution were derived from online digital maps. Urban travel structure data were collected according to the Guangzhou Annual Transport Development Report. Built environment data—including building footprints (floor count, footprint area), road networks, metro station entrances, and bus stops—were sourced from online digital map platforms. Road network density, distance to metro entrances, and distance to bus stops were calculated using ArcGIS 10.5 Network Analyst. Land use data were obtained from the 2021 Guangzhou Land Use Survey (government-administered, non-publicly available), providing parcel-level classification into residential, commercial, industrial, public, and other categories. Population density data were derived from government statistical yearbooks and disaggregated to grid cells through spatial join based on grid centroid location. Housing price data were scraped from Anjuke, a leading online real estate platform, comprising community-level average transaction prices (RMB/m2) for 2021. The Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) interpolation method was applied to generate a continuous surface of housing price distribution across all grid cells. Leave-one-out cross-validation of the IDW interpolation yielded a mean prediction error of −0.00001786 and an RMSE of 0.002185 across all 11,745 sample points, indicating negligible systematic bias and high predictive accuracy. Land surface temperature (LST) data were retrieved from the thermal infrared bands of Landsat-8. The retrieval procedure involved radiometric calibration, atmospheric correction, and estimation of land surface emissivity using vegetation indices combined with a linear spectral mixture model. The single-channel algorithm was then employed to derive the final LST product (Table 1). A grid is created by the fishnet tool of ArcMap 10.5. After excluding grids with no transport CO2 emissions, a total of 9,777 grids were included in the analysis. The calculation was performed in three steps. First, grid-level trip turnover was estimated. Mobile phone signaling data from China Mobile on a typical weekday in Guangzhou were obtained, and invalid records were cleaned. Using a 30-min threshold, user stay behaviors and travel behaviors were identified from the signaling data. Taking the grid as the basic spatial unit, daily trip data were aggregated into each grid to construct an Origin-Destination (OD) matrix. The primary fields of the OD table include: origin administrative district, origin grid ID, destination administrative district, destination grid ID, and trip count. For trip distance estimation, the geometric centroids of the origin and destination grids were used as inputs to the Amap (Gaode) routing API via web crawling to obtain the shortest road-network distance for each OD pair. Based on the total resident trip volume reported in the Guangzhou Annual Transport Development Report 2021, the trip volumes in the OD matrix derived from mobile phone signaling data were scaled proportionally to obtain calibrated grid-level daily trip volumes. The daily trip volume for each grid was recorded as Dj, where j denotes the grid index. Second, mode-specific trip volumes were calculated. The transport report provides the citywide travel mode structure, including the proportion of motorized trips in the total resident trips of each administrative district and the modal share of each major transport mode. Based on the district-level modal shares reported in the transport statistics, the trip volume by each transport mode within each grid was calculated by multiplying the grid’s total trip volume by the corresponding modal share of the district in which the grid is located. This approach assumes uniform modal shares within each district. It should be noted that the uniform modal share assumption introduces a degree of uncertainty to the grid-level TCE estimates. In reality, modal shares may vary within a district according to local road network density, transit accessibility, and land use characteristics—for example, grids near metro stations are likely to have higher public transit shares than those in peripheral parts of the same district. This within-district heterogeneity may affect the estimation accuracy of individual grid cells, although the overall spatial pattern of TCE—primarily driven by the grid-level distribution of trip origins—is unlikely to be fundamentally altered. For the density dimension, Floor Area Ratio (FAR) and population density (DPP) were adopted as intuitive indicators to characterize urban development intensity and population agglomeration, respectively. In general, the frequency and volume of urban travel are positively correlated with density-related indicators. For the diversity dimension, the Land Use Mix Index (LUM) was employed to capture the heterogeneity of land use types within a given spatial unit, calculated using the entropy method. In general, travel distance is negatively correlated with the diversity indicator. For the design dimension, Road Network Density (RND) serves as a proxy for the dominant functions of a district, as reflected in its street layout—industrial parks typically exhibit lower RND than central business districts. Previous studies have suggested that high-density road networks facilitate diversified land use functions. For destination accessibility, the Distance to Employment Area (DEA) was adopted, measured as the distance from each grid cell to the nearest industrial, commercial, or business land use, serving as a proxy for trip destination accessibility. For distance to transit, two indicators were employed: Distance to the Nearest Metro Entrance (DME) and Distance to the Nearest Bus Stop (DBS). Beyond a certain threshold, proximity to public transit stations substantially weakens residents’ willingness to choose greener, low-carbon public transport modes. 2.3.3 Subgroup classification: rationale and variables To test the hypothesis that the effects of built environment factors on TCE are context-dependent—rather than spatially uniform—the full sample was stratified along three dimensions representing distinct moderating mechanisms. These stratification variables were selected not as explanatory factors in the main model (they are not part of the 5Ds), but as contextual lenses through which the 5Ds–TCE relationship is re-examined. Urban location (building height) was proxied by the average number of floors within each grid cell, derived from building footprint data. Building height serves as an integrated indicator of land rent, capita accumulation, and urban centrality. The classification into low-rise (1–3 floors), medium-rise (4–6 floors), and high-rise (≥7 floors) follows the standard defined in the Unified Standard for Civil Building Design (GB 50352-2019).It distinguishes the verticalized core (high-rise, predominantly commercial and high-end residential) from the horizontal periphery (low-rise, often peri-urban or industrial). This classification captures gradients in travel time cost: in high-rise cores, residents face higher opportunity costs of commuting time, making them more responsive to built environment improvements that shorten trips. Economic development level (housing price) was proxied by interpolated grid-level housing prices (RMB/m2). Housing prices reflect neighborhood socioeconomic status and, by extension, residents’ disposable income and the relative weight of travel economic costs in their modal choices. The thresholds were determined using the quantile method (tertiles), with breakpoints derived from the empirical data distribution. Higher-price areas are expected to exhibit stronger TCE responsiveness to built environment interventions, as time costs dominate over fuel costs in mode choice. Outdoor thermal environment (land surface temperature, LST) was adopted as a proxy for outdoor active mobility comfort. LST was retrieved from Landsat-8 thermal bands. The classification thresholds were also determined using the quantile method (tertiles) based on the empirical LST distribution. This classification allows testing of whether built environment interventions are more effective under thermally moderate conditions—where walking and cycling are feasible and attractive—versus under extreme temperatures that discourage outdoor physical activity. All statistical analyses were conducted in ArcGIS, SPSS and RStudio. 2.4.1 Spatial autocorrelation and hot spot analysis To characterize the spatial structure of the dependent variable, Global Moran’s I was first computed to test for spatial autocorrelation of TCE across grid cells. The indices are computed by Global spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I) in ArcMap 10.5, where the spatial relationship is conceptualized as inverse distance, the distance method is Euclidean distance, and the standardization method is row. Hot Spot Analysis was then applied to identify statistically significant clusters of high TCE values. The indices are computed in ArcMap 10.5, where the spatial relationship is conceptualized as a fixed distance band, the distance method is Euclidean distance, and the standardization method is none. 2.4.2 Correlation and multicollinearity diagnostics While the GeoDetector model does not require strict independence among explanatory variables, we nonetheless examined the correlation and multicollinearity of the candidate factors using SPSS. This step was intended to reduce information redundancy and enhance the interpretability of the subsequent factor detection results. 2.4.3 GeoDetector model The GeoDetector is a spatial variance-based method that quantifies the explanatory power of a categorical (or discretized) independent variable on the spatial heterogeneity of a dependent variable, without assuming linearity (Wang et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2016; Wang J F et al., 2024). It is particularly suited for this study because (1) built environment–TCE relationships are known to be nonlinear, (2) it explicitly tests spatial stratified heterogeneity—the core of our subgroup analysis, and (3) it provides an intuitive q-statistic for both standalone and interactive effects. The core of the GeoDetector is the q-statistic, which measures the explanatory power of a factor for the spatial stratified heterogeneity of a dependent variable. The q-statistic is defined as 1 minus the ratio of the sum of within-stratum variances to the total variance. If a factor effectively explains the spatial differentiation of the dependent variable, the variance within each stratum will be smaller than the total variance, resulting in a higher q-value. The q-statistic ranges from 0 to 1, with a larger value indicating stronger explanatory power. The factor detector calculates the q-value for each individual factor, while the interaction detector evaluates whether the combined effect of two factors is greater than, equal to, or less than the sum of their standalone effects. Because the GeoDetector is based on variance decomposition rather than predefined functional forms, it does not assume a linear relationship between the independent and dependent variables, making it suitable for capturing nonlinear and interactive effects in complex urban systems. Data discretization. Since GeoDetector requires categorical predictors, all continuous variables were discretized using the optimal parameter method implemented in the GD package’s optidisc function in RStudio. Five discretization strategies (equal, natural, quantile, geometric, and standard deviation) were tested with interval counts ranging from 3 to 10. The explanatory power of a single influencing factor on TCE spatial differentiation is measured by the q statistic. Using the optidisc function in the GD package, the optimal discretization schemes—determined by maximizing the q-statistic—were as follows: FAR and LUM were discretized using the standard deviation method with 10 intervals each; DPP was discretized using the geometric method with 10 intervals; RND, DEA, DME, and DBS were discretized using the quantile method with 8, 9, 10, and 9 intervals, respectively. Factor detector quantifies the standalone explanatory power of each factor on the spatial differentiation of TCE using the q-statistic. Interaction detector evaluates whether the combined effect of two factors is greater than, equal to, or less than the sum of their standalone effects. In this study, the factor detector was applied first to the full sample (establishing the baseline hierarchy), then separately to each subgroup defined in Section 2.3.3. The interaction detector was applied following the same full-sample, subgroup logic. While the GeoDetector effectively identifies nonlinear relationships and interactions, its interaction detection is based on overlay stratification rather than a formal statistical test of nonlinearity. We employed Generalized Additive Models (GAM) as an independent cross-validation approach. GAM fits relationships between variables through smooth functions without pre-specifying functional forms, making it well-suited for detecting nonlinear effects (Wood, 2017). Because the GeoDetector q-statistic is known to be sensitive to the discretization scheme, different discretization methods and interval numbers may affect the factor ranking and interaction results. To assess the robustness of the results to discretization choices, a sensitivity test was conducted using an alternative scheme in which all seven variables were uniformly discretized using the natural breaks method with 6 intervals. 2.5 Robustness test To ensure the reliability and robustness of the analytical results, a scale sensitivity test was performed by reconstructing the analytical framework using a 1 km × 1 km grid as the basic spatial unit. The global spatial autocorrelation, Pearson correlation, hot spot analysis, and GeoDetector model were recalculated to verify the consistency of the spatial pattern and driving mechanism. Specifically, the stability of spatial agglomeration characteristics, the distribution of hot spots, the ranking of explanatory power (q-statistic), the dominant factor, and the significance level of each factor were comprehensively examined under different grid scales. 3 Results 3.1 TCE exhibit a polycentric agglomeration pattern The estimated total daily TCE in Guangzhou is approximately 19,700 t CO2. The largest hot spot (15 km radius) is not a single contiguous core but a corridor-type agglomeration belt formed by multiple sub-centers linked by north–south arterial roads. Unlike previous existing studies, previous literature has largely focused on the linear or nonlinear relationships between the number of urban centers and transport-related carbon emissions (Jung et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023; Wu and Li, 2024), as well as the impacts of inter-center distance on such emissions (Jung et al., 2022; Deng et al., 2026). However, few studies have paid attention to the carbon emission characteristics of transport corridors formed among urban centers. However, the Guangzhou case reveals a nuance that is easily overlooked: peripheral town centers have generated independent influence radii comparable to those of medium-sized cities, indicating that these sub-centers have crossed a critical mass threshold and evolved into self-sustaining emission poles rather than mere bedroom communities. This conclusion has also been verified in other studies (Jones and Kammen, 2014; Sun et al., 2020). The importance of formulating targeted carbon reduction policies for peripheral towns will only intensify as these areas continue to develop and mature. 4.2 Development intensity as the primary planning lever: explaining the FAR–DEA synergy through time cost and spatial matching The dominant explanatory power of FAR in the full sample (q = 0.309) corroborates compact city theory, yet its effect size exceeds that reported in comparable Western city studies (Ergas et al., 2016; Leibowicz, 2017)—likely attributable to Guangzhou’s extremely high population density and the associated agglomeration economies, which concentrate a large number of trip origins within high-FAR grids. More critically, the FAR–DEA interaction (q = 0.323) not only surpasses FAR’s standalone explanatory power but is also validated by GAM as a genuine nonlinear enhancement (ΔAIC = 204.38)—indicating that density without employment accessibility is inefficient density. The mechanism underlying this nonlinear synergy can be explained from two perspectives. First, travel time cost. In high-FAR areas (typically urban cores), residents face higher opportunity costs of commuting time, when employment is also nearby (low DEA), shortened travel distances make walking and cycling both physically and temporally feasible, inducing a nonlinear modal shift from private cars to active transport or public transit—a shift that linear models would fail to predict. Second, jobs-housing spatial matching. The spatial proximity of high-density residential areas to accessible employment reduces cross-district commuting demand (Zhao et al., 2011); when the two are disconnected (i.e., jobs-housing separation), long-distance cross-district car commuting becomes locked in by spatial structure, regardless of improvements in road network design or transit facilities. This explains why FAR’s q-value in low-rise areas is merely 0.044—development intensity must cross a certain critical threshold before the carbon-reduction amplifying effect of employment accessibility can be activated. This synergistic effect is not merely additive; it is inherently relational. High FAR without employment accessibility intensifies local trip generation but offers few alternatives to car-based travel, while good employment accessibility without sufficient density disperses trip origins over a larger area, making transit investment less viable. When both conditions coincide, the spatial structure appears to be particularly conducive to reducing trip distances and facilitating mode shift, although we acknowledge that additional behavioral factors may also play a role. Our finding thus refines the conventional jobs-housing balance discourse by demonstrating that the carbon-reduction benefits of employment proximity are contingent upon development intensity (Lu et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2011). 4.3 Spatial heterogeneity and thermal environment moderation: contextual differences in explanatory power and implications for climate-adaptive spatial planning The monotonic increasing gradient effects revealed by building height and housing price stratifications (Section 3.3.1) expose the fundamental limitations of uniform spatial planning policies. In low-rise/low-price areas, FAR’s explanatory power is virtually negligible (q = 0.044 and 0.230). In contrast, in high-rise/high-price areas, FAR is associated with over 40% of TCE spatial variation (q = 0.508 and 0.406). A plausible explanation for this contrast is that travel time cost may dominate mode choice for high-income groups—fuel prices exert limited economic constraints (Small and Dender, 2007), but time lost to congestion is highly salient. Thus, compact, mixed-use development in affluent cores effectively shortens commutes and promotes public transit/active travel uptake. This gradient effect carries important spatial equity implications: uniform density bonus policies or TOD may tend to favor high-income cores while leaving low-income peri-urban residents with little improvement in their carbon burden—a potential risk of green gentrification. Sustainable spatial planning should address this challenge through targeted infrastructure investment rather than one-size-fits-all density regulations. The inverted U-shaped LST pattern in LST-related explanatory power (Section 3.3.2) introduces an entirely new dimension to the 5Ds literature. All built environment factors peak in the 24.9 °C–26.3 °C band and decline at both lower and higher temperature extremes—a pattern we tentatively interpret as potentially related to travel comfort, although LST is a proxy for surface thermal conditions rather than a direct measure of human thermal comfort (e.g., UTCI, PET). Moderate temperatures make walking and cycling both physically comfortable and time-competitive, thereby amplifying the carbon-reduction effects of pedestrian-friendly design (RND), compact density (FAR), and transit accessibility (DME/DBS). When temperatures fall below 24.8 °C or rise above 26.4 °C, the discomfort of outdoor exposure increasingly outweighs the convenience of short trips, driving residents toward temperature-controlled private vehicles—systematically attenuating the effectiveness of built environment improvements. This finding carries an urgent climate-adaptation warning: as global warming intensifies extreme heat days in subtropical cities, the expected carbon benefits of spatial planning interventions may be systematically eroded (Georgescu et al., 2024). Future planning frameworks must incorporate climate-adaptive design elements—including green corridors, street shading, and building ventilation—not merely as thermal comfort measures, but as essential infrastructure for sustaining the explanatory power of the built environment for TCE. It should be noted that the LST data were retrieved from a single Landsat-8 scene acquired at approximately 10:00 to 10:30 a.m. local time, which may not fully represent thermal conditions during morning and evening commuting peaks. 4.4 Implications for differentiated, climate-adaptive sustainable spatial planning Synthesizing the empirical findings, we propose a three-tiered planning logic framework for sustainable spatial planning in developing-region megacities: Tier 1—Urban cores and high-income zones (high-rise, high-price, moderate temperature zones): Planning should prioritize intensification and jobs-housing coordination. The FAR–DEA synergy exhibits the highest explanatory power here (q = 0.581 in high-rise areas), indicating that TOD must be complemented by employment-oriented density—concentrating mixed-use development around employment sub-centers to shorten commutes. Specifically, in grids with FAR ≥0.58 (the 75th percentile of the full-sample distribution) and DEA ≤100 m (the 25th percentile), jobs-housing coordination can be most effective in reducing TCE. Density bonuses should be conditional on achieving jobs-housing balance, with compliance monitored through regularly updated indicators such as average commute distance, public transport modal share, and employment–residence proximity within each zone. Tier 2—Peri-urban low-density zones (low-rise, low-price): In these areas—where average building heights are typically below 11 floors and FAR explains only 4.4% of TCE variation—density regulation alone shows limited explanatory power. Planning priorities should shift from regulating FAR to improving transit accessibility (with targets such as DBS ≤195 m and DME ≤939 m for at least 60% of residential grids, based on the 25th percentile thresholds of the observed distribution) and establishing employment nodes in peripheral districts. The goal is not to force higher density but to reduce the spatial mismatch that locks residents into long-distance car commutes. This requires coordinated land-use and transport planning across administrative boundaries—a governance challenge often underestimated in Chinese planning practice. Tier 3—Climate-adaptive overlay for all zones: The inverted U-shaped LST moderation, with a peak in the 24.9 °C–26.3 °C band, suggests that thermal conditions should be considered as a planning parameter at the decision-making front end. However, it should be noted that this band was derived from quantile-based data stratification; the optimal temperature range for human thermal comfort may vary across populations and seasons. Future refinement through local resident thermal comfort surveys is recommended to better calibrate planning interventions. For areas projected to experience increased heat days, planning strategies should include: (a) increasing green coverage and shade-providing street trees to lower perceived temperatures; (b) designing ventilation corridors along prevailing wind directions to enhance natural airflow; and (c) installing cooling shelters (air-conditioned public spaces) along active travel routes to extend the thermal comfort window for walking and cycling. These measures are not ancillary to spatial planning but are integral to sustaining the carbon benefits of density and design interventions in a warming climate. 5 Conclusion This study examines the spatial differentiation of transport carbon emissions (TCE) and its built environment drivers under the 5Ds framework, using Guangzhou as a case study of a rapidly urbanizing megacity in a developing region. Integrating mobile phone signaling data, multi-source spatial data, and the GeoDetector model across 9,777 grid cells, we derive three main conclusions. First, TCE in Guangzhou exhibits a polycentric agglomeration pattern with three tiers of hot spots distinguished by influence radius (1∼2 km, ∼4 km, and ∼15 km), reflecting the city’s finger-like urban expansion along transport corridors. Peripheral sub-centers with populations exceeding one million have emerged as independent emission poles, indicating that carbon mitigation strategies in polycentric urban regions must be distributed across multiple centers rather than concentrated solely on the CBD. Second, development intensity (FAR) is the dominant explanatory factor at the full-sample level (q = 0.309), followed by population density (q = 0.170) and road network density (q = 0.140). Critically, the FAR–employment accessibility (DEA) interaction exhibits the strongest synergistic effect (q = 0.323), validated by GAM as genuine nonlinear enhancement (ΔAIC = 204.38). This confirms that density without employment proximity is insufficient—jobs-housing coordination is an indispensable complement to density regulation. Third, the effects of built environment factors are systematically moderated by spatial context and thermal environment. Building height and housing price stratifications reveal monotonic gradient effects—FAR’s explanatory power increases from 0.044 in low-rise to 0.508 in high-rise areas, and from 0.230 in low-price to 0.406 in high-price zones—exposing the spatial inequity risk of uniform planning policies. Land surface temperature exhibits an inverted U-shaped moderation (peak at 24.9 °C–26.3 °C), demonstrating that the explanatory power of spatial planning factors varies with thermally contingent and may decline under climate-induced heat stress. These findings support a differentiated, context-sensitive planning framework that integrates density regulation, jobs-housing coordination, and climate-adaptive design—moving beyond the conventional assumption that uniform built environment interventions yield uniform carbon outcomes. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis relies on a single weekday and a single Landsat-8 scene. This design cannot capture weekday-to-weekend travel variation, seasonal thermal comfort differences, or inter-annual trends. The inverted U-shaped LST pattern should therefore be interpreted as preliminary evidence based on one specific meteorological day, requiring multi-day or multi-seasonal data for confirmation. Second, LST showed moderate correlations with several 5Ds variables (particularly LUM, DEA, and DBS), suggesting some overlap between thermal stratification and built environment attributes. While these correlations do not indicate severe multicollinearity, the independent moderating role of LST cannot be fully isolated. Future research using more direct thermal comfort indicators (e.g., UTCI, PET) could further disentangle these effects. Third, the IDW interpolation for housing prices may smooth over sharp price discontinuities (e.g., major roads, rivers), potentially introducing minor measurement errors that could affect the reported gradient effects. Fourth, the q-statistic employed in the GeoDetector measures explanatory power for spatial stratified heterogeneity rather than a direct causal effect. Endogeneity and reverse causality—for example, high-FAR areas may attract more trips rather than FAR itself causing emissions—cannot be fully excluded. The reported associations should therefore be interpreted as spatial explanatory relationships; causal inference would require longitudinal data or quasi-experimental designs. Fifth, the original building height classification resulted in unbalanced subgroup sizes (low-rise: 7,076; medium-rise: 2,141; high-rise: 560). Although our bootstrap and equal-sized tertile robustness checks confirmed the monotonic gradient pattern, differences in subgroup sample size and variance may affect the comparability of q-statistics across strata. The higher bootstrap means observed in all subgroups suggest that the original q-values may be conservative, though this pattern likely reflects sampling variation rather than a systematic bias. Sixth, the uniform modal share assumption may affect the estimation accuracy of individual grid cells, although the overall spatial differentiation pattern is unlikely to be fundamentally altered. Future research could refine the estimates by incorporating grid-level weighting factors such as road network density or transit accessibility. For future research, cross-city comparative studies in the Greater Bay Area, longitudinal data tracking planning reforms, and incorporation of subjective travel behavior and thermal perception data would further strengthen the empirical and theoretical foundations for climate-adaptive sustainable spatial planning in developing regions. In particular, larger samples in high-rise areas, finer-resolution housing transaction data, and multi-temporal thermal data could help refine the estimates and validate the contextual moderation patterns observed in this study.
