Keizersgracht Academy for Global Studies, Amsterdam, Netherlands Article metrics View details Abstract Ecological degradation has emerged as a critical challenge to achieving sustainable development, particularly in G20 economies where economic growth, social welfare objectives, and environmental constraints frequently interact. Using annual data for 17 G20 countries over 2000–2021, this study examines the effects of social welfare (SCWF), economic policy uncertainty (ECPX), trade openness (TROP), life expectancy (LIEX), natural resource rents (NARN), and tourism (TRSM) on ecological footprints (ECFP). Employing a Pooled Mean Group–Autoregressive Distributed Lag (PMG-ARDL) model, the analysis captures both short-run dynamics and long-run equilibrium relationships while accounting for cross-sectional dependence and heterogeneity across countries. The findings reveal that natural resource rents and social welfare expansion significantly increase ecological footprints, indicating that resource-intensive development and consumption-oriented welfare systems can undermine environmental sustainability. Life expectancy and economic policy uncertainty are negatively associated with ecological footprints. These estimates do not establish causal effects or demonstrate the influence of environmental regulation, which is not measured in the model. Trade openness exerts mixed effects, increasing environmental pressure in the short run but promoting ecological sustainability in the long run, whereas tourism consistently intensifies ecological degradation. These findings highlight the complex trade-offs between economic prosperity, social progress, and environmental protection in G20 economies. From an SDG perspective, the results provide important implications for achieving SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), SDG 13 (Climate Action), and SDG 15 (Life on Land). The study advocates integrated policy frameworks that promote sustainable resource management, environmentally responsible tourism, and balanced welfare strategies to support long-term ecological resilience and sustainable development. 1 Introduction Two important elements that endanger the well-functioning of human life are ecological deterioration and income dissimilarity. Experts in the domains of energy science, environmental studies, and research claim that climate variation is the primary driver of global warming and, as a result, a threat to the environment (Zafar et al., 2019). These phenomena have an immediate effect on people’s health and general quality of life. In an analogous way, economic inequality impedes efforts to advance social welfare and poverty alleviation goals (Hong et al., 2024). As a result, throughout several generations, wealth inequality and environmental quality have been widely recognized as fundamental challenges for humankind and roadblocks to sustainable growth (Chen et al., 2024). In 2015, the United Nations (UN) introduced a series of objectives known as the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) with the aim of tackling and mitigating the challenges. The objectives focus on economic, social, and environmental challenges through improving quality of life, reducing inequality, and eradicating poverty (Ahmad et al., 2020). Since their inception, there has been a dire need for strategic policy implementation and decision-making that encourage sustainable development in a variety of areas, including societal, economic, ecological, welfare, and fitness, with an emphasis on future peers (Uzar, 2020; Ali et al., 2021). Additionally, by consuming less energy and using natural resources responsibly, we can mitigate damage to the environment while also raising the standards of living, reducing poverty, and improving the wellbeing of society (Baloch et al., 2020; Uddin et al., 2020). The adoption of innovative technologies and clean energy sources that slow down the deterioration of the environment might help bring about positive changes, but it's crucial to keep in mind that increasing wealth levels can also cause fundamental shifts in the economic climate of a country (Uzar and Eyuboglu, 2019). Various measures, such as releases of greenhouse gases, carbon emissions, and ecological footprint, can be utilized to gauge environmental degradation. These figures are a result of the tremendous and unintentional increase in energy consumption and resource exploitation caused by globalization and rapid economic growth (Emre Caglar, 2020). The environmental consequences are exacerbated by the overuse of natural resources and fossil fuels, leading to a significant rise in carbon dioxide emissions. With a typical yearly increase of 1.1%, the amount of CO2 emitted globally increased substantially from 21,331.5 in 1990 to 34,169 in 2021. It is essential to bear in mind that relying exclusively on CO2 emissions as a measure of environmental impact is insufficient, since it fails to account for other critical resource reservoirs such as soil, forests, mining, and oil. To have a more precise understanding of environmental deterioration, it is important to consider the ecological footprint (ECFP). The ECFP evaluates the effects of human activity on the environment by contrasting human consumption with the ability of the ecosystem to regenerate (Zafar et al., 2019; Rafindadi and Usman, 2021). The global hectares required for the production of commodities and the absorption of waste are estimated taking into account the carbon footprint, croplands, oceans, forests, grazing area, and built-up land (Khan et al., 2021; Duan and Li, 2023). Moreover, Figure 1 illustrates the trends in natural resource rents (NARN) across the selected G20 economies from 2000 to 2021. The figure reveals substantial cross-country heterogeneity, with Russia consistently recording exceptionally high values, while Indonesia, Australia, Argentina, China, and several other economies exhibit noticeable fluctuations over time. A pronounced increase is observed for several countries around 2008, followed by a general decline, although country-specific variations persist throughout the study period. Overall, the figure highlights considerable differences in the level and temporal dynamics of natural resource dependence among G20 economies. Global economic growth is severely hampered by political uncertainty, which has recently been more prominent in environmental concerns. Fiscal, monetary, trade, and other pertinent policies are all included in this uncertainty, which is known as Economic Policy Uncertainty (ECPX) (Adedoyin and Zakari, 2020; Liu et al., 2025). ECPX is an institutional factor that shapes the surrounding commercial environment for economic beings, which in turn affects how economic institutions make decisions. Economic policy uncertainty measures uncertainty surrounding economic policymaking; it does not measure the stringency or effectiveness of environmental regulation. Its environmental implications may operate through investment, production, and energy demand, but these mechanisms are not directly tested in this study. According to a number of studies, the ECPX index, which measures uncertainty, can have a significant impression on economic movement (Baker et al., 2016). Environmental governance may receive less attention because of the increase in ECPX and the disruption of environmental protection laws. However, amid poor economic conditions, businesses may disregard environmental governance rules and rely more on less expensive conventional energy sources, which exacerbates environmental deterioration (Yun et al., 2026; Charfeddine and Mrabet, 2017). Further affecting environmental quality, ECPX can mark energy ingesting through price changes brought on by supply and demand shocks (Hailemar et al., 2019; Pirgaip and Dinçergök, 2020). ECPX can therefore either enhance or deteriorate environmental circumstances depending on a nation’s environmental policy. Environmental concerns continue to be a top global concern despite substantial study, attempts by legislators, and academic work. There is currently no broad agreement on the relationships between indices of human wellbeing, poverty alleviation, inequality reduction, and environmental degradation, although studies have recently investigated additional dimensions with this goal in mind. It has become difficult to discuss how income inequality, social welfare promotion, and environmental quality are related. Some research contend that environmental issues are social in character and are caused by income disparities, whereas other studies do not perceive a direct correlation between income disparities and environmental quality. Amartya Sen’s social welfare index, which takes GDP per capita and income disparity into consideration, is one of many measures of social wellbeing and income discrimination that have been presented in the literature. Social wellbeing is improved through lowering income variation and raising GDP per person. This index emphasizes how vital it is to boost a nation’s output to advance welfare and is attentive to how it is dispersed among the populace. The impact of uncertainties, such as debt catastrophes, monetary crises, wars, trade disagreements, and other global qualms, on environmental deterioration is also acknowledged in modern environmental literature. Due to these doubts, ECPX is receiving more attention. Empirical data emphasizes how important it is to take ECPX into account while conducting research on energy use and environmental quality. A country’s economic growth may also be hampered by energy conservation policies, according to some academics (Ma et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026). To sustain a consistent rate of economic development while also enhancing environmental quality, academics are looking into economic policies, laws, and regulations (Charfeddine and Mrabet, 2017). In the realm of excessive use of natural resources and renewable energy consumption, G20 countries face a multitude of energy-related issues. However, it is noteworthy that academics have mostly overlooked crucial elements such as social welfare, life expectancy, and trade openness when making forecasts in this domain. The lack of information in the literature is what spurs more research into the relationships between these parameters. Increased economic activity, urbanization, industrialization, and population rise have all boosted global energy consumption (Zeren and Akkuş, 2020). Energy is the driving force behind economic development and progress. The management of the world’s socioeconomic and environmental wellbeing heavily depends on the availability of energy supplies. The significance of affordable, contemporary, and dependable energy in advancing global welfare has been highlighted by the UN’s 2030 Development Goals (SDGs). The use of renewable energy, which represents a means of accessing contemporary, cost-effective, and reliable energy, has paramount significance in fostering economic growth (Akintande et al., 2020). Approximately two-thirds of our population, 90% of its GDP, and 75% of its commerce are represented by the G-20, a global group that promotes economic cooperation (Sheraz et al., 2021; Arvin et al., 2021). Both mature and emerging economies are included in this group. The G-20 economies, however, have not gotten enough attention in the context of the topic covered in this study. The present study examines these economies over 2000–2021. These countries are known for having the strongest economies in the world and for producing the most carbon emissions. Between the years 1991 and 2015, the G-20 accounted for more than three-fourth of the global economic output. During that period, their financial assets saw an annual growth rate of around 3.39%, surpassing the global average of 2.7% per year. Furthermore, the G-20 economies use the most energy worldwide and emit the most CO2 (Paramati et al., 2017; Wang and Luo, 2025). The G-20 economies are in charge of 78% of global greenhouse gas emissions, according to an environmental perspective (De Hoyos and Sarafidis, 2006). In 2018, energy demand and consumption grew, which resulted in an approximately 1.8% increase in CO2 emissions (Sheraz et al., 2021). This research aims to look at how ECFP affect environmental quality, specifically in G20 countries known for their high energy use and resource extraction. As shown by the works of (Jiao et al., 2021; Sharma et al., 2021a; Sharma et al., 2021b; Zhang et al., 2022) previous research has emphasized the significance of examining environmental quality in diverse economies, including those in Asia. Furthermore, Figure 2 presents the ecological footprint (ECFP) patterns across the selected G20 economies from 2000 to 2021. The figure shows substantial cross-country differences, with China exhibiting the highest ecological footprint, followed by the United States and India. Other economies maintain comparatively lower levels, although their patterns vary over time. Overall, the figure highlights the considerable heterogeneity in ecological pressure across G20 economies and the particularly large environmental footprint of major economies. The purpose of this study is to examine how social welfare, economic policy uncertainty, trade openness, life expectancy, natural resource rents, and tourism influence ecological footprints in G20 economies over the period 2000–2021. To achieve this objective, the study employs the Pooled Mean Group–Autoregressive Distributed Lag (PMG-ARDL) approach to estimate both short-run dynamics and long-run relationships. Before estimating the model, cross-sectional dependence, slope heterogeneity, panel unit-root, and cointegration tests are conducted to establish the relevant properties of the panel data. The PMG-ARDL estimator is particularly appropriate because it accommodates heterogeneous short-run responses across G20 countries while estimating their common long-run relationships. This study makes several important contributions to the existing environmental sustainability literature. First, it uniquely examines the combined effects of social welfare, economic policy uncertainty, trade openness, life expectancy, natural resource rents, and tourism on ecological footprints in G20 countries, offering a broader and updated perspective. Second, by incorporating tourism, a major but often overlooked factor, the study adds new insights into the environmental implications of global mobility and service sectors. Third, the use of advanced panel PMG-ARDL techniques alongside robust cross-sectional dependence and cointegration tests ensures greater methodological rigor. Overall, the findings help guide policymakers in designing targeted strategies for reducing ecological degradation while balancing economic and social objectives. In addition to its environmental and economic dimensions, this study holds strong relevance for public health policy. Life expectancy, a key variable in this research, serves as a proxy for population health and reflects broader health system performance, sanitation, and living conditions. By showing how environmental degradation and social welfare dynamics influence ecological footprints, our findings offer valuable insights for designing integrated health–environment policies. For instance, policies that reduce ecological burdens through cleaner energy use, resource efficiency, and sustainable tourism can directly improve air and water quality, which in turn enhance health outcomes. In G20 countries, where aging populations and healthcare systems are under increasing strain, aligning environmental strategies with public health goals is crucial. Thus, this study contributes not only to environmental sustainability discourse but also offers implications for preventative health policy frameworks, especially in high-emission and high-income contexts. Based on the research objective the following research questions have been designed. Q1: How does social welfare impact ecological footprints in G20 countries? Q2: What is the effect of economic policy uncertainty on ecological footprints in G20 countries? Q3: Does trade openness contribute to changes in ecological footprints in G20 countries? Q4: What is the relationship between life expectancy and ecological footprints in G20 countries? Q5: How do natural resource rents influence ecological footprints in G20 countries? Q6: What role does tourism play in affecting ecological footprints in G20 countries? Finally, Section 6 concludes the study by summarizing key insights and offering policy recommendations for enhancing environmental quality in G20 countries. 2 Literature review The unsettling consequences of human activity driven by economic and social progress are presently endangering the environment of the planet. The overuse of energy and natural resources, along with ecological neglect, has exacerbated these concerns. Legislators and environmental activists have tried, but failed, to address these issues. In addition, the prior environmental deterioration has been exacerbated by the misuse of natural resources, the decrease in poverty, the elimination of wealth inequality, the improvement of welfare, the growth of international commerce, and worries about health. In keeping with the goals of this research, we focus on five major relationships that have been noted in the environmental literature: the relationships between the environment and health status; the relationships between the environment and ECPX; and the relationships between natural resources, trade openness, income inequality, and the environment. The improvement of health outcomes and the decrease in mortality rates are shared global objectives. A common metric to evaluate a person’s health in terms of longevity is life expectancy. It is a reliable predictor of death and provides information on the population’s health. Commonly used as a gauge of population health is life expectancy at birth. LIEX is one of the most often used health indicators, even though health is a complicated term with many facets. Life expectancy is now being considered as a sign of health condition in recent years in environmental literature. A number of studies, including those by (Sharma et al., 2021a; Li et al., 2020; Saleem et al., 2022), contend that while increasing life expectancy promotes economic growth, it also worsens the environment. Other research, such as that by (Charfeddine and Mrabet, 2017), emphasizes the interconnectedness of healthiness standing and ecofriendly excellence, arguing that raising life expectancy can likewise raise environmental quality. Moreover, the intersection of public health policy and environmental sustainability is increasingly emphasized in interdisciplinary research. Environmental degradation, especially air and water pollution, directly contributes to respiratory diseases, reduced life expectancy, and higher public health burdens. At the same time, health policies that improve access to clean environments, regulate emissions, and promote healthier urban living conditions also contribute to ecological stability. Policies targeting environmental determinants of health—such as green urban planning, improved sanitation, and climate-resilient infrastructure—not only improve population wellbeing but also reduce long-term ecological pressures. As such, examining life expectancy in relation to ecological footprint provides a meaningful lens to understand how health-oriented policies can reinforce environmental sustainability goals in G20 countries, where healthcare infrastructure and environmental exposure risks are deeply intertwined. Both developed and developing nations agree that rising income levels and trade volumes are essential for promoting global economic development. Nevertheless, it's vital to take the environment’s effect of this growing economic activity into account. There are mainly two schools of thinking on how TROP affects the ecology (Shahbaz et al., 2019; Ahmad et al., 2016; Al-Mulali et al., 2015). make the case that free trade improves environmental quality by encouraging technological developments and strengthening environmental standards. However, some researchers, including (Pata and Caglar, 2021; Lv and Xu, 2019; Zamil et al., 2019) have shown that the expanding trade trend, which is fueled by economic expansion and increased energy use, has negative environmental effects (Shahbaz et al., 2019). research, for instance, a 1% increase in trade openness causes a 0.2470% rise in CO2 emissions, which is bad for Pakistan’s environment. In a thorough investigation involving data from 182 nations (Wang and Zhang, 2021), found that although trade openness has minimal effect on nations with lower middle-incomes, it progresses the state of the atmosphere in high- and high-middle-income nations. The ecology in countries with low incomes suffers greatly because of free trade, on other side. The study of natural possessions and how they impact environmental quality has drawn a lot of attention from scholars. Many academics think that nations endowed with a wealth of natural resources may produce and export a great deal, which would result in substantial long-term economic development. They also highlight the notable increases in resource extraction and energy use in these countries, both of which have unfavorable effects on the atmosphere (Ahmad et al., 2020; Hassan et al., 2019). On the other side, scientists that provide a contrary viewpoint, such (Khan et al., 2021; Fan et al., 2026), contend that natural resources eventually have a positive effect on environmental quality. They underline how natural possessions could help improve environmental conditions. Investigation of the bond between income dissimilarity and ecological quality have been conducted separately. The Gini coefficient has been used in this research as a simple indication of income distribution. While other studies looked at nations, others concentrated on examining the regional implications of income disparity. Disagreement exists, nonetheless, regarding the connection amid wealth inequality and ecological quality. These findings allow for a broad categorization of this research. According to the first category, growing income and wealth gaps drive environmental neglect, which has a detrimental effect on the ecosystem (Uzar, 2020; Baloch et al., 2020; Barra and Zotti, 2018). In contrast, a second set of studies contends that wealth disparity actually enhances environmental quality (Demir et al., 2019). According to the third group of studies, the distribution of income has little effect on the state of the environment (Zhang et al., 2023a; Adams et al., 2020). Throughout our analysis, it has become clear how closely connected health, social welfare, economic considerations, and the environment are. However, it's crucial to acknowledge that several political, medical, social, and economic concerns have surfaced recently, having a substantial impact on human life in several ways. Economic uncertainty has intensified because of events like the Second Gulf War in 2003 and the worldwide COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, which have an impact on enterprises and global economic activity (Zhang et al., 2023a). It is crucial to research these objections in environmental literature. Research that examines the effect of energy production and use on ecological quality has been assessed in this context. According to studies, ECPX has been found to have detrimental effects on the environment by raising CO2 emissions and deteriorating environmental quality (Pirgaip and Dinçergök, 2020; Charfeddine and Mrabet, 2017; Adams et al., 2020). By lowering CO2 emissions, ECPX has been shown by (Liu and Zhang, 2022) to enhance environmental quality. ECPX had the greatest effect on lowering CO2 emissions in the short term, according to another study by (Adedoyin and Zakari, 2020). To fulfill the objectives of sustainable development, it is essential to consider how economic activities may affect the environment (Xu et al., 2024). The research that is now available highlights the several factors that influence changes in environmental quality, including health, global commerce, resource depletion, income distribution, and reservations. The ECFP is a more comprehensive indication of ecological deterioration than CO2, which has been the primary tool employed in previous studies. However, this study takes a wider approach. This is because the whole extent of environmental deterioration cannot be adequately described by concentrating solely on-air pollution. Income difference has a considerable influence on ecological quality, and numerous studies have shown that this difference is the root cause of many environmental problems. In past investigations, various social welfare and economic dissimilarity indices were used. The social welfare index developed by Amartya Sen, which has more dependable theoretical foundations and incorporates more welfare maxims, is introduced in this work. The per capita GDP and income inequality are both considered to have an impact on social wellbeing by the index. The authors argue that Amartya Sen’s social welfare index is a valuable addition to the research because environmental literature has not given it much attention. ECPX is an institutional component that influences economic decision-making by changing the external business environment for economic organizations. Political uncertainty is in fact a huge concern for the global economy. ECPX’s effect on environmental quality must be considered given its power over economic institutions. Additionally, there is a direct association between ecological quality and health status. Because activities taken to enhance health may also have good consequences on the environment, it is crucial to examine this link. The present research intends to close knowledge gaps and improve the environmental literature by examining the interaction between social welfare, ECPX, and life expectancy on environmental quality. The ARDL panel is castoff in this research to investigate the connection between the variables under investigation and ECFP. This modeling approach facilitates the evaluation of both short- and long-term effects, leading to a comprehensive comprehension of the interactions between the variables. Tourism (TRSM) has emerged as a major driver of economic development but has also raised serious environmental concerns, particularly in relation to the ECFP. As international tourist arrivals increase, so does the demand for transportation, energy, land, and water resources, which intensifies environmental degradation (Campos-Soria et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2026). Studies such as (Menegaki, 2019; Munawar et al., 2022) argue that tourism activities, especially in countries heavily reliant on the sector, contribute significantly to the rise in carbon emissions, biodiversity loss, and ecological imbalances. The surge in mass TRSM has led to higher ECFP, particularly through aviation emissions, infrastructure development, and excessive resource consumption. Nonetheless, the relationship between TRSM and environmental quality is not entirely negative. Some research suggests that well-managed TRSM can enhance environmental protection efforts by funding conservation projects and promoting environmental awareness (Nathaniel et al., 2021). Eco-tourism, green tourism policies, and environmental certifications have been shown to mitigate the negative impacts of TRSM in certain regions (Razzaq et al., 2023). However, the success of such initiatives largely depends on strict regulatory frameworks, sustainable planning, and local community involvement. Without such measures, the environmental costs of TRSM often outweigh its benefits, especially in vulnerable ecosystems. Although extensive research has examined the determinants of TRSM, much of the existing literature has primarily focused on economic, energy, and demographic factors, often overlooking the evolving role of TRSM as a critical environmental driver. While studies have separately explored how trade openness, natural resource rents, and life expectancy affect environmental quality, limited attention has been given to a comprehensive framework that simultaneously incorporates tourism activities, social welfare, economic uncertainty, and natural resource dynamics in shaping ecological outcomes, particularly for the G20 economies. This gap is important because TRSM, with its rising contribution to both economic growth and environmental degradation, represents an increasingly significant but underexplored dimension. By integrating TRSM alongside variables like social welfare, economic policy uncertainty, and trade openness, this study offers a more holistic understanding of the complex factors influencing ECFP. Thus, it fills a critical void in the literature and provides timely insights for policymakers by using robust econometric techniques (PMG-ARDL, CIPS, and Westerlund co-integration) to design more comprehensive strategies for environmental sustainability. While numerous studies have explored environmental sustainability using CO2 emissions or other single-dimension indicators, our study makes a novel contribution by employing the ecological footprint (ECFP) as a more comprehensive metric. By using the PMG-ARDL framework along with the CIPS and Westerlund co-integration tests, we uncover both short- and long-run dynamics between economic, social, and environmental variables in the G20 context—something rarely done in previous literature. Importantly, our use of life expectancy and social welfare as explanatory variables connects this environmental analysis directly to public health outcomes, offering a more integrated approach than studies that isolate environmental or economic effects. This multidimensional framework allows for better policy design that spans environmental conservation and preventive public health strategy. Based on the literature discussions we have proposed the following research hypotheses. H1SCWF positively influences ECFP in G20 countries. H2ECPX negatively influences ECFP in G20 countries. H3TROP has a negative influence on ECFP in G20 countries. H4LIEX negatively influences ECFP in G20 countries. H5NARN positively influences ECFP in G20 countries. H6TRSM positively influences ECFP in G20 countries. The theoretical framework presented in Figure 3 integrates the social, institutional, economic, health, resource, and tourism-related determinants of ecological sustainability. It proposes that social welfare (SCWF), natural resource rents (NARN), and tourism (TRSM) increase ecological footprints through higher consumption, resource extraction, infrastructure expansion, and energy use. Economic policy uncertainty is hypothesized to be negatively associated with ecological footprints, potentially because uncertainty restrains investment and production. This mechanism remains untested and should not be interpreted as evidence of stricter environmental regulation. Trade openness and life expectancy are also hypothesized to have negative associations through cleaner technology transfer and greater environmental awareness, respectively; these explanatory mechanisms are not directly evaluated. These relationships form the basis of hypotheses H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, H6. FIGURE 3 Theoretical framework of the determinants of ecological footprints in G20 economies. 3 Research methodology The theoretical foundation of this study is grounded in the interaction between economic activities, social dynamics, and environmental outcomes. Social welfare improvements, while essential for societal development, often lead to increased consumption and resource exploitation, thereby exerting upward pressure on ECFP (Freeman et al., 2020). Whereas ECPX influences investment and production decisions, which can either curb or accelerate environmental degradation depending on the nature of responses from industries and consumers (Plepys, 2002). Moreover, TROP facilitates the flow of goods and services across borders; while it can encourage the adoption of cleaner technologies through exposure to international markets, it may also increase ECFP if it promotes resource-intensive exports (Udeagha and Breitenbach, 2023). Furthermore, LIEX reflects societal wellbeing and healthcare improvements, which often accompany higher environmental awareness and demands for cleaner environments, thus potentially reducing ECFP. On the other hand, NARN represents the economic benefits derived from resource extraction, and excessive reliance on these resources typically leads to environmental depletion and greater ecological burdens. Additionally, tourism contributes to economic growth but can escalate environmental pressure if not managed sustainably, particularly through increased energy consumption, waste generation, and land use changes (Yan et al., 2021). Hence, this framework supports the hypothesis that economic, social, and resource-related factors are intricately linked to environmental outcomes, justifying the investigation of their collective and individual impacts on ECFP in the G20 context. Moreover, the variables were chosen based on strong theoretical and empirical links to environmental sustainability. Therefore, SCWF, ECPX, TROP, LIEX, NARN, and TRSM directly influence ECFP through consumption patterns, policy dynamics, economic integration, health development, resource dependence, and environmental pressures. Moreover, the selection of G20 countries is grounded in their significant global environmental and economic impact. These nations represent approximately 85% of global GDP, 75% of international trade, and nearly 80% of global CO2 emissions, making them critical actors in the sustainability transition. Studying the G20 provides insights into the behaviors of the most influential economies in shaping ecological outcomes. Regarding indicator choice, the ecological footprint (ECFP) offers a more holistic environmental measure than CO2 alone, encompassing land use, carbon absorption, and resource demand. Natural resource rents (NARN) were selected to capture the economic value of resource extraction—a key driver of ecological degradation in resource-dependent economies. Trade openness (TROP) was included to reflect the role of global trade flows in both accelerating and potentially mitigating environmental pressures through technology transfer and green trade practices. Together, these indicators provide a multidimensional lens on sustainability performance in major economies. While alternatives like renewable energy use or environmental regulation exist, the selected indicators provide a broader socio-economic and environmental perspective necessary for analyzing G20 countries. The final estimation sample comprises 17 G20 countries: Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, France, Germany, India, Indonesia, Italy, Japan, Mexico, Russia, South Africa, Turkey, the United Kingdom, and the United States. Saudi Arabia and South Korea were excluded because consistent annual data for all study variables were unavailable over the full study period. The European Union was excluded as a separate unit because it is a regional aggregate overlapping with individually included member countries, which would introduce duplicate coverage. The resulting dataset is a strongly balanced panel covering 17 countries over 22 years (2000–2021), comprising 374 country-year observations. After accounting for the one-period lag used in the dynamic specifications, the final PMG-ARDL and CS-ARDL estimations are based on 357 observations. To reduce skewness, stabilize variance, and permit elasticity-based interpretation, all variables were transformed into their natural logarithmic forms before the econometric analysis. Specifically, ecological footprint (ECFP), social welfare (SCWF), economic policy uncertainty (ECPX), trade openness (TROP), life expectancy (LIEX), natural resource rents (NARN), and tourism (TRSM) were log-transformed. Consequently, the estimated PMG-ARDL coefficients represent elasticities, meaning that a 1% change in an explanatory variable is associated with a corresponding percentage change in ecological footprint, holding other factors constant. The descriptive statistics are reported in the variables’ original units, whereas the unit-root, cointegration, and PMG-ARDL analyses are based on their natural-logarithmic forms. Furthermore, cross-sectional dependence is particularly relevant for G20 economies because international trade, financial integration, global energy markets, coordinated policies, and common crises can transmit shocks across countries. The conventional PMG-ARDL estimator accommodates heterogeneous short-run dynamics but does not explicitly eliminate bias arising from unobserved common factors. It also imposes homogeneous long-run coefficients across countries. PMG-ARDL is retained as the baseline estimator because the PMG–MG Hausman comparison provides conditional support for long-run pooling. The PMG–DFE comparison concerns additional pooling restrictions and does not independently establish long-run homogeneity. These results provide empirical, although not definitive, support for using the more efficient PMG estimator. Given the significant cross-sectional dependence and slope heterogeneity, we additionally employ the CS-ARDL estimator as a robustness test. CS-ARDL augments the country-specific equations with cross-sectional averages of the dependent and explanatory variables and their lags, thereby capturing unobserved common factors and allowing greater heterogeneity in both short- and long-run relationships. Moreover, the CS-ARDL robustness analysis was conducted using Stata 18. The model included one lag of the dependent variable and contemporaneous and one-period-lagged values of each explanatory variable. Country-specific equations were augmented with contemporaneous cross-sectional averages of the logged dependent and explanatory variables and one lag of these averages to account for unobserved common shocks. The environmental effects of numerous factors in diverse places have been thoroughly studied in prior study (Jiao et al., 2021; Sharma et al., 2021a; Sharma et al., 2021b; Sharma et al., 2021c). By exploring the dynamic effects of variables including ECPX, SCWF, TROP, NARN, and LIEX on ECFP, we want to advance this body of prior research. It combines the strengths of both the Mean Group and Pooled OLS estimators. The research on panel stats recognizes the presence of interdependence between cross-sectional units. It is possible to trace this dependency to both undetected and shared shocks. Cross-sectional reliance is largely caused by elements like trade advances, globalization, monetary or commercial integration, and harmonization of economic policies. Cross-sectional dependence is a crucial issue that must be properly addressed since ignoring it might result in inconsistent and incorrect findings as well as lower estimating efficiency. While the methodology applied in this study offers robust insights into both short- and long-run dynamics, it is not without limitations. One potential constraint lies in the sensitivity of the results to the selected time period (2000–2021), which includes several global shocks such as the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. These events may influence the stability of economic-environmental relationships. Additionally, although the PMG-ARDL model effectively captures heterogeneous short-run effects and homogeneous long-run dynamics, results may vary if different lag structures or country groupings are used. Future studies could address these limitations by incorporating structural break tests or applying alternative models to different time spans for validation. Negative and positive values indicate footprint-reducing and footprint-increasing effects, respectively. 5 Discussion The empirical findings demonstrate that ecological sustainability in G20 economies is jointly shaped by social, economic, health, resource-related, and tourism factors. The results confirm that social welfare, natural resource rents, and tourism increase ecological footprints, whereas economic policy uncertainty and life expectancy reduce ecological pressure in both the short and long run. Trade openness produces a time-dependent effect: it initially increases ecological footprints but contributes to environmental improvement over the long run. These findings reveal that policies designed to promote welfare, resource-based development, international integration, and tourism may generate different environmental consequences across time horizons. The following discussion interprets these results in relation to the proposed hypotheses, theoretical arguments, and previous empirical evidence. Economic policy uncertainty is negatively associated with ecological footprints in both periods. A 1% increase is associated with a 0.276283% decrease in the long run and a 0.009803% decrease in the short run, significant at the 1% and 10% levels, respectively. This finding should not be interpreted as evidence that policy uncertainty represents stricter environmental regulation or directly causes improvements in environmental quality. One possible explanation is that greater uncertainty delays investment, production, trade, and energy-intensive economic activities, temporarily reducing resource consumption and ecological pressure. Alternatively, firms may respond to uncertainty by postponing expansion or adopting more flexible and resource-efficient production practices. However, these potential mechanisms are not directly examined in the present model and should therefore be regarded as possible interpretations rather than established causal channels. The result demonstrates a statistically significant association between ECPX and ECFP, while identifying the underlying mechanisms requires further analysis incorporating variables such as environmental regulation, investment, industrial production, and technological adoption. These results are consistent with previous findings (Pirgaip and Dinçergök, 2020; Zahra and Badeeb, 2022) and (Adams et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2019) but differ from the conclusions drawn by (Adedoyin and Zakari, 2020; Liu and Zhang, 2022), indicating that the relationship between environmentally conscious production and ecological footprint may vary depending on country-specific factors and methodological choices. Life expectancy is negatively associated with ecological footprints in both periods. A 1% increase is associated with a 3.599942% decrease in the long run and a 5.016623% decrease in the short run, significant at the 1% and 5% levels, respectively. This finding highlights that improvements in life expectancy contribute significantly to enhancing environmental quality across G20 countries. The stronger short-run effect suggests that immediate health and environmental initiatives may quickly translate into lower ecological pressures, while the long-run association reflects the cumulative benefits of sustained health improvements and environmental awareness. Over time, greater life expectancy may be associated with increased public consciousness regarding sustainable practices, as well as government efforts to provide cleaner environments that support an aging population. Several mechanisms may explain the observed negative relationship between LIEX and ECFP. The availability of clean water, improved air quality, and better healthcare services contribute directly to better health outcomes and increased longevity. As life expectancy rises, individuals may develop stronger preferences for environmental preservation and adopt behaviors that reduce their ecological impact. Furthermore, governmental policies aimed at safeguarding the health of senior citizens often include investments in cleaner, greener environments, reinforcing the link between improved life expectancy and reduced ecological footprints. Education and awareness campaigns promoting healthy, sustainable lifestyles further strengthen this dynamic. These findings are consistent with prior studies, notably (Charfeddine and Mrabet, 2017), and support the study’s H4 hypothesis that life expectancy negatively influences the ecological footprint within G20 economies. Natural resource rents are positively associated with ecological footprints in both periods. A 1% increase is associated with a 0.006541% increase in the long run and a 0.064921% increase in the short run, significant at the 1% and 10% levels, respectively. Although the magnitude of the long-run effect is relatively modest, the short-run coefficient reflects a more pronounced immediate impact, suggesting that short-term extraction and use of natural resources exert stronger environmental pressures. This consistent positive relationship underscores that the reliance on natural resource exploitation in G20 countries is detrimental to ecological quality, as it exacerbates environmental degradation through a rising ecological footprint. The findings are consistent with prior studies (Charfeddine and Mrabet, 2017; Hussain et al., 2023) that have similarly documented the environmental costs associated with natural resource dependence. Furthermore, the results provide empirical support for hypothesis H5, which posits that natural resource rents positively influence the ecological footprint in G20 economies. Social welfare is positively associated with ecological footprints in both periods. A 1% increase is associated with a 0.382430% increase in the long run and a 3.458317% increase in the short run, significant at the 1% and 5% levels, respectively. While this result may initially appear counterintuitive, it highlights the complex interplay between social welfare expansion and environmental quality. Several factors could explain the positive association. One possible explanation is that improvements in social welfare, particularly among lower-income groups, lead to higher consumption of goods and services, thereby increasing the demand for NARN. The subsequent rise in economic activities contributes to higher ecological footprints, placing additional stress on environmental systems and increasing pollution levels (Zhao et al., 2024). The differing short- and long-run impacts of natural resource rents (NARN) on ecological footprint can be interpreted through the lens of resource curse and institutional response theories. In the short term, increases in NARN typically fuel rapid industrial and extractive activity, leading to immediate spikes in ecological degradation due to deforestation, emissions, and unsustainable land use. However, in the long run, some G20 nations may develop mechanisms to manage resource wealth more sustainably—such as investing in green technologies, enforcing environmental regulations, or establishing sovereign wealth funds for environmental projects. Still, these adaptive strategies vary widely across countries, which may explain the relatively smaller (but still positive) long-run impact of NARN. This time-dependent behavior highlights the importance of governance quality and regulatory maturity in shaping how natural resource wealth influences sustainability outcomes over different time horizons. Thus, while the expansion of SCWF supports socioeconomic objectives, it may simultaneously exert unintended environmental pressures. Our findings align with previous research, notably that of (Demir et al., 2019), which similarly identified the environmental costs associated with social welfare improvements. Furthermore, these results provide empirical support for hypothesis H1, which posits that SCWF positively influences the ECFP in G20 countries. On the other hand, trade openness exhibits contrasting associations across periods. A 1% increase is associated with a 0.375924% decrease in ecological footprints in the long run and a 0.123323% increase in the short run, significant at the 1% and 10% levels, respectively. This divergence between short- and long-term effects can be attributed to different mechanisms operating over these periods. In the short run, the expansion of global trade tends to stimulate economic growth by boosting production, consumption, and industrial activities, all of which heighten the demand for energy and natural resources (Zhang et al., 2023b). Consequently, this increased economic activity leads to greater environmental pressures and a rise in ecological footprints. However, over the long run, the dynamics shift. As trade continues to expand, G20 countries are increasingly subjected to international environmental regulations, standards, and technological advancements. These pressures encourage the adoption of cleaner production methods, the transition to renewable energy, and the implementation of stricter environmental policies. Over time, such adaptations mitigate the initial environmental degradation associated with trade expansion, allowing trade openness to contribute positively to environmental sustainability. Therefore, while TROP may initially exacerbate environmental pressures, it ultimately facilitates improvements in ecological quality as G20 countries integrate green technologies and regulatory frameworks into their trade and production systems. These findings align with those reported by (Shahbaz et al., 2019; Al-Mulali et al., 2015; Voumik et al., 2024). This outcome validates H3 of the study, which posits that trade openness (TROP) has a negative influence on ecological footprints (ECFP) in G20 countries. Finally, TRSM is positively associated with ecological footprints in both periods. A 1% increase is associated with a 0.416432% increase in the long run and a 0.231413% increase in the short run, significant at the 1% and 5% levels, respectively. The positive association in both timeframes suggests that tourism, while contributing to economic development and cultural exchange, also places substantial pressure on environmental resources in G20 nations. In the short run, the increase in tourism-driven demand for transportation, accommodation, food services, and recreational activities can directly escalate energy consumption and resource depletion, thus intensifying ecological degradation. Over the long run, the cumulative effects of infrastructure development for tourism—such as the expansion of airports, resorts, and other facilities—continue to elevate ecological footprints. Moreover, if tourism growth is not matched with sustainable practices and green innovations, its environmental consequences can become more pronounced over time. These findings are consistent with prior studies, such as (Habib et al., 2022), who found that tourism increases environmental degradation in emerging economies, and (El et al., 2020), who highlighted tourism’s contribution to CO2 emissions in Mediterranean countries. Similarly (Voumik et al., 2024; Kongbuamai et al., 2020), observed that the expansion of tourism activities tends to amplify environmental pressures unless accompanied by stringent environmental regulations and the promotion of eco-friendly tourism practices. Thus, while tourism remains a vital economic sector, G20 countries should emphasize sustainable tourism initiatives, invest in green infrastructure, and implement policies that minimize its adverse environmental impacts to ensure a balance between economic benefits and ecological preservation. These findings are consistent with recent empirical work such as (Wang L. et al., 2024), who found similar long-run reductions in environmental degradation linked to life expectancy, and (Wang C. et al., 2024), who emphasized the environmental burden of tourism and natural resource use in large economies. The negative long-run effect of trade openness aligns with studies like (Terra Dos Santos et al., 2023), which suggest that greater global integration eventually supports cleaner technologies. Moreover, our results expand on (Alomair et al., 2025), who examined ecological sustainability using broader socio-economic indicators. These insights have important implications for both environmental and public health policymakers. For example, policies that reduce ecological pressure through sustainable tourism, clean energy investment, and natural resource regulation can also contribute to reducing disease burdens linked to pollution, poor air quality, and climate-related health risks. Public health authorities in G20 countries should therefore be involved in shaping climate and environmental policies, as these interventions deliver co-benefits for health system resilience, environmental preservation, and long-term social welfare. 6 Conclusion and policy implication Addressing ecological degradation has been a major global concern during the last few decades. The economy, human wellbeing, and our basic survival may suffer because of inadequate rules and policies in this domain. This paper concentrates on a case study of G20 nations to properly address these problems and suggest appropriate approaches. It aims to investigate the short- and long-term impacts of social welfare and economic policy uncertainty, two key elements of the twenty-first century, on ecological footprint (ECFP), a generally used metric of environmental degradation. The paper also considers how ECFP is impacted by LIEX, TROP, and NARN. The use of panel ARDL approach has been used to review the impact of these variables on ECFP. Our analysis reveals some important insights into how various factors influence ECFP in G20 countries, ultimately impacting environmental quality. Economic policy uncertainty and life expectancy are negatively associated with ecological footprints in both the short and long run. The ECPX result does not demonstrate that uncertainty improves environmental quality or reflects stricter environmental regulation. Reduced investment, production, and energy demand are possible explanations, but the model does not test these mechanisms. TROP has a dual impact: in the short run, it is positively related to ECFP, meaning it initially harms environmental quality by increasing ecological footprints. However, in the long run, TROP shows a negative relationship with ECFP, indicating that over time, it can help reduce ecological footprints and improve environmental quality. On the other hand, NARN and SCWF both have positive relationships with ECFP in both the short and long run. Meanwhile TRSM has shown a positive relationship with ECFP both in the short run and the long run. This suggests that increased tourism, use of natural resources and social welfare initiatives, if not managed wisely, can lead to greater ecological footprints and environmental degradation. These associations highlight the importance of considering environmental pressures alongside socioeconomic objectives. They do not establish the effectiveness of specific resource-management, welfare, or educational interventions. The findings identify broad policy considerations rather than establish the effectiveness of specific instruments. The positive associations of natural resource rents, social welfare, and tourism with ecological footprints suggest that environmental sustainability deserves consideration alongside resource development, welfare provision, and tourism expansion. These associations do not imply that welfare provision should be curtailed or establish which taxes, subsidies, or regulations would reduce ecological pressures. Appropriate interventions require country-specific evaluation. The contrasting short-run and long-run associations of trade openness suggest that its environmental implications may evolve over time. However, the model does not identify the mechanisms responsible for this difference or demonstrate the effectiveness of particular trade or technology policies. Similarly, the negative association between economic policy uncertainty and ecological footprints does not justify increasing uncertainty. The association involving life expectancy also does not establish the environmental effects of specific healthcare interventions. Policy design should therefore draw on additional evidence concerning institutional capacity, economic structure, distributional consequences, and implementation costs. The present estimates inform the areas warranting attention, while evaluation of individual policy instruments remains beyond the study’s empirical scope. This study has several limitations. The use of country-level aggregate data may conceal regional and sectoral differences within G20 economies, while the PMG-ARDL estimator’s assumption of homogeneous long-run coefficients may not fully capture their institutional and economic diversity. Moreover, the model does not explicitly account for structural shocks or other relevant determinants, such as renewable energy, green innovation, environmental regulation, and governance quality; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than definitive causal effects. Future studies may employ disaggregated data, structural-break and nonlinear models, and alternative estimators such as AMG and CCEMG. They may also incorporate additional sustainability variables and compare the G20 with other country groups to assess the broader applicability of the findings.
