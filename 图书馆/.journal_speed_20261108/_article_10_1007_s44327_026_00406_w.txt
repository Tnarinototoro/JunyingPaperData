In Luxor, Egypt, the performance of these spaces is strongly influenced by accessibility, environmental comfort, safety, and cultural legibility. This study investigates universal design as an integrative framework to enhance urban well-being and improve tourist experiences in heritage urban environments. A mixed-methods approach was employed, combining a systematic literature review, analysis of international best practices, and empirical fieldwork. Primary data were collected through questionnaire surveys administered to 339 participants (55% visitors, 40% residents, 5% professionals) across two key urban areas: Railway Station Square and the surroundings of Luxor Temple, using a five-point Likert scale. Descriptive statistical analysis was applied to the survey data, including frequency distributions and mean score calculations. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare perceptions between residents and tourists. Observational data were interpreted using universal design indicators. The findings reveal significant deficiencies: approximately 95% of respondents confirmed the lack of accessible infrastructure for all users, 88.2% reported the absence of adequate seating and shading, and approximately 82.4% considered street surfaces unsafe for movement. These deficiencies negatively influence perceived comfort, ease of movement, and overall user satisfaction. This study concludes that integrating universal design principles into heritage urban planning can substantially improve urban well-being and enhance the quality of the tourist experience, while supporting inclusive and sustainable development in culturally sensitive cities. Highlights Universal design improves accessibility and perceived urban well-being in heritage tourist cities. Empirical assessment of public spaces in Luxor reveals critical accessibility and comfort gaps. Findings support integrating inclusive design principles into heritage urban development strategies. Urban Policy Architecture Industrial Design Space and Place in Culture Heritage Management Housing and Built Environments 1 Introduction Tourist cities, particularly those with significant heritage and cultural value, face increasing challenges in balancing the preservation of historical identity with the diverse functional needs of users, especially in the context of growing visitor numbers and the heterogeneity of their demographic, cultural, and physical characteristics [ 1 ]. Within this context, public urban spaces constitute critical components of the urban fabric, as they significantly influence tourist experience, enhance residents’ quality of life, and contribute to long-term social and economic sustainability [ 2 ]. Universal design has emerged as a contemporary approach in urban planning and design that seeks to create environments usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, without the need for adaptation or specialised design solutions [ 3 ]. Beyond physical accessibility, universal design extends to sensory and psychological dimensions, promoting inclusion, equity, and overall urban well-being [ 4 ]. In parallel, the concept of tourist-friendly urban spaces focuses on creating environments that are safe, legible, comfortable, and accessible for diverse user groups, thereby enhancing the quality of the visitor experience while also benefiting local residents [ 5 ]. These two concepts are strongly interrelated, as universal design provides the foundational principles through which inclusive and tourist-friendly urban environments can be achieved [ 3 , 6 ]. Despite growing scholarly attention to universal design, urban well-being, and tourist-oriented public space design, these themes are often examined separately or within contemporary urban contexts in developed countries. Recent research by Bianco [ 7 ] and Esfandfard et al. [ 8 ] has emphasised the need for more integrated approaches, yet limited studies have applied universal design principles systematically within heritage cities of developing nations. This gap is particularly significant in contexts where cultural preservation, intensive tourism, and limited infrastructure resources intersect [ 9 ]. Moreover, many urban tourism development initiatives still lack rigorous assessment of how public spaces perform in relation to the needs of diverse user groups and how this influences their experiential quality and satisfaction levels [ 10 ]. Luxor represents a critical case in this regard, as one of the world’s most prominent heritage cities, characterised by continuous tourist flows and high cultural significance [ 11 ]. Although recent urban development projects have focused on improving the visual and symbolic quality of tourist areas, less attention has been given to evaluating functional performance in terms of accessibility, comfort, safety, and inclusivity from a universal design perspective [ 12 ]. 1.1 Research gap and research questions The critical review of existing literature reveals three major research gaps. First, there is a conceptual fragmentation, as previous studies have largely examined universal design, urban well-being, and tourist-friendly spaces as separate themes rather than as interconnected dimensions of urban quality. Second, there is a contextual limitation, as most empirical investigations have been conducted in contemporary urban environments within developed countries, with limited evidence from heritage cities in developing contexts. Third, there is a methodological deficiency, as few studies provide measurable frameworks linking universal design indicators with user perceptions of well-being and tourist experience in real urban settings. Accordingly, this study seeks to address the following research questions: RQ1. To what extent do the selected public urban spaces in Luxor comply with universal design principles? RQ2. How do existing spatial conditions influence users’ perceptions of accessibility, urban well-being, and tourist experience? RQ3. Which universal design deficiencies represent the most significant barriers to inclusive and tourist-friendly urban environments? RQ4. How can universal design principles be operationalised within heritage urban contexts to improve inclusivity, usability, and overall urban quality? 1.2 Research aim and objectives This study aims to evaluate the extent to which selected public urban spaces in Luxor comply with universal design principles and to examine how such compliance influences urban well-being and tourist experience within a heritage tourism context. To achieve this aim, the study pursues four main objectives: 1. to assess the level of compliance of selected urban spaces in Luxor with universal design principles; 2. to evaluate users’ perceptions of accessibility, comfort, safety, and inclusiveness within the study area; 3. to identify the major spatial deficiencies affecting urban well-being and tourist experience; and 4. to develop an analytical framework that integrates universal design, urban well-being, and tourist-friendly urban-space principles for application in heritage cities. 2 Significance of the study This study is significant in addressing a clear research gap related to the application of universal design principles in heritage tourist cities, particularly within developing and Arab contexts. While existing literature has extensively discussed accessibility, urban well-being, and tourism-oriented design, these themes are often treated separately and mainly within contemporary urban environments. This research contributes to the field by integrating universal design, urban well-being, and tourist-friendly urban spaces within a single analytical framework, thereby extending the role of universal design beyond physical accessibility to include psychological comfort, perceived safety, ease of movement, and equitable use of public spaces [ 13 ]. From an applied perspective, the study provides a practical evaluation framework for assessing the performance of public urban spaces in heritage cities experiencing intensive tourist use. Through the case of Luxor, the research highlights gaps between current urban development interventions and the actual needs of diverse user groups, including residents and tourists. The findings support planners and decision-makers in developing more inclusive and human-centred urban environments, while aligning with international sustainability agendas, particularly Sustainable Development Goal 11, which promotes inclusive, safe, and sustainable cities [ 14 ]. 3 Methodology This study adopts a mixed-methods research design to investigate the relationship between universal design principles, urban well-being, and tourist experience in heritage urban spaces. However, existing literature tends to classify these needs into psychological, physical, and visual dimensions without adequately examining their interaction within complex heritage tourist contexts, often overlooking challenges posed by high visitor density, multicultural user groups, and seasonal fluctuations. Psychological needs encompass safety, belonging, privacy, comfort, and social connection, while visual needs relate to environmental legibility, orientation, and landmark recognition—factors that strongly influence place attachment and user satisfaction [ 13 , 15 ]. Nevertheless, literature frequently treats these dimensions independently, with limited investigation into how spatial design simultaneously affects emotional well-being, cognitive perception, and user behaviour in heritage tourism environments characterised by crowding, cultural diversity, and intensive spatial use [ 12 , 16 ]. Physical needs concern infrastructure and environmental elements that support movement, comfort, and activity performance, including pathways, urban furniture, green spaces, and recreational facilities [ 15 ]. Although considerable attention has been devoted to evaluating physical quality, most assessments focus on functional adequacy rather than inclusivity, often neglecting whether spaces remain equally usable for individuals of different ages, abilities, and mobility requirements [ 3 , 17 ]. As illustrated in Fig. 2 , human needs constitute the foundation upon which urban quality and user satisfaction are built. Nevertheless, existing research lacks a comprehensive framework linking these needs with inclusivity, well-being outcomes, and tourist experience within heritage urban settings. Fig. 2 Full size image Types of human needs within urban spaces [ 15 ] 4.2 Universal design (UD) Universal Design (UD) has evolved from accessibility-oriented approaches toward a broader design philosophy aimed at creating environments that can be used by the widest possible range of users without the need for adaptation or specialised solutions [ 3 , 18 , 19 ]. The literature generally acknowledges the seven principles of universal design as an effective framework for promoting accessibility, usability, safety, and social inclusion [ 7 ]. However, a critical review reveals that most universal design research has concentrated on buildings, products, and indoor environments, while applications within public urban spaces remain comparatively underdeveloped. Moreover, many studies continue to equate universal design primarily with physical accessibility measures such as ramps, elevators, and pavement design. This narrow interpretation overlooks broader dimensions of universal design related to spatial cognition, sensory perception, psychological comfort, and social participation [ 7 ]. Recent research argues that successful universal design should be evaluated not only by compliance with accessibility standards but also by its capacity to create equitable, intuitive, and dignified urban experiences for all users [ 8 , 17 ]. Nevertheless, empirical validation of this proposition remains limited, particularly within heritage cities of developing countries where tourism pressures, historical preservation requirements, and infrastructural constraints coexist. Therefore, a significant gap remains regarding the application of universal design as an integrated evaluation framework capable of assessing both spatial performance and user well-being in heritage tourism contexts. Existing studies indicate that well-being-oriented design promotes healthy urban environments through walkability, access to green infrastructure, opportunities for social interaction, and improved environmental quality [ 22 ]. Such interventions have been consistently associated with reduced stress levels, enhanced mental health, and improved life satisfaction [ 23 , 24 ]. Despite these contributions, much of the well-being literature implicitly assumes that urban environments are equally accessible to all users. This assumption often neglects existing inequalities in access, mobility, and participation, particularly within tourist cities where spatial resources may be unevenly distributed. Furthermore, while well-being frameworks effectively address emotional, psychological, social, and environmental dimensions, they generally provide limited guidance regarding how these outcomes can be operationalized through inclusive spatial design practices [ 25 ]. As a result, the relationship between well-being and accessibility remains insufficiently theorized. Figure 4 presents the principal dimensions of well-being-oriented design. Although these dimensions contribute substantially to understanding human-centred urban environments, achieving well-being outcomes requires integration with universal design principles to ensure equitable access to these benefits across different user groups. Fig. 4 Full size image Well-being dimensions [ 26 ] 4.4 Tourist-friendly urban spaces Tourist-friendly urban spaces are increasingly recognised as essential components of destination competitiveness and visitor satisfaction. These spaces provide functional, cultural, recreational, and social opportunities that collectively shape tourists’ perceptions of urban destinations [ 26 ]. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of accessibility, safety, cultural identity, environmental quality, and service provision in enhancing tourist experiences [ 27 , 28 ]. Heritage cities depend heavily on the quality of their public spaces to support visitor movement, orientation, and engagement with cultural assets [ 29 ]. Nevertheless, the literature reveals an important conceptual limitation. Many tourism-oriented studies prioritise visitor satisfaction while paying comparatively less attention to residents’ needs and long-term urban sustainability. Consequently, tourism-focused urban interventions sometimes contribute to overcrowding, exclusion, or the deterioration of local quality of life [ 29 ]. Researchers have also emphasised that heritage and cultural identity significantly enhance place attractiveness by providing authentic and memorable visitor experiences [ 16 ]. However, heritage value alone does not guarantee accessibility, comfort, or inclusivity. In many cases, preservation priorities may conflict with contemporary user requirements [ 12 ]. These observations suggest that tourist-friendly urban design requires a more balanced framework capable of simultaneously addressing visitor expectations, resident well-being, accessibility, and cultural preservation. Universal design offers a promising foundation for achieving this balance through its emphasis on equitable and inclusive use. 4.5 Integration of universal design, well-being, and tourist-friendly spaces The critical review of previous studies reveals that the three concepts universal design, well-being-oriented design, and tourist-friendly urban spaces have largely evolved as parallel research streams. Universal design primarily focuses on accessibility, equity, usability, and inclusion [ 7 ], whereas well-being-oriented design emphasises psychological, emotional, and health outcomes [ 22 ]. Tourist-friendly urban design, meanwhile, concentrates on enhancing visitor satisfaction and destination attractiveness [ 29 ]. Although each framework contributes valuable insights, few studies have attempted to integrate them within a unified analytical model. Existing research generally examines these concepts independently, resulting in fragmented approaches that fail to capture the multidimensional nature of public space performance in heritage tourism contexts. This fragmentation is particularly evident in developing countries, where limited empirical studies investigate how accessibility, urban well-being, and tourist experience interact within the same urban environment. Consequently, there remains a significant theoretical and practical gap regarding how universal design principles can simultaneously support resident well-being, visitor satisfaction, and heritage conservation. Accordingly, this study proposes an integrated conceptual framework that positions universal design as an overarching approach capable of linking human needs, well-being outcomes, and tourist-friendly urban environments. Based on this premise, this part of the study analyzes a set of leading global experiences that have successfully applied universal design principles in distinctive urban and tourism projects. 5.1 Case study selection criteria The case studies analysed were selected based on a set of scientific and methodological criteria. First, the selected cases are leading global models in the application of universal design principles within successful urban and tourism projects. Second, these projects reflect the ability of universal design to enhance urban well-being and improve the experience of users of all ages, physical abilities, and cultures. Third, the selected cases represent clear geographical, cultural, and contextual diversity. Fourth, these models demonstrate the integration of functional and aesthetic aspects in the design of public spaces. Finally, the case studies are a rich source of lessons learned and design practices that can be applied in similar heritage contexts. Based on these criteria, three prominent international case studies were selected: The High Line Park (Manhattan, New York, USA), Superkilen Park (Nørrebro, Copenhagen, Denmark), and La Rambla, Barcelona, Spain. 5.2 The high line park, Manhattan, New York, USA In a city brimming with world-famous public spaces, Manhattan’s High Line Park (Fig. 6 ) continues to attract attention and praise from locals and tourists alike. This project is one of the most prominent contemporary global models that has successfully employed universal design principles within an urban tourist space, contributing to the enhancement of urban well-being and the improvement of the quality of the tourist experience [ 30 ]. High Line Park was developed as an elevated linear park and greenway built on a former abandoned railway track on the west side of Manhattan. The project extends for approximately 1.52 miles and covers an area of approximately 27,499 square meters, transforming abandoned industrial infrastructure into a vibrant urban space that integrates natural elements into the dense urban fabric [ 30 ]. Fig. 6 Full size image Location of the high line in Manhattan, New York (Google Maps, 2025) The project not only provides green spaces and walking paths but also serves a cultural and social function by hosting art installations and community events, promoting social interaction and outdoor activities. These design features have increased the site’s appeal, with the number of visitors rising from around four million in 2011 to nearly seven million in 2018, making it one of the most visited public spaces in New York City and a prominent urban and tourist landmark [ 31 ]. Studies indicate that urban green spaces, such as the High Line, contribute to improved mental health by encouraging physical activity, reducing feelings of isolation, and enhancing overall life satisfaction [ 23 ]. The design of High Line Park clearly draws on the principles of universal design. The principle of equitable use is achieved by providing access to all users through the integration of lifts and ramps, as well as multiple entry points distributed along the route, reducing travel distances and enhancing accessibility. The park is also open to the public without restrictions or fees, which supports social inclusivity [ 32 ]. The project embodies the principle of flexibility in use through the diversity of activities accommodated by the paths and seating areas, which include relaxation, social interaction, photography, and artistic performances, meeting the diverse needs of users throughout the day and enhancing their perception of the quality of the place [ 32 ]. The principle of low physical effort has also been reinforced by providing comfortable seats spaced at close intervals, taking into account the needs of the elderly and people with different physical abilities. With regard to safety elements, the principle of tolerance for error has been taken into account by designing the edges of the path with high glass barriers that provide protection without obstructing the view of the city, in addition to the use of non-slip flooring to ensure safety in various weather conditions, which reduces the likelihood of accidents during use [ 30 ] Figs. 7 and 8 . The park was developed in one of the city’s most culturally diverse neighbourhoods to promote social interaction and build a unifying community identity, making it one of the world’s most prominent examples of the use of universal design as a tool for social integration in public spaces [ 33 ]. Fig. 9 Full size image Location of Superkilen Park in Nørrebro, Copenhagen, Denmark (Google Maps, 2025) The park has been promoted as one of the world’s largest parks supporting cultural diversity, allowing all users, regardless of their cultural origins or backgrounds, to freely explore, use, and interact with the park’s elements. The park features urban elements and public furniture inspired by 57 different countries, with each piece or cultural element representing a specific area within the park, reflecting the concept of multiculturalism in a tangible way within the urban space [ 34 ]. The park features a variety of interactive spaces that embody the concepts of inclusivity and diversity in a practical way, taking into account the provision of walkways and activity areas that can be easily used by all age and physical groups, including children, the elderly, and people with disabilities, as well as attention to safety elements, lighting quality, and visual comfort (Fig. 10 ). This reflects the project’s commitment to applying universal design principles to ensure a safe and accessible experience for all visitors. Fig. 10 Full size image Plazas and seating areas that enhance comfort along pedestrian and bicycle pathways [ 35 ] The spatial design of the park is based on its division into three main areas, distinguished by colour and function: the red zone, the black zone, and the green zone. Each area has spatial characteristics and activities that reflect different patterns of urban use. The red zone is designed as an extension of the sporting and cultural activities associated with the Nørrebrohallen Sports Centre and features red-toned materials and flooring that extend towards the facades of the surrounding buildings, creating a dynamic, multi-purpose space. On weekends, this area transforms into a vibrant local market that attracts visitors from within and outside the neighbourhood. The black zone is the social heart of the park, with permanent tables, Turkish benches, and barbecue areas transforming it into an outdoor living room. Cultural elements, such as the Moroccan fountain, contribute to daily interaction among residents, while the white wavy lines on the dark pavement guide movement and organize the space without imposing specific paths, promoting ease of use and social interaction. In contrast, the green area provides quieter natural spaces, where hills and grassy areas create an environment suitable for family leisure activities such as picnics, relaxation, and light games, responding to a fundamental demand from neighbourhood residents who expressed a clear need for more open green spaces within the urban area (Fig. 11 ) [ 33 ]. The street is a prime example of an active public space that combines daily movement, cultural activities, tourism, and social interaction, making it a central element in the city’s urban fabric. Fig. 12 Full size image Location of La Rambla, Barcelona, Spain (Google Maps, 2025) It has been redeveloped as part of Barcelona’s efforts to improve the quality of urban life and promote the universal design of public spaces, with a focus on accessibility and ease of use for all types of users [ 36 ]. The street’s urban design is based on providing wide, level pedestrian walkways that allow safe and easy movement for pedestrians, including the elderly, people with disabilities, wheelchair users, and pushchairs, thus achieving the principle of equitable use within the framework of universal design. The visual and aesthetic quality of the street has also been enhanced through the use of artistic paving and thoughtful integration with the surrounding historic facades and heritage buildings, as well as the provision of seating areas and spaces dedicated to outdoor cultural and artistic activities. This has contributed to transforming the street into an interactive urban space that promotes the psychological and social well-being of visitors and encourages them to stay and interact within the space [ 37 ]. The development also focuses on achieving clarity of movement and ease of orientation, as the visual connection with the surrounding urban landmarks has been enhanced, supporting the natural orientation of pedestrians and reducing visual confusion while moving around. The organisation of open spaces along the street has also been taken into account to reduce congestion and achieve higher levels of visual and kinetic comfort, especially during peak tourist periods, while maintaining the flow of movement within the urban space [ 38 ] Fig. 13 . Priority of social integration: Achieving inclusivity in design ensures the participation of all segments of society without discrimination, thereby enhancing a sense of belonging and urban citizenship. 2. Balance between function and aesthetics: Functional efficiency and visual appeal can be combined in the design of public spaces to achieve psychological and physical comfort for users. 3. Importance of flexibility of use: Multiple uses and a variety of activities within the same urban space extend its functional life and increase its social and tourist value. 4. Promoting local identity within a global framework: Universal design does not conflict with cultural specificity but rather reframes it in a contemporary urban context that expresses the place and its inhabitants. 5. Sustainability as a universal perspective: Integrating the principles of inclusivity and sustainability enhances quality of life in the long term and supports sustainable tourism based on environmental and social responsibility. 6. Enabling community participation: In most of the cases studied, residents and users participated in the design process, which led to the projects successfully meeting real needs and achieving public acceptance. 6 Luxor, Egypt, as a case study Luxor is one of the most important tourist cities in Egypt and the world, as it embraces a unique cultural heritage that attracts large numbers of visitors of different nationalities. However, urban growth and tourism pressures necessitate a comprehensive design vision that ensures accessibility, improves the quality of public spaces, and enhances the tourist experience for all users, including the elderly and people with disabilities. This part of the research aims to analyse the reality of the urban environment in Luxor and assess its compliance with the principles of universal design. Luxor Governorate is in southern Egypt (Fig. 14 ), bordering Qena Governorate to the north, Aswan Governorate to the south, the Western Desert to the west, and the Eastern Desert to the east. The governorate is divided into seven districts, with Luxor City as the centre, containing more than 800 archaeological sites, many listed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The city is located on the east bank of the Nile, 635 km south of Cairo. In terms of urban planning, the city is characterised by a diverse urban fabric that combines historical character with traditional high-density residential areas, along with mixed uses. Commercial activity is concentrated in the city centre, with tourism-related services along the corniche [ 40 , 41 ]. Fig. 15 Full size image Locations of the selected study areas in Luxor (Google Earth, 2025) 6.2 Study methodology This study was based on field observation and questionnaire surveys. The user is considered the primary focus of measurement in urban space development processes, as it is through the user that the degree of usability of space and its responsiveness to individuals’ functional and aesthetic needs are measured. The principle of community participation was activated through the preparation of a questionnaire in both paper and electronic versions, with the aim of surveying the opinions of users of the area under study, as well as visitors and tourists, about the impact of universal design principles on user comfort, well-being, quality of movement, and equal access for all groups. A non-probability convenience sampling strategy was employed to recruit residents, domestic visitors, international tourists, and professionals. This approach was adopted because conducting probability-based sampling was not practically feasible within a busy heritage tourism environment. Although the sampling strategy does not permit statistical generalisation to the wider population, it enabled the collection of responses from a diverse range of users who experience the study areas under different conditions and for different purposes. The questionnaire was administered between January and March 2025 using both face-to-face and online modes. Face-to-face recruitment was conducted at the study sites—Railway Station Square, Al-Mahatta Street, Abu Al-Haggag Square, and the entrance to Luxor Temple—at different times of the day to capture a diverse range of users. The online questionnaire was disseminated via Google Forms through social media platforms and local community groups to reach additional residents, professionals, and other users who might not have been present at the study sites during the fieldwork period. Participants were eligible if they were at least 18 years old and had used the study areas at least once during the previous year. A total of 420 individuals were approached during the face-to-face recruitment process. The final analytical sample comprised 339 valid questionnaire responses collected through the combined face-to-face and online survey modes. Accordingly, the response rate is reported only for the face-to-face component, while the online questionnaire served as a supplementary recruitment method and was not used to calculate an overall response rate. The final sample included 187 visitors (55%), 136 residents (40%), and 16 professionals (5%), thereby providing a diverse cross-section of users with direct experience of the study areas.. The sample included 339 participants of different nationalities and abilities (Fig. 16 ). Validity assessment: Content validity was established through an extensive review of the literature on universal design, urban well-being, and tourist-friendly urban spaces. The questionnaire structure was subsequently reviewed by a panel of academic experts in architecture, urban design, and planning. Minor modifications were implemented based on expert feedback. A pilot survey was then conducted with a preliminary sample of 30 respondents representing different user groups to verify question clarity, response consistency, and overall applicability. Reliability assessment: Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient. The overall questionnaire achieved Cronbach’s Alpha value of 0.84, indicating good reliability and acceptable internal consistency among the survey items. According to commonly accepted methodological standards, values above 0.70 are considered satisfactory for social science research. The internal consistency of each universal design construct was also assessed: Equitable Use (α = 0.82), Flexibility in Use (α = 0.79), Simple and Intuitive Use (α = 0.76), Perceptible Information (α = 0.81), Tolerance for Error (α = 0.78), Low Physical Effort (α = 0.83), and Size and Space for Approach (α = 0.80). The results showed that this principle has not been achieved to the required level in the study area. Approximately 58% of participants indicated that it is easy to access streets and public spaces using various means of transport. However, a total of 95% of respondents confirmed that the mobility environment within these areas is not equipped to meet the needs of all users, especially children, the elderly, and people with mobility disabilities. This deficiency is linked to the lack of effective traffic and pedestrian management, as the proliferation of street vendors and the mixing of vehicle and pedestrian traffic hinders movement and increases risk levels. For tourists (55% of respondents), the lack of wayfinding and perceptible information significantly reduces the quality of the heritage experience. Visual clutter from advertisements distracts attention from cultural assets, while limited seating and shading reduce comfort during extended visits. Furthermore, poor signage makes it difficult to navigate between heritage sites, undermining the overall visitor experience. 2. Residents (40% of respondents) face daily challenges due to the absence of seating, shading, and safe surfaces, which diminishes their everyday quality of life. The dominance of commercial uses (50%) limits opportunities for social interaction, while pedestrian-vehicle conflicts create persistent safety concerns. The lack of public restrooms and basic amenities further discourages extended use of the area by residents. 3. Elderly users (4% of respondents) are particularly affected by the absence of ramps and accessible surfaces, which effectively excludes them from full participation in public life. The lack of rest areas and seating causes fatigue, while poor lighting and unsafe surfaces create fall risks. Additionally, limited shade exacerbates health risks during extreme heat conditions, making the area particularly challenging for older adults. 4. People with disabilities (4% of respondents) face the most severe barriers. Approximately 95% of respondents confirmed that the space is inaccessible to people with mobility impairments, and a similar percentage confirmed the absence of visual and auditory signals. Furthermore, 88.2% confirmed the absence of suitable ramps. These findings indicate that the space effectively excludes users with disabilities from full participation in public life, contradicting the principles of universal design and social inclusion. 8.3 Heritage identity and amenity paradox A notable finding is the contrast between strong heritage identity and poor functional performance. While 88.2% of respondents recognised prominent landmarks that aid in spatial memory and association (Simple & Intuitive Use), the same respondents identified severe deficiencies in accessibility, comfort, and safety. This paradox suggests that the area’s cultural and symbolic value does not compensate for its functional inadequacies. In other words, the strong visual and symbolic presence of heritage assets creates high expectations among users, but these expectations are not fulfilled by the physical quality and usability of the surrounding urban spaces. This finding is consistent with Elsorady’s [ 16 ]observation that heritage value alone does not ensure positive user experiences. The strong mental image associated with Luxor Temple, Abu Al-Haggag Mosque, and the railway station creates high user expectations that are not met by the physical and functional quality of the surrounding urban spaces. This paradox highlights the need for a more balanced approach to urban development in heritage cities, one that integrates functional performance with cultural preservation to ensure that heritage assets are complemented by accessible, comfortable, and safe public spaces. 8.4 Socio-demographic variations residents vs. tourists To examine whether user type influences perceptions of universal design performance, a comparative analysis was conducted between residents and tourists. By integrating theoretical analysis, international case-study evaluation, field observations, and user perception surveys, the research developed an analytical framework capable of assessing the inclusiveness and usability of public urban spaces in heritage tourism contexts. The findings demonstrate that the studied public spaces exhibit considerable deficiencies in several core universal design dimensions, particularly equitable accessibility, perceptible information, tolerance for error, and low physical effort. Although the study area possesses a strong cultural identity and contains significant heritage landmarks that contribute positively to place attachment and imageability, these qualities alone are insufficient to ensure a positive user experience. The results suggest that inadequate pedestrian infrastructure, limited wayfinding systems, insufficient seating and shading, poor accessibility provisions, and safety-related shortcomings appear to negatively influence perceived urban well-being and tourist satisfaction. The study further confirms that universal design should not be viewed solely as an accessibility-oriented approach but rather as a comprehensive framework that integrates functional, psychological, social, and environmental dimensions of urban quality. The analysis of international best-practice cases demonstrated that successful heritage and tourism-oriented urban environments are characterised by the integration of accessibility, safety, flexibility, comfort, and cultural identity within a unified design strategy. The findings suggest that strengthening universal design principles may contribute to improved urban well-being and enhanced tourist experience quality. The proposed framework offers a practical assessment tool that can assist planners, urban designers, and decision-makers in evaluating and improving public-space performance in heritage tourism destinations. 9.1 Recommendations Based on the findings, the following prioritised recommendations are proposed, organised according to their implementation timeline and expected impact. Priority 1 (Short-term, High Impact): Immediate interventions that can be implemented with relatively low cost and high effectiveness include installing clear multilingual wayfinding signage at key decision points to improve orientation and navigation for both tourists and residents. Additionally, shaded seating areas should be added along pedestrian routes to enhance thermal comfort and provide resting opportunities. Pedestrian crossings should be improved with tactile paving and clear markings to ensure safety for all users, particularly those with visual impairments. Traffic calming measures should also be implemented around tourist areas to reduce vehicle speeds and minimise pedestrian-vehicle conflicts. Priority 2 (Medium-term): Over the medium term, a comprehensive pedestrian network should be developed to connect key heritage sites, facilitating safe and enjoyable walking experiences. Universal design standards should be integrated into heritage conservation guidelines to ensure that accessibility is considered a fundamental aspect of preservation efforts. Designated pedestrian-only zones should be established during peak tourist hours to prioritise pedestrian safety and comfort. Furthermore, accessible public toilets and rest areas should be provided at strategic locations to address the critical lack of basic amenities identified in this study. Priority 3 (Long-term): For long-term sustainability, an inclusive urban mobility plan for Luxor should be developed to address the broader transportation and accessibility challenges facing the city. Universal design principles should be formally integrated into the city’s master plan to ensure that all future urban development projects adhere to inclusive design standards. Finally, community participation mechanisms should be established in urban areas to ensure that the voices of residents, tourists, and other stakeholders are heard in the planning and decision-making processes, fostering a sense of ownership and ensuring that interventions meet actual user needs. 9.2 Study limitations Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the research relied primarily on self-reported perceptions collected through questionnaire surveys, which may be influenced by individual experiences and subjective judgments. Second, the study was conducted within a specific spatial and cultural context in Luxor City; therefore, the findings may not be directly generalisable to other heritage tourism destinations with different urban, social, or environmental characteristics. In addition, the analysis focused mainly on descriptive statistical techniques and field observations. Consequently, the study identifies patterns and associations rather than causal relationships between universal design characteristics and perceived well-being outcomes. Future research may benefit from employing larger comparative samples, longitudinal investigations, and advanced statistical analyses to further examine these relationships across different heritage urban contexts. Finally, while the study considered the perspectives of diverse user groups, some categories of users, particularly individuals with severe disabilities and elderly users, may remain under-represented within the survey sample. The exploratory descriptive analysis of these subgroups (n = 13 for elderly users; n = 7 for users with mobility impairments) revealed consistent patterns of disadvantage, but the small sample sizes preclude generalisable statistical conclusions. Further research employing targeted sampling strategies and larger sample sizes is necessary to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the specific universal urban design requirements for these vulnerable groups. This limitation is particularly significant given that these groups represent the primary beneficiaries of universal design interventions. Additionally, the sample was predominantly male (77%), which may limit the generalisability of the findings to female users of the study areas. Future research should employ targeted recruitment strategies to ensure more balanced gender representation. 9.3 Future research directions Future studies may expand the proposed framework by applying it to a wider range of heritage cities across different cultural and geographical contexts. Comparative analyses between heritage and non-heritage urban environments could further clarify the role of cultural identity in shaping inclusive urban experiences. Future research may also incorporate advanced spatial analysis techniques, including GIS-based accessibility assessment, space-syntax analysis, and digital mobility tracking, to complement perception-based evaluation methods. Longitudinal studies of seasonal changes in tourist behaviour and urban-space performance would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between universal design and visitor experience over time. Furthermore , future investigations may employ structural equation modelling (SEM) or similar analytical approaches to explore causal relationships between universal design attributes, urban well-being indicators, and tourist satisfaction levels. Moreover, given the under-representation of vulnerable user groups in the current sample, future research should employ targeted recruitment strategies to ensure adequate representation of elderly individuals and people with various types of disabilities. Such studies could employ qualitative methods, including in-depth interviews and participatory design workshops, to capture the lived experiences and specific needs of these groups. This would complement the quantitative findings of the present study and provide a more comprehensive understanding of how universal design principles can be effectively operationalised to serve all members of the community, particularly those who are most dependent on inclusive urban environments. Ethics declarations Ethical approval and consent to participate The study protocol was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Higher Institute of Engineering and Technology, Luxor, Egypt. The IRB granted an exemption from formal ethical approval because the research involved anonymous voluntary questionnaire participation, did not collect sensitive personal data, and did not involve any clinical or experimental interventions. The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and the institutional guidelines of the Higher Institute of Engineering and Technology, Luxor, Egypt. Participation in the questionnaire survey was entirely voluntary and anonymous. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the questionnaire survey. For participants completing the paper version of the questionnaire, written informed consent was obtained. For participants completing the online version, electronic informed consent was obtained before they proceeded to complete the survey. All participants were informed about the purpose of the study, the confidentiality of their responses, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Consent for publication Written informed consent to publish was obtained from all participants. Participants were informed that anonymised data collected through the questionnaire surveys would be used for research purposes and potentially published in aggregated, statistical form. No identifiable personal information, images, or individual participant data are included in this manuscript. Competing interests The authors declare no competing interests.
