You can register here Notify me Abstract From the Indigenous perspective of the Borum-Kren people, biomes do not exist in isolation; what exists is territory in relation to other elements. Nak (land) is not divided into administrative categories; kijeme (home) has no rigid boundaries; jopik (mountain), forest, river, mountain range and climate all make up the same living home. Nimpare (kin) includes humans, trees, waters, winds, animals, rocks and spirits that connect the Amazon, the Atlantic Forest, the Cerrado and other biomes, in such a way that caring for one is crucial to the continuing survival of the others. Through this understanding, this article analyzes how biomes and Indigenous peoples are interconnected through kinship networks from an indigenous perspective and how these socio-ecological networks have historically been disrupted by colonial territorial occupation processes, manifested as ecocide, genocide and epistemicide. We argue that the contemporary ecological crisis is inseparable from a crisis of civilization and epistemology, in which models of conservation biodiversity, which is based on spatially delimited ecological categories, render Indigenous epistemologies invisible. The study follows a conceptual analysis, connecting historical ecology, anthropology, conservation biology, decolonial studies and Indigenous epistemologies fundamentally in the Borum-Kren territorial knowledge situated through the reflections of the authors – Indigenous researchers from the Borum-Kren and Goyá peoples. As a theoretical contribution, we propose the concept of Ancestral Intelligence, understood as the ability to reactivate networks of reciprocity between peoples and biomes through biocultural memory and territorial retaking, contributing to socioecological regeneration, epistemic justice and climate governance.
