Conservation Social Sciences &Indigenous and Local Knowledge Volume 7 - 2026 | https://doi.org/10.3389/fcosc.2026.1910283 Traditional water knowledge and Nile crocodile (Crocodylus niloticus) conservation in Southern Africa: a systematic review of policy integration, institutional gaps, cultural uses, and barriers BU Beaven Utete * Department of Freshwater and Fishery Sciences, Chinhoyi University of Technology, School of Wildlife and Environmental Sciences, Chinhoyi, Zimbabwe Article metrics View details Abstract The Nile crocodile (Crocodylus niloticus) is classified as of Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. As apex predators, crocodiles play vital ecosystem clean-up roles. However, anthropogenic activities threaten its population in wetlands in Southern Africa. The species attracts significant interest from humans within the Traditional Water Knowledge (TWK) domain. This Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-guided review assessed the uses of crocodiles, inclusion levels, and barriers and benefits of integrating TWK in Southern African Development Community (SADC) countries’ wildlife and water policies for crocodile conservation. TWK poses an existential threat to crocodile conservation through intergenerational negative perceptions bordering on myths and ethnozoological medicinal use incorporating its eyes, tail, head, scales, gall bladder, belly skins, liver, and bile duct secretions. Anthropogenic-driven destruction of nesting sites abetted by poor monitoring of wetland habitats by wildlife authorities causes declines in populations and shifts in distribution. Crocodile ranching is effective to sustain populations of crocodiles though its reliance on wild egg collection for enhancing production negates its overall contribution to conservation. TWK is poorly conceptualized and covered in wildlife and water conservation policies in SADC. Integrating geospatial techniques and citizen science in TWK provides a holistic framework to conserve crocodile populations in Southern Africa. Introduction Traditional water knowledge (TWK) defines indigenous people’s profound and inherent understanding, comprehension, and interpretation of natural processes with their ecological dependence on hydrological cycles and spiritual and religious cultural connections (Asad et al., 2023). Berkes (2012) and Asad et al (2022), Asad et al, 2023) indicated that TWK is an intergenerational historical knowledge of living closely with local water system ecology to become potential active and participatory agents of water change. This intimate relationship extends beyond physical, tangible, and practical relationships with water into contextual insight, belief, sense, and practices integrated with a culture in a specific location (Asad et al., 2022, Asad et al, 2023). TWK constitutes an epistemological component of Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) (Maposa and Mhaka, 2013; Dube, 2024; Kupika et al., 2025) and simultaneously coexists and conflicts with “Western” or scientific methods of water resources management in African landscapes (Tarakini et al., 2020; Asad et al., 2022). TWK is critical for sustainable crocodile conservation, often fostering coexistence through cultural respect rather than just scientific management (Pooley, 2016a; Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). TWK differs from broader TEK by centering specifically on hydrological dynamics, waterscape management, and aquatic spiritual values, whereas TEK encompasses the total environment (Berkes, 2012; Kupika et al., 2025; Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). Local communities use spiritual, ceremonial, and practical methods to monitor wildlife populations and habitats, providing insights into nesting sites, animal behavior, and wetland health, which can bridge conservation gaps in conventional science (Matanzima, 2022). Integrating aspects of local culture through citizen science participation in the management of water resources such as crocodiles helps shape the local conservation perceptions (Muyambo and Maphosa, 2014; Matanzima, 2022). Social participatory programs involving TEK are more acceptable as they are deemed inclusive of local needs and aspirations (Kupika et al., 2025). Furthermore, they are perceived to accord respect to local beliefs and ethics, even in the absence of monetary and tangible benefits (Maposa and Mhaka, 2013). Conceptually, TWK is based on the socio-ecological framework comprising (i) empirical location, (ii) resource conservation practices, (iii) customary (traditional) cultural and social governance institutions, and (iv) a relational world view or cosmo vision (Asad et al., 2022, Asad et al, 2023; Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). The four dimensions of TWK integrate human agency with biophysical (aquatic and terrestrial) environments to ensure sustainable water stewardship (Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). Precise contextual geolocation of a human community and the sacred phenomenon (object) and the associated local empirical knowledge, e.g., generations of qualitative data regarding hydrological patterns, weather cycles, spring locations, and soil-water dynamics, is the baseline for the TWK concept (Berkes, 2012). The second dimension is water resource conservation and management strategy implemented (Berkes, 2012). This TWK aspect embodies community-based techniques such as localized rainwater harvesting, contour or slope terracing, reverence, and protection of sacred water sites (Matanzima, 2022). The third dimension incorporates cultural governance institutions (Chibememe et al., 2014). It considers the roles of traditional governance structures, community taboos, and council leadership in equitable allocation of resources and conflict resolution (Chibememe et al., 2014). The local community perceptions of water, as a living entity and a sacred resource that needs maximum conservation rather than as an economic commodity, foster an ethical profound moral responsibility, probity, and reciprocity (Matanzima, 2022; Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). These four dimensions are grounded on the theoretical framework of socio-hydrology, TEK, and legal pluralism and endeavor to connect human behavior, social memory, spirituality, and cultural feedback loops directly with physical hydrological cycles (Chibememe et al., 2014). TEK analyzes resource use through an interlocking mechanism intertwining knowledge and practice and beliefs passed down through generations while legal pluralism explains customary and community-based water rules that operate alongside or conflict with modern statutory frameworks (Chibememe et al., 2014). Nile crocodiles (Crocodylus niloticus) form part of cultural and religious, scientific, and economic aspects of society (Utete, 2021). Their inclusion in local traditional ceremonies, use of parts as local traditional medicine, crocodile ranching, and human–crocodile interaction (conflict and coexistence) are intergenerational (Pooley, 2016a, Pooley, 2016b; Utete, 2021; Matanzima, 2022; Chakanyuka and Utete, 2022; Utete and Mabika, 2025). Crocodile ranching or farming refers to the removal from the wild of juveniles or eggs of crocodiles for regulated hatching and culture in special facilities for commercial purposes (Fergusson, 2010; Utete, 2021; Utete and Mabika, 2025). The multipurpose use of crocodiles makes them an integral part of TWK, TEK, and modern “scientific” water resources conservation in Africa (Fergusson, 2010; Pooley, 2016a; Combrink et al., 2019). Utete and Mabika (2025) indicated that overlap and poor interpretation and implementation of game and fisheries laws have led to operational mismanagement, inaction, and decision paralysis for management of freshwater resources in Southern Africa. There is a need to assess wildlife and water resources management policies in Southern African Development Community (SADC) member states and evaluate the inclusion levels of TWK as drivers and barriers to crocodile conservation (Utete and Mabika, 2025). Wetland systems in SADC only have one native crocodilian species, the Nile crocodile, and no individual country in the region harbors another endemic crocodile species (Jenkins et al., 2006; Lemaire et al., 2024). Mature crocodiles are apex predators with virtually no natural predators, but the eggs and juveniles are heavily preyed upon by Nile monitor lizards (Varanus niloticus), spotted hyenas (Crocuta crocuta), brown hyenas (Parahyaena brunnea), striped hyenas (Hyaena hyaena), African fish eagles (Icthyophaga vocifer), and snakes, e.g., African rock pythons (Python sebae) in SADC (Cott and Pooley, 1972; Pooley, 2016b; Ross, 1998; Utete, 2021). Nile monitor lizards and hyenas are common throughout SADC wetlands (Utete and Matanzima, 2024; Gonhi et al., 2023). They are natural predators of crocodiles and raid, unearth, and consume buried eggs, in the process decreasing hatching success rates and, thus, reducing populations (Utete and Matanzima, 2024). Hippopotamuses (Hippopotamus amphibius) do not eat crocodiles, but intensely compete for territory and kill adults that wander into pods or nesting territory (Utete, 2020). Leopards (Panthera pardus), lions (Panthera leo), hyenas, fish eagles, and African rock pythons target juveniles, sub-adults, and adults, thus reducing crocodile populations (Games and Moreau, 1997; Combrink et al., 2011). Humans collect crocodile eggs, juveniles, and even sub-adults for commercial (ranching) and cultural purposes, threatening the wild populations (Pooley, 2016b). Predator–prey dynamics act as a natural mechanism to control the crocodile population and balance ecosystem functions (Games and Moreau, 1997). Nevertheless, there is no evidence on the overall impact of natural predation on the wild crocodile population in SADC (Games and Moreau, 1997; Jenkins et al., 2006; Utete, 2021). The ethnobiological theory (for the zootherapy of crocodiles) seeks to explain the use of crocodiles and their parts, e.g., fat, teeth, dried tissue, scales, and tails for traditional medicine (Pooley, 2016a). This practice is based on symbolic resonance reasoning and local beliefs that crocodiles and their parts have mystic powers that embody survival, longevity, resilience, and physical prowess and restore human strength, overcome fear, or treat debilitating conditions (Pooley, 2016a). This functions together with humoral principles of ethnobiology that classify foods, medicines, and conditions into opposing thermal or symbolic qualities (Pool, 1987; García-Hernández et al., 2023). The principles assert that health wellbeing requires maintenance of dynamic equilibrium, and illness or poor health is a consequence of an excess or either hot (e.g., fevers) or cold (perceived spiritual attacks) extremes, which do not necessarily imply body temperature but rather categorical classification for state of condition of a disease or illness (Pool, 1987; García-Hernández et al., 2023). The deployment of sensory filters, i.e., organoleptic properties, e.g., bitter tastes and pungent smells, acts as cultural cues that dictate which medicinal (dietary and herbal) therapies are to be deployed. The three principles of ethnobiology, i.e., thermal classification, dietary and herbal therapeutics, and sensory filters, are applicable to TWK use of crocodiles, although there has been no evaluation on the overall impacts of zootherapy uses on the species’ populations in SADC (Jenkins et al., 2006; Pooley, 2016a). Brief overview of wildlife and water resource management protocols in SADC The wildlife policies of the 16 SADC countries, namely, Angola, Comoros Islands, Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), Madagascar, Mozambique, Mauritius, Namibia, Seychelles, South Africa, Tanzania/Zanzibar, Botswana, Kingdom of Eswatini, Lesotho, Malawi, Zambia, and Zimbabwe, were examined in this review. Different SADC countries have key conservation policies with differing provisions for wildlife, fish, aquaculture, and crocodile ranching (Utete and Mabika, 2025). The main foundational legal instrument for wildlife conservation is the SADC Protocol on Wildlife Conservation and Law Enforcement (1999), which establishes a common framework for the conservation and sustainable use of wildlife in Southern Africa (SADC, 1999). While the protocol was signed in 1999, it is operationalized through evolving regional strategies, most notably the SADC Law Enforcement and Anti-Poaching (LEAP) Strategy (SADC, 1999). The SADC Protocol on Wildlife Conservation and Law Enforcement (1999) promotes sustainable use, harmonizes national legal instruments, and enhanced cooperation to combat illegal wildlife trade (SADC, 1999). It encourages the creation and management of Transfrontier Conservation Areas (TFCAs) and directs member states to share information, joint patrols, and cooperate on prosecution (SADC, 1999). The protocol is anchored on the importance of community-based natural resource management (CBNRM) and ensuring optimal local benefits from wildlife (Utete and Mabika, 2025). Member states align their respective wildlife and conservation policies with the SADC Protocol on Wildlife Conservation and Law Enforcement (1999). SADC states ratify other legally non-binding regional and international treaties such as the African Union Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (ABSAP) 2023–2030, Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Flora and Fauna (CITES), Convention on Biodiversity (CBD), Nagoya Protocol on Access Benefit Sharing (ABS), Ramsar Convention on Wetlands of International Importance especially as Waterfowl Habitat (1971), and Kunming Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF). Ratification is necessary to align national laws with international treaties to prevent wildlife crime and manage shared resources effectively for continuity across spatial global scale (Chibememe et al., 2014). Regardless, SADC states contextualize the alignment to the unique habitat, institutional structures, and policy contexts prevailing in their countries for effective operationalization (SADC, 2000; Chibememe et al., 2014; Utete and Mabika, 2025). The Revised Protocol on Shared Watercourses in the SADC (2000) is another legally binding instrument governing water management in the region (SADC, 2000). This protocol focuses on sustainable, equitable utilization of shared watercourses and mandates that member states cooperate to prevent environmental degradation and manage water demand (SADC, 2000). It is premised on the fact that water resource management policies intersect across agricultural and environmental and wildlife institutions in SADC (Utete and Mabika, 2025). Crocodiles are simultaneously regarded as a wildlife resource and an elite agricultural economic commodity across SADC (Child, 1987, Child, 2009; Utete, 2021; Utete and Mabika, 2025). Competing goals of conservation, agriculture and development, human crocodile conflicts (HCCs) and coexistence, and sustainable crocodile ranching for increasing employment, foreign currency generation, tourism, and recreation pose a threat to natural habitats and crocodile populations (Jenkins et al., 2006; Child, 2009; Combrink et al., 2019; Pooley et al., 2019; Utete, 2021). The effect of TWK underlined by local perceptions and use values of crocodiles is an understated factor in regulating the populations and distribution of crocodiles and wildlife in SADC (Jenkins et al., 2006; Pooley et al., 2019; Matanzima, 2022; Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). There is a need to assess water resources conservation policies and evaluate the magnitude of integration of TWK concepts including clarity of the contextual and location-specific traditional uses and threats to crocodiles, explicit inclusion of traditional aspects in water policies, gaps, and potential formal integration for crocodile conservation in SADC (Child, 2009; Dube, 2024; Utete and Mabika, 2025). This systematic review synthesizes the use of crocodiles and their derivatives, inclusion levels of TWK as a confounding variable to assess the threats, and conservation potential for crocodiles. The specific objectives of the study were to assess the uses of crocodiles, inclusion levels, and barriers and benefits of integrating TWK in SADC countries’ wildlife policies for crocodile conservation. Materials and methods The literature search and the Results and Discussion section follow the order of the objectives. Systematic review was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses PRISMA 2020 (Page et al., 2021) framework (Figure 1) and spanned from 1900 to 2025. To assess the dynamics of integrating TWK in wildlife policies for crocodile conservation in SADC, the study used the analycentric framework that included policy content coverage, institutional overlap mapping, and socio-ecological HCC mapping through a hierarchical lens scoping across each SADC state for a valid assessment (Appendix 1). In order to determine the main themes for assessing the challenges of infusing TWK into crocodile conservation in SADC, a qualitative approach, i.e., the six-step Thematic Analysis method with open, axial, and selective coding of factors, was adopted. In this approach, each primary bubble represents a core theme, and the factors within them serve as thematic clusters. The main themes are the broad, overarching categories that emerge at the open coding stage, and then the axial coding stage where the clusters are chosen, that is, the factors within the bubble. The third stage, the selective or refining stage, is where the finer points, which may overlap or connect with bubbles, are then placed, intersecting the bubbles (Figure 2). Results and discussion Traditional water uses of crocodiles in SADC Use of crocodiles and their parts in TWK in SADC is relatively homogeneous with an almost 90% similarity (Table 2). Crocodiles are mainly used for medicinal, spiritual, and subsistence purposes, with specific body parts (e.g., teeth, skin, fat, claws, and underbelly) utilized by various ethnic groups as talismans for magical protection consumption (Pooley, 2016a). Crocodile oils and fat or grease are used to treat ailments like asthma; skin ulcers; rheumatism; burns; chest, ear, and respiratory issues (e.g., lung infection and persistent coughing); and ringworm infection in Namibia, Zambia, and Zimbabwe. Claws and teeth are used as protective amulets against physical attacks and witchcraft in Namibia, Mozambique, Lesotho, Swaziland, South Africa, Botswana, and Zambia (Pooley, 2016a; Nieman et al., 2019). Gallbladder and gallstones are utilized by traditional healers for various treatments including ear and lung infections, and as an antidote to poison (Pooley, 2016a). Crocodile belly skin is used for protection against lightning strikes in the DRC (Pooley, 2016a). Crocodile meat, especially that of juveniles, is consumed in Mozambique, Namibia, and Zimbabwe to enhance longevity, boost sexual performance, and improve health (Pooley, 2016a). Crocodile teeth and skin are used as decorative (e.g., necklaces) and ornamental items for signifying strength and prowess of the hunter and for crafting sandals, bags, and belts in Tanzania, Mozambique, South Africa, and Zambia (Nyirenda, 2015; Pooley, 2016a; Nieman et al., 2019). Crocodiles are feared and revered, and their parts are highly valued for protective purposes (Cott and Pooley, 1972; Matanzima, 2022) against bad luck or witches and spiritual enemies, and act as love charms (muti, muthi) or potions and aphrodisiacs, increasing intelligence, and boosting economic prosperity in Botswana, Lesotho, Kingdom of Eswatini, South Africa, and Zimbabwe (Pooley, 2016a; Nieman et al., 2019). Crocodile parts are used to acquire power, social standing, and wealth (Cott and Pooley, 1972). Traditionally, crocodile meat is considered a delicacy in South African cultures, and is smoked and dried for preservation (Pooley, 2016a). Some riparian communities eat small pieces of raw crocodile meat to acquire immunity from future crocodile and carnivore attacks (Pooley, 2016a). TWK utilizes the presence of crocodiles to assess the health of aquatic ecosystems and fish abundance, inadvertently enhancing the conservation of the species (Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). Crocodiles are considered sacred and associated with local (fady) taboos, as spiritual protective custodians of water resources, which forbid them from being hunted, abetting their conservation in SADC (Pooley, 2016a; Matanzima, 2022; Matanzima and Saidi, 2020). Communities use crocodiles as a totem, for instance, Chiwena (Tonga) with a clan name Munkuli for the crocodile inferring symbolic strength, cunning traits, and endurance in Zimbabwe (Zivave and Muzamba, 2025). Crocodiles represent leadership and symbolize power, and this is used by chiefs to signify authority in Lesotho, Zambia, and Zimbabwe (Matanzima and Saidi, 2020). Crocodile imagery is used in traditional (adulthood) initiation ceremonies emphasizing bravery and the ability to face life’s challenges in Lesotho, Kingdom of Eswatini, and South Africa (Pooley, 2016a; Nieman et al., 2019). Furthermore, crocodiles are associated with the protection of burial sites, with the belief that spirits of ancestors may take the form of a crocodile to guard the deceased in Lesotho, Kingdom of Eswatini, and South Africa (Pooley, 2016a). Despite the modest exploitation at a local scale, traditional use makes a substantial and largely undocumented contribution to crocodile conservation (Pooley, 2016a). The undocumented nature of crocodile uses, adults killed in HCC, the number of eggs illegally collected and nests destroyed, and secrecy of some rituals and mythical powers diminish the contribution of TWK towards crocodile conservation (Utete, 2021; Utete and Mabika, 2025). This necessitates the documented accurate use of crocodiles for TWK purposes for its effective integration into contemporary wildlife policies in SADC. To calculate the similarity in traditional crocodile part usage from literature records, e.g., Pooley (2016a) and Nieman et al. (2019) data, qualitative records were converted into a binary presence and absence matrix. The Jaccard Index was then applied to these matrices to determine similarity, with fidelity levels (Alexiades, 1996) used to analyze specific usage popularity and categorization into distinct levels and for easy interpretation: 0–10 = Negligible; 10–20 = Poor; 20–40 = Average; 40–60 = Fair; 60–80 = Good; and 80–100 = Excellent (Table 2). High fidelity levels (FL %) combined with Jaccard similarity indices (JSIs) demonstrate cultural agreement on utility of a part of an animal (Alexiades, 1996). From the use value indices, there was heterogeneity in use of crocodiles in TWK in SADC (Table 2). Similarity of uses of crocodile parts in TWK is excellent across SADC as different countries, tribes, and ethnical clans utilize the same parts for almost similar purposes and ailments with subtle differences in the secrecy of the processing of concoctions (Pooley, 2016a). Integration of TWK in SADC wildlife policies for crocodile conservation There is heterogeneity in SADC water and wildlife policies with varying levels of integration of TWK elements. In Angola, Decree No. 14 539 (effective from 1969) is a foundational, older, legal act protecting and specifically prohibiting the hunting and export of crocodiles (and their skins). TWK in Angola, particularly among communities in the highlands, plays a crucial role in crocodile conservation by managing sacred aquatic habitats, such as the Lisima Lya Mwono (Source of Life) peatlands. There is a fair coverage and inclusion of TWK practices, rooted in respect for water sources. This acts as “soft conservation” methods that protect key breeding and foraging areas for the crocodiles in Angola (Appendix 1). Communities in the Okavango Delta play a significant role in crocodile conservation by aligning human activity with ecological patterns in Botswana (Gondo, 2022). TWK, regarding seasonal water fluctuations, breeding habits, and navigation, helps manage HCC though modernization threatens this knowledge (Ketlhoilwe and Koketso, 2016; Gondo, 2022). Local communities, such as the BaYei, understand that low-water periods are critical for nesting crocodiles (Ketlhoilwe and Koketso, 2016). Thus, high-intensity human activity such as fishing and reed harvesting is often traditionally managed or restricted during these nesting times to reduce disturbance in crocodile breeding areas (Gondo, 2022). TWK of river systems, including identifying proper soils, slopes, and sandbanks for nesting and quiet, shallow areas for hatchlings, helps communities avoid critical habitats, minimizing disturbance to crocodiles during the nesting periods (Gondo, 2022; Bolaane, 2024). TWK including cultural taboos or respect for the river and river deities prevents overexploitation of water resources and maintained crocodile habitats and, together with intense crocodile ranching, aided population stability in Botswana (Dzoma et al., 2008; Bolaane, 2024). TWK plays a critical role in crocodile conservation in the DRC, particularly through cultural taboos, sustainable, small-scale fishing, and community-led monitoring that perceive crocodiles as an integral part of healthy aquatic ecosystems (Comptour et al., 2016). Local communities near the Lac Télé Community Reserve protect crocodile habitats and clean water and nesting sites, which are vital for biodiversity (Bishobibiri et al., 2023). TWK-based cultural norms are powerful “soft conservation” tools that, while sometimes eroding due to modernization, provide deep ecological regulatory mechanisms (Comptour et al., 2016; Bishobibiri et al., 2023). Local communities apply TWK and use crocodiles as indicators of water quality and safety, recognizing them as “umbrella species” reflecting sustainable aquatic ecosystems (Bishobibiri et al., 2023). TWK is increasingly blended with scientific monitoring, e.g., Geographical Positioning Systems (GPS) and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) mapping, to identify, monitor, and protect key crocodile habitats (Bishobibiri et al., 2023). Integrating TWK with modern conservation policies is crucial for successful crocodile management across SADC not only in the DRC (Bishobibiri et al., 2023; Utete and Mabika, 2025). Cultural taboos (fady) play a critical role in crocodile conservation by designating selected habitats as sacred, restricting hunting, and fostering coexistence in Madagascar (Jones et al., 2008; Andriamarovololona and Jones, 2010). Crocodiles in Madagascar have adapted to living in caves and remnant wetlands, and sacred lakes where local beliefs often protect them from human disturbance and persecution (Jones et al., 2008; Andriamarovololona and Jones, 2010). TWK-based protection of wetlands serves as a safeguard for the remnant crocodile population, which is a keystone species (Andriamarovololona and Jones, 2010). Although HCC exists (Behra, 1996), local beliefs prevent the total eradication of crocodiles in some areas. However, in areas where traditional beliefs have been lost, effective conservation is difficult to implement only through formal legislation in Madagascar (Andriamarovololona and Jones, 2010). Coverage of TWK in wildlife and water conservation policies is good (Appendix 1) because of the need to conserve a low population of the remnant crocodiles left in Madagascar (Jones et al., 2008; Perez et al., 2024). TWK in Malawi combines local ecological understanding of crocodile behavior with traditional spiritual beliefs to manage HCC focusing on risk reduction and community safety (Cott and Pooley, 1972; Fergusson, 2010). Communities living near water bodies such as the Shire River rely on TWK to detect danger and identify crocodile presence through environmental cues (Mbewe, 2019). TWK includes tracking crocodile movements and understanding behavioral patterns, e.g., increased hunting activity proximal to villages during specific conditions such as drought when prey is low in wetlands (Mbewe, 2019). In some communities, TWK regarding sacred sites (e.g., wells and springs) can act as informal, local protection for aquatic habitats and biodiversity in Malawi (Mbewe, 2019). Some villagers utilize TWK for protection from crocodiles and hippopotamuses, alongside or in place of conventional safety measures (Wallace and Leslie, 2008). Traditional leaders play a key role in enforcing social norms around water use, aiming to softly manage resources (Mbewe, 2019). The increasing pressure on rivers for daily necessities means people often enter high-risk areas leading to frequent attacks (Mbewe, 2019). There is a growing effort to document and integrate TWK with scientific approaches for better aquatic ecosystem management in Malawi (Wallace and Leslie, 2008; Mbewe, 2019). Crocodile conservation relies on TWK to monitor aquatic ecosystems, manage habitats, and mitigate HCC in Mozambique (Pooley, 2008; Dunham et al., 2010). Integrating TWK allows for better tracking of breeding sites, habitat quality, and population trends, particularly in remote areas where conventional monitoring is difficult (Pooley, 2016b). Some of the approaches include combining scientific frameworks with local perceptions of aquatic health, for tracking habitat changes like dam construction on major rivers (Dunham et al., 2010). Participatory inclusion of traditional leaders and elders on management committees helps respect cultural norms regarding animal handling and protection of biodiversity (Hughes, 1970; Pooley, 2016b). TWK helps identify troublemaker or problem crocodiles and critical nesting sites, which is essential for managing HCC in Lake Cahora Bassa (Pooley, 2008; Wallace and Leslie, 2008; Dunham et al., 2010). TWK is used to assess the natural history of crocodiles, especially in areas lacking scientific data (Dunham et al., 2010). Effective conservation in Mozambique requires integrating TWK with modern management policies, particularly in wetlands where habitat modification is frequent (Wallace and Leslie, 2008). Mozambique manages crocodile habitats in conjunction with similar practices in Zimbabwe and South Africa, focusing mainly on managing HCC (Wallace and Leslie, 2008; Dunham et al., 2010; Pooley, 2016b). TWK act as “soft conservation” tools, regulating resource use, protecting nesting sites, and enforcing prohibitions on killing or eating crocodiles, which are often considered sacred or associated with water spirits (McGregor, 2005; Utete, 2021; Matanzima, 2022; Simakani et al., 2024). Local communities use the crocodile (often referred to as wena or ngwenya) as a totem, which fosters respect and bans the harming of the animal, acting as a soft conservation approach (Matanzima, 2022; Zivave and Muzamba, 2025). Crocodile presence is sometimes managed through rituals that connect the species to water spirits, which influences how community members approach, manage, or avoid certain water bodies (Matanzima, 2022). TWK encourages sustainable fishing and astute water use, which limits habitat destruction and HCC in wetlands in Zimbabwe (Wallace and Leslie, 2008; Chakuya and Reniko, 2018; Chakanyuka and Utete, 2022; Simakani et al., 2024). TWK in South Africa plays a vital role in crocodile conservation, focusing on maintaining wetland connectivity, habitat health, and monitoring water levels (Pooley, 2016b; Pooley et al., 2019). TWK promotes coexistence, though increasing HCC and environmental changes necessitate its fusion with modern “scientific” management (Pooley, 2008; Combrink et al., 2019). Indigenous communities use TWK to preserve specific isolated waterholes and wetlands during dry seasons as crucial refuges for crocodiles (Pooley, 2016b, Pooley et al, 2019). Understanding crocodile behavior like nesting times and hunting spots (Calverley and Downs, 2017) is part of TWK used to minimize HCC, particularly by separating livestock from high-risk rivers (Pooley, 2016b; Pooley et al., 2019). There is substantial research on crocodiles in South Africa, but it is heavily skewed towards the use of “modern” science for data collection and water pollution analyses and impacts on nesting sites (e.g. Ashton, 2010; Botha et al., 2011; Ferreira and Pienaar, 2011; Du Preez et al., 2018; Humphries et al., 2022; Dabrowski et al., 2025) and often incorporates TWK as an afterthought (Pooley, 2016b; Combrink et al., 2019). TWK in Eswatini recognizes the deep interconnection between natural aquatic environments and human culture (Pooley et al., 2019). Inherent TWK guides the sustainable use of wetlands, contributing to biodiversity conservation and decreased HCC (Pooley, 2016b; Pooley et al., 2019). TWK intertwines with local water sources and wildlife, promoting respectful interaction with the environment including predators (Pooley et al., 2019). Eswatini’s National Adaptation Plan (NAP) processes have included initiatives to collect and use TWK for predicting weather patterns and managing ecological changes, which impacts how water-dependent species like crocodiles are managed (Pooley et al., 2019). TWK combined with formal conservation helps in understanding the movements and habits of crocodiles, crucial for safety and reducing HCC in areas outside of protected zones (Pooley, 2016b; Pooley et al., 2019). TWK is centered on respecting sacred water bodies, regulating river usage, and preserving riparian habitats, thus enhancing crocodile conservation in Lesotho (Mokgoatšana, 2020). TWK-based cultural belief systems, such as regarding crocodiles as ancestral spirits, play a significant role in their conservation in Lesotho (Mokgoatšana, 2020). Local TWK frames crocodiles within folklore and taboos, fostering a culture of respect rather than active eradication (Mokgoatšana, 2020). This TWK supports conservation by curbing unnecessary killing of crocodiles in local waterway (Pooley et al., 2019). Understanding TWK is essential for developing sustainable, inclusive, and culturally appropriate crocodile management strategies in Lesotho and across SADC (Pooley et al., 2019). In Tanzania, particularly in the Eastern Arc catchment area, TWK is used for sustainable aquatic management (Scott and Scott, 1994). TWK including monitoring water levels in seasonal pools helps track crocodile concentrations, manage habitats, and mitigate HCC (Mwita, 2010). Local communities near the Upper Zigi and Lower Mngeta rivers are engaged in tracking water quality and availability, and monitoring the health of aquatic ecosystems (Scott and Scott, 1994; Mwita, 2010; Assefa et al., 2025). TWK involves understanding the seasonal migration and concentration of crocodiles into smaller pools during the dry season i.e., August to January (Assefa et al., 2025). Residents often use TWK to identify problem crocodiles, usually large individuals (>3 m) that threaten human safety, and manage interactions in shared water sources (Lamarque et al., 2009). TWK recognizes the role of wetlands in supporting crocodile populations, which are recognized for their role in the ecological system balance (Scott and Scott, 1994; Lamarque et al., 2009; Mwita, 2010; Assefa et al., 2025). TWK helps identify hotspots, nesting sites, and seasonal movement patterns, crucial for mitigating HCC and ensuring the species’ conservation in the Okavango on the sides of Angola, Botswana, and Namibia (Aust et al., 2009; Bolaane, 2024; Gondo, 2022). Local residents use TWK to identify and locate crocodile slide marks along the banks to determine high-risk areas such as breeding and nesting zones, allowing for safer management of river resources (Bolaane, 2024). Residents use TWK to understand the behavior of the animals to minimize the risk of attack while accessing the water (Le Roux, 2023). The Management Plan for crocodiles for the Kunene region proposes integrating TWK with scientific research as imperative to create effective and place-based conservation strategies (Le Roux, 2023). TWK in Zambia, particularly along the Zambezi River, plays a vital role in crocodile conservation by informing spatial management and reducing HCC (Wallace and Leslie, 2008; Chomba et al., 2012). TWK helps identify critical nesting areas, allowing community-based management to protect habitats while navigating cultural views that often revere or fear crocodiles as powerful aquatic spirits (Musambachime, 1987). Local communities identify vital habitat features such as sandy banks for nesting and quiet, deep pools that overlap with urban areas (Musambachime, 1987). The overarching perception is of hate towards the crocodiles in Zambia (Musambachime, 1987; Nyirenda, 2015; Pooley, 2016b; Milupi et al., 2020). Integrating TWK and conventional scientific data on wetlands is important in managing crocodile populations in Zambia (Fergusson, 2010; Milupi et al., 2020). Studies (e.g., Wallace and Leslie, 2008; Nyirenda, 2015) in the Lower Zambezi show distinct and manageable crocodile population variations between protected and non-protected areas, although the research largely excluded the valuable contribution of TWK in Zambia. A discernible pattern is that countries with a well-developed crocodile ranching industry (Average score) normally have fair, average, good, or even excellent coverage of TWK in wildlife and water policies in SADC (Appendix 1). These countries place a premium on optimizing biodiversity economic gain from crocodiles through encouraging ranching for export related purposes with a view to maximize profits from coexistence with the species (Child, 2009; Utete and Mabika, 2025). SADC countries with a poorly developed crocodile ranching sector mostly have a poor or negligible coverage of TWK in water and wildlife policies (Jenkins et al., 2006). An exception is Mauritius where the remnant crocodile population (numbering approximately 2,500) is well protected for mostly recreational and tourism purposes and is well covered in the main wildlife policy (Jenkins et al., 2006). There is aquatic and spatial neglect by wildlife authorities in as much as terrestrial national protected areas receive robust policy scaffolding: the implementation of the regulations is low, and this is compounded by weak boundaries between terrestrial and water systems and more so in shared water systems (Nyirenda, 2015; Utete and Mabika, 2025). All SADC states have a local conservation status of Protected for crocodiles, ostensibly, because of strict adherence to the IUCN classification scheme that they have ratified (Jenkins et al., 2006). However, policies outside of protected areas tend to be highly reactive (Nyirenda, 2015). Instead of pro-active coexistence, the regulatory focus is on Problem Animal Control (PAC) (Chakanyuka and Utete, 2022). Complexities in infusing TWK use of crocodiles in SADC water and wildlife policies Across SADC, the common challenges affecting TWK use of crocodiles and its integration in wildlife and water policies are indicated in Figure 2. The main issue concerns increasing HCC, which is largely driven by negative (hatred) perceptions and retaliatory action towards the species because of past negative experiences (Musambachime, 1987; Fergusson, 2010; Pooley et al., 2019). Anthropogenic activities e.g., urbanization, mining, and agriculture, lead to habitat destruction and threaten the conservation status of the species (Utete, 2021). Crocodile trafficking fueled by greed and socioeconomic pressures lead to poaching and whitewashing of skins (Figure 2). Erosion and marginalization of TWK-associated traditions, taboos, myths, and folklores punctuated by intergenerational knowledge transmission gaps threaten crocodile populations in SADC (Zivave and Muzamba, 2025). TWK is marginalized and excluded and poorly documented in modern “scientific” techniques (Figure 2). This is compounded by marginalization of (and weakened) TWK institutions in modern scientific crocodile conservation programs in SADC. Lack of community benefits weakens observance of TWK in crocodile conservation initiatives threatening the conservation status of the species (Figure 2). It is imperative to note that cultural variation in secrecy, ritual knowledge, and social taboos systematically bias the literature review toward published ethnographies that may not represent the full diversity of practices (Matanzima, 2022; Assefa et al., 2025; Chiosso and Tamburini, 2025). A detailed hierarchical analysis of challenges impeding crocodile conservation in the context of TWK inclusion in wildlife policy is rather complex and contextual for each SADC country (Utete and Mabika, 2025; Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). Variation in TWK policy integration is driven by institutional rigidities, socio-demographic differences, epistemological biases, and inherent local governance structures (Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). Legal recognition gaps and differing worldviews between formal science and indigenous practices create distinct barriers across different SADC states (Utete and Mabika, 2025). Context- and location-specific stochastic and dynamic factors such as the absence of formal statutory recognition for TWK in national water laws, fragmented administrative bodies, and centralized authorities that largely block multi-level knowledge transfer and power asymmetries reflected by dominance of bureaucratic control over community-based rules and customary leadership impede its inclusion at the country level across SADC (Utete and Mabika, 2025). In addition, the systemic privilege (coupled with colonial perceived inferiority or out datedness) granted to modern or Western science evidence over (unquantified and often undocumented) indigenous knowledge-based findings varies at the country level, though the net effect is exclusion or piecemeal inclusion of TWK in water and wildlife policies in SADC (Matanzima, 2022; Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). Geospecific variations in climate and water scarcity, demographic composition, political will, gender dimensions, ethnic and cultural variations in human–nature relations, and property rights imply that there is variability in erosion of intergenerational communication channels and consequently the inclusion levels for TWK in SADC. Social Network Analysis (SNA) of crocodile conservation governance in Africa evaluates knowledge flows, power asymmetries, and institutional linkages across stakeholder networks (Costa, 2021). Structural examination of the wildlife policies and institutional strength identified critical gaps in HCC management and cross-border resource sharing in SADC (Costa, 2021; Utete and Mabika, 2025). In the SNA for crocodile conservation in SADC, structural relationships and knowledge flows consider information bottlenecks, bridging social capital and feedback loops (Dahlberg and Sandström, 2024). There are information bottlenecks as scientific data on crocodile population dynamics are trapped within centralized research institutions such that conservation education and regulations fail to reach fringe communities (Dahlberg and Sandström, 2024). Wildlife authorities and local traditional authorities then act as vital network bridges facilitating two-way knowledge exchange between marginalized resource users and top-level policymakers (Mahefarisoa et al., 2025). Regardless, there are inefficient feedback loops resulting in weak horizontal ties between local water management boards and wildlife authorities and delayed responses to HCC (Mahefarisoa et al., 2025; Gontse et al., 2026). The power asymmetries and stakeholder networks for crocodile conservation include centrality dominance where wildlife agencies and commercial ranching and trophy-hunting operators typically hold a high degree of authority, monopolizing decision-making and economic incentives derived from sustainable use (Utete and Mabika, 2025). Within the conservation hierarchy, the communities proximal and mostly affected by HCC occupy peripheral network positions with low structural bargaining power, breeding institutional distrust and non-compliance (Gontse et al., 2026). This results in local negative perceptions, exploitative TWK uses, and retaliatory habitat destruction, threatening crocodile conservation. There is asymmetric distribution of resources as financial and regulatory power flows downward through hierarchical channels rather than collaborative platforms reducing local capacity for mitigation (Gontse et al., 2026). Besides trust deficits, river basins crossing multiple national boundaries suffer from uncoordinated policies reducing the effectiveness of wildlife and water conservation in SADC (Gontse et al., 2026). TWK implementation for water resources including crocodile conservation is either excluded or poorly coordinated through fragmented institutional linkages (Utete and Mabika, 2025). A plausible intervention target may be to strengthen local community inclusion and integration and decentralizing authority towards local co-management frameworks in order to improve compliance with regulated TWK use of crocodiles and their parts, inadvertently mitigating HCC in SADC. Benefits of infusing TWK in crocodile conservation policies and initiatives in SADC Traditional insights often compensate for limited scientific monitoring of freshwater crocodiles, especially in remote areas (Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). Integrating TWK in distinguishing between local and troublemaker (PAC) crocodiles and modern ecological data allows for more targeted, cost-effective, and locally accepted management strategies (Pooley et al., 2019; Utete, 2021). TWK, which emphasizes honoring the water, often encourages safer human practices in crocodile habitats, directly reducing HCC (Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). Elitist crocodile ranching is not part of the overall population estimates for the species in the IUCN Red List assessments (Jenkins et al., 2006). Captive crocodiles, ostensibly, because of the commercialized orientation of the ranches, do not contribute much to the wild populations mostly in countries such as Botswana, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, and Namibia (Dzoma et al., 2008; Pooley et al., 2019; Utete, 2021). However, crocodile farming has created subsidized wild security with the crocodile ranches acting as an economic shield for the species where some of the revenue from the crocodile farms actively funds anti-poaching patrols (Fergusson, 2010) and population surveys (Musambachime, 1987; Child, 2009; Dunham et al., 2010; Utete, 2021) that the state agencies cannot afford. The rate of development of crocodile ranching differs across SADC (Utete and Mabika, 2025). Countries with highly developed crocodile ranching operations, e.g., Namibia, Malawi, Mozambique, South Africa, Zambia, and Zimbabwe, have high inclusion levels of crocodile aspects in wildlife policies (Utete and Mabika, 2025). However, this does not necessarily translate into high inclusion levels for TWK in the crocodile conservation context (Appendix 1). There are widespread deficiencies in municipal wastewater treatment coverage in literature though most internal and shared river basins face rising loads of microplastics, heavy metals, nutrients, and urban effluents across SADC (Mashamba et al., 2025). Crocodiles as apex indicators of watershed health show persistent endocrine-disrupting chemicals in their tissues and heavy metals also bioaccumulate, leading to long-term reproductive organ size reduction and declining juvenile recruitment rates (Botha et al., 2011; Du Preez et al., 2018). However, the aspect of assessing the levels of pollution across water ways and their effects on crocodiles has been approached in a fragmented manner with research concentrated in South Africa (e.g., Ashton, 2010; Botha et al., 2011; Ferreira and Pienaar, 2011; Du Preez et al., 2018; Humphries et al., 2022; Dabrowski et al., 2025; Davhana et al., 2025) and anecdotal studies reported in Zambia and Zimbabwe (Lemaire et al., 2024). These studies have adopted the modern conservation science lens, and if integrated with citizen science aspects in TWK, they can form definitive warning systems of water pollution and its effects at the apex end of aquatic ecosystems (Ferreira and Pienaar, 2011; Utete, 2021). Future studies must include the perspectives of TWK practitioners who are the main drivers and barriers of crocodile use and conservation in wetland systems in SADC (Utete and Mabika, 2025; Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). There is a need to develop a region-wide wildlife conservation policy that specifies recognition of TWK as legitimate knowledge. There is a need to consider the inclusion of TWK holders in decision-making and incorporation of TWK practices and practitioners in management plans. The baseline must be adequate resource allocation for TWK documentation and legal protection for TWK-relevant sites not only for crocodile conservation but also for all hydrobionts (Utete and Mabika, 2025). There are challenges in trying to balance TWK with modern conservation, which requires navigating a complex friction pathway between cultural heritage and biodiversity protection (Jenkins et al., 2006). For instance, zootherapy-based uses of crocodile parts for medicinal purposes, while beneficial for locals, represent an existential threat to the species’ wild population (Pooley, 2016b; Nieman et al., 2019). It is important to not outrightly condemn TWK practices as outdated and traditionalistic and anti-conservation without acknowledging the indigenous way of life and access benefit sharing and local indigenous community rights that in some way simultaneously exploit and conserve the species (Chibememe et al., 2014). Policy developers need to delicately balance cultural valuation and conservation realities as skewed analysis (towards modern conservation science) echoes colonial era exclusions of indigenous rights and community rights (Chibememe et al., 2014). The way forward is enhancing community-based frameworks that align protection goals with local governance where indigenous communities living alongside and proximal to dangerous wildlife such as crocodiles are integrated and help to co-manage and value populations rather than perceiving negatively the wildlife policy and regulations across SADC (Chibememe et al., 2014; Utete, 2021; Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). Exploring captive breeding or ethical substitution to satisfy TWK medicinal demands without depleting wild stocks represents and offers a more nuanced and balanced long-term conservation solution for crocodiles in SADC (Utete and Mabika, 2025). Recommendations for future research and implementation The TWK aspect inclusion is low across SADC, and it is a priority area for future research (Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). Wildlife policies differ across SADC (Utete and Mabika, 2025). However, policy reform must consider integrating TWK in crocodile conservation based on a common framework. Such a framework should document place-based knowledge (challenges and opportunities), initiate identification and acknowledge information transmission channels and gaps, champion developing a database of authentic knowledge holders and site-specific information, prioritize the identification of wetland ecosystem services, and motivate the identification of TWK use and management strategies while specifying responsible traditional and modern governance institutions and structures and providing clarity on mandates (Asad et al., 2022; Asad et al., 2023). TWK can be operationalized by integrating local bio-indicators into monitoring networks, empowering customary community-based governance, and co-designing nature-based infrastructure (Asad et al., 2022). This includes tracking local ecological indicators and training local elders and residents to record water table levels, flow rates, and pollution using generational, place-based observation methods (Asad et al., 2023). The key is to establish partnerships with traditional leaders and local water masters to enforce seasonal conservation rules, sacred riverbank protections, and equitable distribution. Integrating crocodile conservation into regional wildlife policies in SADC requires specific institutional, financial, and scientific resources, including legal frameworks, community-based management capacity, and ecological monitoring data (Utete and Mabika, 2025). Formalizing TWK, often tied to TEK, in wildlife and environmental policies carries risks like cultural commodification, bureaucratic rigidity, and epistemic marginalization (Assad et al., 2023). Some of the risks include erosion of local authority, institutional friction, codification mismatch, and loss of sacred context and ethics that originally enforced conservation (Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). Regardless, formalization and codification must be done in a calculated manner so as not to empower specific elite groups or local leaders over others, creating new local conflicts over resource access and benefits (Utete and Madzivanyika, 2026). In communities where PAC and crocodile ranching and wildlife policies prioritize commercial interests over local rural safety, communities feel marginalized (Gandiwa et al., 2021). When policies in areas experiencing intense fishing net damage and fatalities lack community-centric compensation, redress, or protection, it leads to severe retaliatory killing, poaching of juveniles, and the destruction of crocodile eggs (Chakanyuka and Utete, 2022; IUCN, 2022). These barriers, combined with high levels of fear and human–wildlife conflict, often paint crocodiles as enemies rather than components of a shared ecosystem needing protection (Somaweera et al, 2019, Somaweera et al, 2020; IUCN, 2022). While contemporary, crocodile assessments indicate that crocodiles are globally secure, localized populations face severe degradation from anthropogenic activities (IUCN, 2022). To mitigate this invisible extinction risk, there is a need to increase the role of TWK in crocodile conservation through gradual phased integration into contemporary SADC water and wildlife policies.
