Antony † * School of Psychological Sciences, CHRIST (Deemed to be University), Bangalore, Karnataka, India Article metrics View details Abstract Introduction: Understanding the psychological determinants of pro-environmental behavior is critical in addressing the escalating climate crisis. Despite increasing recognition of an important predictor of environmentally responsible actions, the role and interaction of eco-emotions remain insufficiently examined. This gap is particularly evident in non-Western contexts such as India. Methods: This study aimed to examine the influence of environmental identity on pro-environmental behavior, with particular attention to the role of eco-emotions within this relationship. The study further examines the mediating roles of eco-anxiety and eco-hope, and the moderating effect of eco- hope on the identity–anxiety–behavior pathway. A cross-sectional survey design was employed, collecting data from 395 adults across India (ages 18–60, Indian residents, fluent in English, no diagnosed mental illnesses) via convenience sampling using online questionnaires. Measures included the Environmental Identity Scale by Clayton, Hogg Eco-Anxiety Scale by Hogg, Climate Change Hope Scale by Li and Monroe, and Pro-Environmental Behaviour Scale by Markle. Regression, mediation, and moderated mediation analyses were conducted using SPSS PROCESS. Results: Results indicated that environmental identity significantly predicted pro-environmental behavior, with eco-anxiety partially mediating this association. Eco-hope did not independently mediate pro- environmental behavior and did not moderate the indirect effect of identity through eco-anxiety. Discussion: These findings suggest that interventions promoting environmental identity should simultaneously cultivate constructive concern and realistic hope, optimizing engagement in sustainable behaviors. The study contributes to the understanding of identity–emotion–behavior dynamics in environmental psychology and underscores culturally contextualized strategies to foster climate action. 1 Introduction Environmental challenges have moved from the margins of public concern to the center of global discourse. The accelerating pace of climate change, loss of biodiversity, and depletion of natural resources are not abstract projections but lived realities affecting millions daily (IPCC, 2021). Environmental change threatens human health by disrupting the physical environment, natural and human systems, socioeconomic conditions, and health-system functioning. As a threat multiplier, it may undermine or reverse decades of health progress (WHO, 2023). Environmental psychology research indicates that people experience a range of complex emotional reactions in response to climate change—such as fear, anxiety, worry, hope, and even despair. These emotions are consistently associated with pro-environmental action, primarily correlational (Ogunbode et al., 2022; Pihkala, 2020; Sangervo et al., 2022). As a result, understanding how these emotional responses relate to behavioral intentions has become a central focus in climate psychology. Consequently, sustainability has shifted from being primarily a policy objective to an essential requirement for protecting human survival and well-being. Psychology holds a unique position in this context because while technological innovation and policy interventions are essential, individual and collective human behavior stands as a particularly nuanced obstacle (Gifford, 2014). The inconsistent behaviors of people in relation to the environment, even when well-informed and outwardly concerned, have emerged as one of the central enigmas of environmental psychology (Kollmuss and Agyeman, 2002). To understand this dichotomy, researchers have increasingly examined environmental identity, which provides (Clayton, 2003) an important framework for understanding why some individuals consistently act in accordance with their ecological values, whereas others do not. The trajectory of research in environmental psychology reflects a significant shift from surface-level attitudes toward deeper self-conceptual frameworks. Early research was dominated by rational-choice models, such as the Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen, 1991), role of attitudes, norms, and intentions in shaping environmentally relevant actions. However, these purely rationalist models could predict consistent behavior change only to a limited extent, as shown by the failure of information campaigns (Schultz and Zelezny, 1999). This accelerated the shift of the field toward identity-based frameworks, which argue that when people see the environment as an integral part of themselves, pro-environmental behavior becomes intrinsic and hence, more consistent (Clayton & Opotow, 2003). For example, individuals who internalize themselves as environmentalists are more likely to make sustainable choices, even when such actions demand personal inconvenience (van der Werff et al., 2013). However, environmental identity alone does not fully account for the disparity in ecological behavior. People with strong environmental identities often recycle but still travel by aeroplane, or they express concern while engaging in high-consumption lifestyles (Whitmarsh and O’Neill, 2010). This discrepancy suggests that cognitive identification is a critical prerequisite, and it must be augmented by additional psychological determinants. The problem becomes clearer when emotions are introduced into the actions. Human action is not exclusively driven by rational deliberation; rather, the association between identity and behavior is significantly strengthened or attenuated by affective states (Stets and Burke, 2000). Emotions function like weather systems within the psyche, fleeting yet powerful in shaping urgency and possibility (Frijda, 1986). Within the domain of environmental psychology, studies on climate emotions include eco-guilt, eco-grief, eco-anger, and eco-shame (Pihkala, 2022), most studies heavily focused on eco-anxiety, whereas eco-hope has been studied the least. Eco-anxiety can be defined as the chronic fear and worry associated with ecological catastrophes and the eventual ecological collapse (Clayton and Karazsia, 2020). More broadly, eco-anxiety may be conceptualized as a pervasive concern regarding the deteriorating relationship between humanity and the natural environment. Recent research has identified neurological correlates of eco-anxiety (Carlson et al., 2024), and its manifestations have been documented in both children (Léger-Goodes et al., 2022) and adolescents (Niedzwiedz et al., 2023). Eco-hope has been conceptualized as an emotion with a constructive orientation toward the environmental future, characterized by pragmatic belief in one’s capacity to make a difference and not by blind optimism (Ojala, 2012; Li and Monroe, 2019). Although eco-anxiety and eco-hope are conceptually distinct, they are not mutually exclusive and may co-occur, interacting in ways that shape behavior more profoundly than either emotion could alone (Sangervo et al., 2022). However, empirical research examining the extent to which eco-anxiety and eco-hope jointly mediate the relationship between environmental identity and pro-environmental behavior remains limited. Existing studies have predominantly investigated eco-anxiety and eco-hope as separate constructs (Clayton and Karazsia, 2020; Stevenson and Peterson, 2016), resulting in fragmented theoretical insights that fail to account for their complex interplay. Notably, hope does not negate concern about climate change; instead, it can coexist with anxiety by channeling the arousal from worry into constructive motivation. Empirical studies indicate that hope may buffer against the heterogeneous effects of climate-related anxiety and thereby support more adaptive engagement in environmental action (Kurth and Pihkala, 2022; Kühner et al., 2024). The Extended Parallel Process Model (EPPM) by Witte (1992) provides a useful framework for understanding the interaction between anxiety and hope. It proposes that when individuals perceive a threat as serious and believe an effective response is possible, they engage in “danger control” actions. In the context of climate change, eco-anxiety may heighten perceived threat severity, while hope may strengthen efficacy beliefs—the belief that one can act effectively. Together, these emotions may increase intentions to engage in pro-environmental behavior. Despite the appeal of this framework, few empirical studies have directly examined how climate-related anxiety and hope jointly to shape such intentions. Evidence highlights the importance of addressing this gap. Similarly, eco-hope has been connected to resilience, sustained engagement in activism, and lifestyle changes that prioritize sustainability (Stevenson and Peterson, 2016). Nevertheless, existing findings are largely derived from Western populations, leaving unresolved whether these relationships operate similarly across different sociocultural contexts (Ojala, 2015). In collectivist societies, eco-hope may be understood more in terms of collective responsibility and shared agency than individual efficacy (Kumar and Tripathi, 2020). Failure to account for such cultural variation may lead to inappropriate generalizations and the development of ineffective interventions for diverse populations. Recent reports suggest that eco-anxiety is increasing among Indian youth, many of whom express fear and uncertainty regarding the future (Rao, 2020). In contrast, empirical research on eco-hope in India remains limited. The lack of studies examining the potential interplay between these emotions represents both a theoretical and practical gap. Without understanding how Indian populations negotiate eco- anxiety and eco-hope within their environmental identity and translate them into sustainable behaviors, interventions may overlook vital resources that sustain engagement. The present study embarks with a focused objective to examine how environmental identity relates to pro-environmental behavior through the mediating roles of eco-anxiety and eco-hope. Specifically, investigates whether these emotions amplify, balance, or nullify the motivational influence of one another. The study aims to ask how the smoke alarm of eco-anxiety and the compass of eco-hope can be aligned so that environmental identity translates into consistent, meaningful pro-environmental behavior. The hypotheses for the study were as follows: H1: Environmental identity predicts pro-environmental behavior. H2: Eco-anxiety positively mediates the relationship between environmental identity and pro-environmental behavior. H3: Eco-hope positively mediates the relationship between environmental identity and pro-environmental behavior. H4: Eco-hope significantly moderates the indirect effect of environmental identity on pro-environmental behavior via eco-anxiety. 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Research design The present study employed a cross-sectional study design to examine the relationship between environmental identity and pro-environmental behavior, while testing the influencing roles of eco-anxiety and eco-hope on the above-mentioned pathway. 2.2 Participants A power analysis was conducted prior to data collection using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2009). The analysis indicated that for detecting a medium effect size (f² = 0.15) in a multiple regression model with up to seven predictors, a minimum sample size of 103 was required to achieve 0.80 statistical power at an alpha level of.05. Given the complex mediation and moderated mediation models planned to be performed, a larger sample was targeted. Ultimately, data were collected from a total of 411 participants. After the removal of outliers, the final sample was reduced to 395 participants. Among these, 254 (64.3%) were female, and 141 (35.7%) were male. Most participants fell within the 18–30-year age range (n = 198, 50.1%), followed by 31–45 years (n = 99, 25.1%) and 45–60 years (n = 98, 24.8%). Most participants lived in urban areas (n = 253, 64.1%), had completed postgraduate education (n = 178, 45.1%), were employed (n = 193, 48.9%), were single (n = 212, 53.7%), and had no children (n = 225, 57.0%). Inclusion criteria required participants to be (a) between the ages of 18 and 60 years, (b) currently living in India, and (c) having proficiency in English. Exclusion criteria were (a) the presence of a diagnosed psychiatric condition, (b) residence outside India, and (c) inability to comprehend English. 2.3 Measures 2.3.1 Environmental identity Environmental identity was measured using the Environmental Identity (EID) Scale (Clayton, 2003). The scale consists of 14 items, such as “Being a part of the ecosystem is an important part of who I am,” rated on a 3-point Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Higher scores reflect stronger environmental identity. In the present study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = .917). 2.3.2 Eco-anxiety Eco-anxiety was measured using the Hogg Eco-Anxiety Scale (Hogg et al., 2021). This scale includes 13 items, such as “I feel anxious about the impacts of climate change,” rated on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate greater eco-anxiety. In the present study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = .920). 2.3.3 Eco-hope Eco-hope was assessed using the Climate Change Hope Scale (Li and Monroe, 2019). This scale comprises 11 items, such as “I believe I can contribute to solving environmental problems,” with responses recorded on an 7-point Likert scale ranging from −3 to +3, reflecting varying levels of disagreement and agreement. Higher scores indicate greater levels of eco-hope. In the present study, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = .747). 2.3.4 Pro-environmental behavior Pro-environmental behavior was measured using the scale developed by Larson et al. (2015). This scale consists of 13 items, such as “Conserve water or energy in my home,” with responses recorded on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). Higher scores indicate greater engagement in pro-environmental behavior. In the present study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (α = .835). In addition to the primary psychological constructs, data regarding socio-demographic details, including gender, age, socioeconomic status, highest level of education, marital status, number of children, area of residence (urban/sub-urban/rural), and occupation. 2.4 Procedure Data were collected via an online survey created using Google Forms™. The survey was disseminated widely through social media platforms (e.g., WhatsApp™, Instagram™, Facebook™), following a convenience sampling strategy. Upon accessing the form, participants were presented with an informed consent form that explained the study purpose, inclusion and exclusion criteria, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and data usage; only those who provided consent could proceed with the questionnaire. The survey required approximately 20–25 minutes to complete. No compensation or incentives were provided for participation. Identifying information (e.g., names, email addresses, IP addresses) was not collected. Responses were made anonymous, stored in a password-protected folder, and accessible only to the authors. The data responses were encrypted. 2.5 Data analysis The data were screened for quality and completeness. All responses with missing data were removed initially. Then, completion times were examined using the survey timestamp, and unrealistically fast responses were excluded. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 with Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Hayes, 2018). The underlying parametric assumptions of normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity were evaluated and found to be adequately met for subsequent statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables and covariates. Pearson product–moment correlation analyses were conducted to examine bivariate relationships among environmental identity, eco-anxiety, eco-hope, and pro-environmental behavior. Scale reliabilities were assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Regression-based mediation and moderated mediation analyses were conducted using the PROCESS macro for SPSS (Hayes, 2018) to test the relationships hypothesized earlier; the mediation analyses were conducted using Model 4, and the moderated mediation analyses were conducted using Model 14. All indirect effects were tested using bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples and 95% confidence intervals. Model 14 was chosen to test moderation of the second stage of the mediated pathway, that is, whether eco-hope conditions the strength of the association between eco-anxiety and pro-environmental behavior, rather than the pathway from environmental identity to eco-anxiety. The index of moderated mediation and its bootstrapped confidence interval (Hayes, 2015) were used to evaluate this effect directly, consistent with current recommendations for testing conditional indirect effects without relying on the sequential significance tests of earlier causal-steps approaches (Preacher et al., 2007). 2.6 Ethical considerations Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection; participants were informed that their data would be used for research purposes without disclosure of their identity. The study was reviewed and approved by the Research Conduct and Ethics Committee of CHRIST (Deemed to be University) (approval number RCEC/00884/01/26, dated 28 January 2026). Participants were assured that their data would remain confidential and that no identifying information would be disclosed in any publication or report. They were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the survey at any stage before submission without any consequences. 3 Results 3.1 Descriptive statistics and correlational analysis The mean scores and standard deviations (M ± SD) for environmental identity, pro- environmental behavior, eco- anxiety and eco-hope were (5.35 ± 1.01), (2.95 ± 0.71), (1.01 ± 0.67) and (1.12 ± 0.74), respectively. R²= 0.008; p=0.118. 4 Discussion The present study examined the mechanisms by which environmental identity influences pro-environmental behavior through eco-anxiety, as well as whether eco-hope moderates this relationship. The findings indicate that environmental identity shapes pro-environmental behavior primarily through its emotional consequences rather than through a direct, uncomplicated translation of self-concept into action. Environmental identity predicted behavior both directly and indirectly through eco-anxiety, which functioned as a partial mediator; eco-hope, in contrast, neither mediated this relationship nor altered the strength of the anxiety-behavior link. The above has contributed to a deeper understanding of how emotional processes shape environmentally relevant constructs. Consistent with H1, environmental identity significantly predicted pro-environmental behavior (β = 0.19, p < 0.001), although the variance explained was modest (R² ≈ 3.6%). This finding aligns with prior research emphasizing that individuals who define themselves as “environmentalists” or feel connected to nature demonstrate higher engagement in sustainable behaviors (Clayton, 2003; Whitmarsh et al., 2011; Bankole and Adesina, 2024; O’Halloran, 2025). In this context, identifying with environmental values may encourage behaviors consistent with that identity. Similarly, Value- Belief-Norm Theory (Stern, 2000) says that values and self-conceptions strongly influence intentions and actions, which corroborates the observed link between environmental identity and pro-environmental behavior in this Indian sample. Notably, eco-anxiety emerged as a partial mediator of the relationship between environmental identity and behavior, supporting H2. Environmental identity was positively associated with eco-anxiety (β = 0.242, p< 0.001), which in turn positively predicted higher pro-environmental behavior (β = 0.293, p < 0.001). Bootstrapped mediation analysis confirmed a significant indirect effect through eco-anxiety. This supports the notion that environmentally identified individuals experience heightened concern about climate change, which can drive them to perform protective behaviors (Hogg et al., 2021). Moderate levels of anxiety may have more adaptive function, as opposed to extreme distress, thus spurring engagement rather than paralysis; this is consistent with the Protection Motivation Theory (Rogers, 1983), which suggests that appraisal of threat produces behavioral coping when perceived efficacy is sufficient. The results are also consistent with previous research showing that worry and concern about environmental degradation can motivate pro-environmental action, especially when accompanied by perceived personal efficacy and alignment with one’s environmental self-concept (Panno et al., 2021). Thus, the extent to which individuals identify with the environment may strengthen the motivational influence of eco-anxiety on sustainable behavior. Additionally, the construal-level perspective (Trope and Liberman, 2010; Spence et al., 2012), which says that anxiety narrows the psychological distance between the individual and the threat and makes climate change more concrete, personally relevant, and immediate, leading to a state associated with an increased likelihood of behavioral response; and the Norm Activation Model (Schwartz, 1977; Stern, 2000) which suggests that heightened awareness of environmental consequences can activate a personal moral obligation to act, thus, converting diffuse concern into a felt sense of responsibility, provide a further explanation. Eco-anxiety may therefore function not simply as generalized arousal but as the emotional route through which consequences are registered as personally consequential, and through which a moral norm to act is engaged. However, in contrast, eco-hope did not mediate the identity–behavior relationship. While environmental identity was a strong predictor of eco-hope (β = 0.453, p < 0.001), eco-hope itself was not significantly associated with pro-environmental behavior (r = 0.03, p = 0.499). The findings were contradictory to existing literature (Ogunbode et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2024). where hope is frequently identified as a facilitator of proactive engagement (Li and Monroe, 2019). One interpretation could be that hope, in isolation, eco-hope fails to generate the perceived urgency necessary to initiate concrete behavioral change. While Fredrickson’s (2001) Broaden-and-Build Theory of Positive Emotions posits that positive emotions like hope expand cognitive flexibility and broaden coping resources, it is possible that the effects of eco-hope are indirect and long-term, serving to strengthen resilience or sustain motivation over time, rather than translating into immediate behavioral shifts. Consequently, eco-hope may require a complementary affective trigger, such as eco-anxiety, to drive observable pro-environmental behavior. Finally, cultural and contextual factors within India may further shape the manifestation of eco-hope; collectivist cultural orientations, for instance, may prioritize communal environmental solutions over individual behavioral adjustments (Kumar and Mishra, 2020). Contrary to H4, eco-hope did not moderate the indirect effect of identity on pro-environmental behavior via eco-anxiety. The conditional indirect effect analyses demonstrated that the higher environmental identity and eco-anxiety, which in turn related to higher pro- environmental behavior was numerically at high levels of eco-hope (indirect b ≈ 0.06) and weakest at low hope (b ≈ 0.03), the index of moderated mediation was not significant. These results suggest that hope did not significantly amplify the identity-anxiety-behavior pathway. The moderated mediation analysis provided additional clarification. Although the indirect effect of environmental identity on behavior through eco-anxiety remained significant across varying levels of eco-hope, the index of moderated mediation was not significant. This finding indicates that eco-hope did not consistently modify the strength of the anxiety-to-behavior pathway, despite some variation in conditional effects. Environmental identity and eco-anxiety together accounted for a modest proportion of variance in behavior (R² = .117), and the identity-only model explained just 5.1%. This suggests that internal, affect-based processes are only part of the picture. In a context such as India, where infrastructure, cost, and convenience can constrain which pro-environmental behaviors are practically available to an individual regardless of their internal states, structural and economic factors plausibly account for a substantial share of the remaining variance — a possibility the present psychological model was not designed to test, but one worth foregrounding. Collectively, these findings indicate that eco-anxiety and eco-hope did not function as complementary emotional forces, and rather, might have diverged into functionally distinct affective processes. Eco-anxiety, as a threat-based emotion, is closely linked to perceptions of risk and urgency, which facilitate faster action. In contrast, eco-hope may not operate at immediate behavioral engagement. These findings challenge the assumption of the synergistic operation of positive and negative environmental emotions and instead support a more nuanced model in which different emotions fulfill distinct motivational roles. From a practical perspective, these results suggest that interventions designed to promote pro-environmental behavior should not rely exclusively on hope; while hope may support long-term engagement, it is likely insufficient to initiate behavioral change without additional elements that enhance perceived urgency or personal relevance. Several limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design restricts causal inference, and the mediation model should be interpreted as reflecting statistical rather than temporal relationships. Reliance on self-report measures introduces potential biases, including common method variance (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Furthermore, operationalizing eco-hope as a unidimensional construct may have obscured important distinctions between different forms of hope, particularly those related to agency and efficacy. These findings extend the literature on environmental identity and pro-environmental behavior by highlighting a complex interplay of affective pathways. They confirm that eco-emotions are neither unidirectional nor isolated. Their co-occurrence may not necessarily amplify or attenuate behavioral outcomes. This finding is inconsistent with the findings of Ojala (2012), who found that the greater the hope, the greater the environmental engagement, whereas promoting hope without corresponding concern may foster complacency (Pihkala, 2020). Consequently, the dual activation of concern and solution-oriented optimism may be most effective in motivating sustainable behaviors. Theoretical implications of this study are multifaceted. First, the study reinforces identity-based motivation, demonstrating that the self-concept of “environmental” activates a cascade of affective and behavioral responses. Second, it advances understanding of eco-emotions by illustrating that eco-anxiety functions as a behavioral catalyst, while eco-hope did not modulate this indirect pathway. This expands conceptualizations of environmental engagement beyond linear models, and they may play non-interactive roles. From a practical perspective, interventions aiming to foster pro-environmental behavior should simultaneously target identity reinforcement and emotional framing. Educational campaigns could nurture an environmental self-concept, evoke moderate eco-anxiety through awareness of climate threats, and cultivate solution-oriented hope by highlighting effective initiatives. For example, presenting local success stories in renewable energy or conservation projects may be associated with heightened motivation, enhancing both concern and optimism, thereby translating into behavioral engagement. These insights may be particularly salient in India, where environmental literacy is growing but perceived individual agency may be constrained. Several limitations of the study warrant consideration. The cross-sectional and self-report design of the study prevents causal inference and may be subject to social desirability and reporting biases. In addition to the design of the study, the use of convenience samples introduced potential selection bias for the participants. Another key limitation is that confirmatory analysis was not conducted on the tools used in the present sample. The modest variance explained in behavioral outcomes suggests that other influential factors such as social norms, moral obligations, or specific behavioral skills remain unmeasured (Steg and Vlek, 2009). The conceptualization of eco-hope is still nascent, and its measurement may lack sensitivity, partially explaining the null mediation. Additionally, cultural and socio-economic diversity within the Indian context could influence both emotional experience and pro-environmental behavior expression; future studies should examine these moderators. Lastly, the use of convenient sampling methods may have introduced selection bias, thereby limiting the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Several avenues have emerged from the study, which may be further explored in the future. Longitudinal and experimental research could be conducted to clarify causal dynamics, for example, by manipulating eco-hope and eco-anxiety to assess their independent and interactive effects on pro-environmental behavior. Expanding the range of eco-emotions to include guilt, anger, or grief may provide a more comprehensive picture of affective drivers of action (Ojala, 2012; Pihkala, 2020). Qualitative approaches could refine the construct of eco-hope, exploring how different populations conceptualize hope in relation to climate solutions. Finally, intervention studies could evaluate whether identity-based educational programs, coupled with emotional framing, enhance sustainable behavior effectively across diverse cultural and socio-economic contexts. 5 Conclusion In conclusion, the study aims to uncover the intricate interplay between environmental identity, eco-emotions, and pro-environmental behavior. It underscores that identity alone does not suffice to drive sustainable action; instead, emotions, particularly eco-anxiety and eco-hope, act as critical channels and modulators of behavior. The findings indicate that eco-anxiety functions as a significant pathway linking identity to behavior, while eco-hope does not exert a direct or moderating influence on this process. These results suggest that environmental emotions are functionally differentiated, rather than uniformly contributing to behavioral outcomes. Specifically, eco-anxiety acts as an activating mechanism that translates environmental concern into action, whereas eco-hope may not serve a regulatory role among environmental identity, anxiety and behavior. This distinction underscores the necessity of moving beyond simplified assumptions that positive and negative emotions may not operate synergistically. Furthermore, the findings highlight the importance of socio-cultural context in shaping how emotional experiences are translated into action. Overall, this study contributes to a more nuanced and theoretically grounded understanding of affect-driven environmental behavior, emphasizing the need for models that account for both the functional specificity of emotions and the contexts in which they occur. While this research provides a robust foundation, future research should adopt longitudinal or experimental designs to clarify causal pathways, as cross-sectional data limit temporal inferences. Expanding the emotional repertoire to include eco-guilt, eco-anger, or grief, as well as examining self-efficacy and social norms, could further elucidate mechanisms underlying pro-environmental behavior. Moreover, culturally diverse and age-varied samples would enhance the generalizability of these findings, revealing whether the anxiety–hope interplay holds across contexts. Qualitative work exploring subjective experiences of eco-hope may also refine its conceptualization and measurement, contributing to more precise intervention designs.
