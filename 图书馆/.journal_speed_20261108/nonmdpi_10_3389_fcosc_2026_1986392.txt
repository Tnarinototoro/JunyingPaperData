You can register here Notify me Abstract The Creole garden is commonly framed as an insular Caribbean homegarden or a remnant of rural heritage. This case study recenters it in Amazonian French Guiana, where family gardens connect Indigenous landscapes, African diasporas, older Saint Lucian and Martinican migrations, and contemporary Haitian mobility. We describe the Creole garden as a small, largely non-mechanized and multifunctional agroecosystem in which food crops, fruit trees, medicinal, technical and ritual plants, and small livestock are organized through knowledge of seasons, lunar cycles, soils, crop associations and vegetation strata. Drawing on published ethnobotanical research and intermittent fieldwork conducted between 2015 and 2026 with approximately twenty gardeners along the French Guianese coast and in Saül, we examine how these gardens conserve biodiversity not simply by containing species but by keeping plants, varieties, knowledge and relationships in use. The contemporary role of Haitian gardeners is especially revealing: alongside field observations suggesting a decline of full multifunctional gardens in some Guianese Creole households, migrants recreate Caribbean gardening practices through Amazonian species, transnational plant circulation and precarious access to land. In dialogue with the concept of the relational garden, we argue that Creole gardens are both networks and domestic metabolisms connecting people, plants, animals, soils, materials and invisible forces. Their conservation requires supporting the social and material conditions that sustain cultivation, exchange and care.
