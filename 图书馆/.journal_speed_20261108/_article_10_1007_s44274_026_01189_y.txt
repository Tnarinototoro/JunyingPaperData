A firm-level disclosure index is constructed via text mining based on the TCFD framework. Two-way fixed effects models show a positive association, with a one-standard-deviation increase in disclosure corresponding to approximately a 3.6% increase in energy efficiency, robust to endogeneity tests. Transition risk disclosure has a stronger association with energy efficiency than physical risk; the difference widens further when standardized by each dimension’s dispersion. Mediation analysis indicates green innovation and eased financing constraints are partial channels whose separately estimated indirect effects sum to approximately 20% of the total effect, though the direct effect dominates. Heterogeneity analysis reveals stronger effects in state-owned, heavily polluting, and carbon pilot region firms, with insignificant effects outside pilot regions; the association also strengthens significantly after the launch of the national carbon market in 2021. The study contributes to the literature by shifting the focus from capital market reactions to real operational outcomes, distinguishing between physical and transition risk disclosure, and highlighting the role of institutional context in determining whether climate disclosure produces substantive effects on energy performance. Corporate Communication Environmental Economics Environmental Psychology Environmental Communication Environmental Impact Financial Reporting 1 Introduction Climate change poses an increasingly material challenge to corporate strategy and financial performance. The increased frequency of extreme weather, stringent regulations on emissions, and the fast growth of alternative energy technologies make climate risk an essential element of corporate strategic decision-making processes and financial performance [ 5 , 23 ]. In light of these facts, climate risk information disclosure has gained significant traction among regulators and financial markets around the world as an essential mechanism of communication between businesses and their external stakeholders. The Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) framework has gained widespread acceptance since its development in 2017, while the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) has made significant strides in the development of standards for climate disclosures by adopting IFRS S2 in 2024 [ 33 ]. The issuance of China’s Guidelines for Corporate Sustainability Reporting (Trial) in 2024 marked an important step forward in the climate reporting field. Many studies have focused on the economic outcomes of climate risk disclosure in the capital market context, including the efficiency of stock pricing, cost of capital, and investment decisions. More recently, the scope of inquiry has broadened to examine how corporate governance characteristics interact with sustainability outcomes, such as the moderating role of board diversity in the relationship between technological adoption and ESG performance [ 36 ]. However, these studies commonly focus on how market participants and governance structures respond to the disclosure process while paying little attention to how climate risk disclosure leads to real-world operational actions, particularly those enhancing energy efficiency. Energy efficiency represents an immediate way of reducing carbon footprints for firms [ 27 ] and is the focal point of business transformation of the dual carbon goals. Moreover, increased disclosure does not necessarily translate into substantive environmental improvement, as firms may also engage in symbolic disclosure or impression management to satisfy external expectations without undertaking real operational changes (K. Li et al., [ 16 ]). Whether climate risk disclosure can move beyond a legitimation exercise and actually drive measurable gains in energy efficiency therefore remains an open empirical question. If climate risk disclosure fails to translate into energy management improvements, the effectiveness of such disclosure policies is likely to be compromised. This leads to three interrelated research questions. First, does climate risk disclosure have a positive association with corporate energy efficiency? Second, do physical risk disclosure and transition risk disclosure differ in their association with energy efficiency? Third, through what mechanisms does disclosure relate to firms’ energy performance? This study employs a dataset of Chinese A-share listed firms for the years 2012 to 2024 and applies text-mining approaches to establish an individual firm-based index of climate risk disclosures, investigating its connection to the energy efficiency of firms and the mechanism underlying this process. The study makes three contributions to the existing literature. First, it shifts the analytical focus from capital market reactions to real operational outcomes by examining energy efficiency, thereby testing whether disclosure leads to tangible behavioral change rather than merely influencing investor perceptions. Second, by separating physical risk disclosure from transition risk disclosure, it reveals that these two types of signals differ in their effectiveness in promoting energy efficiency improvement, with transition risk disclosure showing a stronger association. Third, by exploiting variation across carbon trading pilot and non-pilot regions, it highlights the role of institutional context in determining whether disclosure produces substantive effects or remains largely symbolic. The mediation analysis through green technology innovation and financing constraint relief further provides insight into the channels that connect disclosure and behavioral change within firms. 2 Literature review and hypotheses development 2.1 Climate risk disclosure and corporate energy efficiency The literature on the economic consequences of climate risk disclosure can be broadly organized into three strands. The first strand examines capital market outcomes. Studies in this area analyze the effect of disclosure on stock price crash risk, pricing efficiency, funding costs, and investor behavior [ 13 , 14 , 19 , 30 ], as well as the significance of carbon emissions for asset valuation [ 4 , 5 ]. Firms exposed to greater physical risk due to climate change tend to decrease leverage following the implementation of the Paris Agreement, and this remains true even when controlling for factors such as credit rating and company-specific factors [ 11 ]. The second strand focuses on corporate behavioral responses. Empirical studies reveal a positive correlation between climate information disclosure and green innovation outputs in the Chinese A-share sample [ 12 , 31 ], while emissions are negatively correlated with climate information disclosure [ 34 ]. However, green patents essentially capture innovation incentives, and carbon emissions reflect the cumulative impact of multiple variables. Neither indicator directly measures how firms adjust their operational use of energy resources. Energy efficiency, as an intermediary measure between innovation inputs and emissions outputs at the process level, offers a better explanation of how organizations adapt their use of energy resources in production but has not yet been incorporated into the climate disclosure analytical framework. The third strand addresses the determinants of corporate energy efficiency, which has focused primarily on policy instruments. Quasi-experimental analysis based on China’s carbon emission trading mechanism shows that carbon markets improve energy efficiency at the provincial level by encouraging innovation and achieving optimal energy composition [ 27 ]. With respect to firms, involvement in the emissions trading system improves energy efficiency at the corporate level, which has been demonstrated through China’s pollution rights trading pilot [ 18 ]. The consumption intensity of coal used in power generation plants located in carbon trading pilot areas has seen a substantial reduction [ 41 ]. In addition, there has been a reduction in coal consumption at the firm level as well as coal intensity [ 44 ]. These findings consistently highlight the significance of market-oriented approaches to improve energy efficiency, although the mechanism behind them is fairly limited in scope, based mainly on prices and incentives. The effect of information disclosure channels has not yet been empirically confirmed. Moreover, a growing body of research on ESG greenwashing suggests that disclosure does not automatically lead to substantive operational improvement. In the Chinese context, CEO turnover has been found to increase ESG greenwashing behavior [ 22 ], while government green subsidies can mitigate it by improving information transparency [ 43 ]. Taken together, the existing literature has extensively examined capital market reactions to climate disclosure and the role of carbon markets in promoting energy efficiency, but has not yet tested whether climate risk disclosure itself drives real improvements in firms’ energy management, nor addressed the possibility that such disclosure may be symbolic rather than substantive. Despite these concerns, legitimacy theory provides an analytic approach to examine how disclosure may nonetheless promote substantive change. Firms use information revelation to prove to society that their practices and processes comply with social norms and values, thus acquiring legitimacy [ 7 ]. However, the effectiveness of this mechanism depends on whether the institutional environment makes symbolic compliance costly [ 28 ]. By making climate risk disclosures within their annual report, a company commits itself to be monitored by regulators, investors, and the public. In China’s institutional context, this monitoring pressure operates at multiple levels: government performance evaluation systems for state-owned enterprises, stock exchange disclosure requirements, and public scrutiny through media and investors. A visible gap between disclosed climate commitments and actual operational practices may trigger not only reputational damage but also regulatory penalties associated with greenwashing (K. Li et al., [ 16 ]). This concept is further explained by stakeholder theory, which indicates that as environmental issues become more recognized, investors, lenders, and government authorities put different but consistent pressures on companies regarding their energy savings and emissions reduction performance [ 9 ]. By increasing the ability of external stakeholders to understand how much energy a firm consumes, climate risk disclosure can help enhance corporate responsibility for energy efficiency matters. Such responsibility, which may be conveyed externally as well as internally, can encourage firms to implement energy efficiency initiatives in operational decisions. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H1: Climate risk disclosure is positively associated with corporate energy efficiency. The climate risk reporting process is not standardized because the heterogeneity of the disclosure itself can produce different types of reports, each leading to different outcomes. Reporting physical risk involves reporting information on extreme weather conditions, natural disasters, and other physical risks related to the climate which have either happened or are expected to happen. Such information is reported in a backward-looking manner, providing limited direction for subsequent corporate action. In comparison, transition risk disclosure has a relationship with future considerations, such as changes in policies for carbon pricing, the introduction of new sources of power, and changes in consumer preference. These signals are more direct and relevant for decision-making purposes. Beyond the informational distinction, there is also an institutional basis for expecting a stronger effect from transition risk disclosure. Carbon pricing mechanisms, emissions trading schemes, and regulatory mandates for low-carbon transition create binding cost pressures that firms must internalize. When firms disclose transition risks such as carbon costs or regulatory compliance requirements, these signals are directly linked to quantifiable financial consequences, making them more actionable than physical risk reports that describe events largely beyond the firm’s control. Physical risk disclosures have a higher impact on emissions in the short term but from a long-term perspective, transition risk disclosure has a higher impact on emission reductions [ 34 ]. The improvement in energy efficiency is a medium- to long-term strategy that requires sustained investments and systematic adaptability. Therefore, the forward-looking and institutionally grounded signals conveyed by transition risk disclosure align more closely with this characteristic. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H2: Transition risk disclosure has a stronger positive association with corporate energy efficiency than physical risk disclosure. 2.2 Mediating mechanisms The effect that climate risk disclosure has on energy efficiency may also operate indirectly, through changes in how firms allocate their resources. The two mechanisms are discussed below. GI acts as an important mediator between information disclosure and improvement in energy efficiency. Climate change-related risk disclosure increases firms’ awareness of risks related to the innovation cycle and clean technology opportunities. In cases where management structures and discloses information on climate change-related risks in annual reports, it acts as a trigger that forces firms to reconsider their technological innovation paths, leading to more R&D expenditure in energy-efficient operations, clean technology equipment, and energy management systems. In the Chinese A-share sample, a positive connection has been established between the disclosure of climate issues and firms’ green innovation productivity, and the effect is significantly stronger for those firms facing stricter financial constraints [ 12 , 31 ]. Climate disclosure under the TCFD framework is also linked to increased innovation efforts in terms of green patents [ 10 ]. The increased number and improved quality of green patents indicate progress in innovations for energy-efficient technologies capable of reducing energy intensity. The digitalization capabilities that enable green innovation further demonstrate the correlation between improved information-processing ability and better environmental performance [ 8 ]. In other words, climate risk disclosure can promote energy efficiency by triggering firms’ green innovation efforts. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H3: Green technology innovation mediates the relationship between climate risk disclosure and corporate energy efficiency. The alleviation of financing constraints is another transmission mechanism. Energy efficiency improvement programs involve considerable investment costs, including technology upgrading, process improvement, and creation of management systems. The availability of funding acts as a crucial constraint on a firm’s capability to implement its energy-saving plans. High-quality climate risk disclosure reduces the information gap between the firm and capital markets, helping investors and creditors to better assess a firm’s climate risk management ability. This in turn leads to a decrease in the risk premium required from such firms. The correlation between climate risk disclosure and financing constraints is negative [ 30 ], and firms that have disclosed more about carbon tend to perform better by achieving a reduction in the interest cost of their debt financing [ 35 ]. However, the extent to which reduced financing constraints translate into innovation engagement depends on ownership structure; state-owned and foreign ownership moderate this relationship in distinct ways across industries and regions [ 37 ]. Firms with strong ESG performance gain access to investors, which results in reduced financing constraints and provides capital for green innovation [ 39 ]. Conversely, financially constrained firms cut back on the amount allocated to green R&D because such costs are associated with productive externality activities that take time to yield results [ 40 ]. Energy efficiency is one of these cost-intensive activities. Relief from financial constraints widens the scope within which firms can make energy efficiency investments, creating a separate mechanism distinct from the technology innovation approach. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H4: The alleviation of financing constraints mediates the relationship between climate risk disclosure and corporate energy efficiency. 3 Methodology 3.1 Sample and data The sample consisted of Chinese A-share listed firms from 2012 to 2024. The year 2012 was when the carbon emission trading pilot schemes started in China; thereafter, policies related to climate began to be released more frequently, and mentions of climate risk started to appear more often in annual reports, providing enough data for text mining. Full-text annual report data were obtained from CNINFO (cninfo.com.cn). Financial and corporate governance data was obtained from CSMAR databases, data for carbon emission from CSMAR ESG databases and wind database, green patents from CNRDS databases, while the temperature of cities was obtained from China Meteorological Administration. The carbon emission data used in this study primarily cover Scope 1 direct emissions and Scope 2 indirect emissions from purchased electricity, as reported by the two databases. For firm-year observations available in both CSMAR ESG and Wind, the CSMAR ESG figure was used as the primary source, and Wind data were used to supplement cases where CSMAR ESG coverage was missing. The coverage of carbon emission data is notably lower in the earlier years of the sample period (2012–2016), as environmental disclosure was largely voluntary during that phase; the retained sample therefore tends to include firms that were more proactive in environmental reporting during those years. The excluded variables included financial companies, those marked as ST or *ST, and all missing values on the independent variable. Winsorization was performed on all continuous variables at the 1st and 99th percentiles. 3.2 Text-mining approach for climate risk disclosure The construction of the climate risk disclosure index involves four components: seed dictionary development, text preprocessing, TF-IDF weighting, and cosine similarity validation. The seed dictionary was built based on the following principles of the TCFD’s pillars, namely, governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets. Previous studies have used approaches such as text-mining to build TCFD index scores using a taxonomy of climate-related terminology within corporate reports [ 21 ]. This study continues this approach of building indices based on taxonomies, but applied specifically to the Chinese-language context. The basic structure for choosing vocabularies was based on the climate exposure keyword method developed for the context of English-language communication [ 26 ] as well as the dictionary of managerial climate attention created for Chinese listed firms [ 15 ]. In turn, the above framework was further extended and adapted by introducing new keywords reflecting the Chinese policy environment such as “dual carbon goals,” “national carbon market,” and “dual control of energy consumption.” After several rounds of manual screening and removal of terms that have ambiguous definitions, the final dictionary was subdivided into a physical risk dictionary and a transition risk dictionary. Two broad climate-related terms (“climate change” and “global warming) were only included in calculating the CRD composite score but not in the PCRD or TCRD dimensions to avoid classificatory confusion for higher order concepts. The physical risk dictionary includes 46 keywords about extreme weather phenomena, hydrological disasters, temperature anomalies, oceanic and cryospheric changes, and ecological degradation. The transition risk dictionary includes 64 keywords regarding carbon pricing and carbon trading, policy and regulation, green finance, energy transition and technology substitution, and carbon management. The full keyword lists are provided in Appendix Tables A1 - A3 . Text extraction was limited to the Management Discussion and Analysis (MD&A) section of annual reports, which contains the most substantive narrative disclosure of climate-related risks and strategies. Financial statements, auditor reports, and standardized tabular content were excluded to reduce noise. The extracted text was then preprocessed using the Python library jieba for Chinese word segmentation. Stop words, numbers, and punctuation were removed, keeping only those words that had substantive semantic meaning. TF-IDF weighting was then performed on the preprocessed text to assign weights that emphasize words appearing frequently in one specific document but infrequently across the whole corpus, thereby capturing climate risk language specific to individual firms’ annual reports. Based on the physical risk and transition risk dictionaries, the composite climate risk disclosure score ( CRD ), physical risk disclosure score ( PCRD ), and transition risk disclosure score ( TCRD ) were calculated for each firm-year observation. To validate the reliability of these indices, a reference corpus for climate risk disclosure was constructed from three sources: the TCFD recommendations and implementation guidance documents, major Chinese climate policy texts including the national carbon peaking action plan and dual carbon policy guidelines, and exemplary climate risk disclosure sections from annual reports of firms rated A or above in CDP climate assessments. The corpus contains approximately 320 text segments totaling over 150,000 Chinese characters. A cosine similarity measure was obtained from the TF-IDF vectors corresponding to the firm’s annual report and that of the reference corpus. The cosine similarity measures generated are values between zero and one. The Pearson correlation between the cosine similarity scores and the CRD index is 0.826 ( p < 0.01), indicating strong convergent validity between the text-mining measure and the reference benchmark. 3.3 Variable definitions Corporate energy efficiency ( EE ) is proxied by a carbon emission efficiency indicator, defined as the ratio of operating revenue to total carbon emissions, expressed in natural logarithm form: \(E{E_{i,t}}=\ln (Revenu{e_{i,t}}/CO{2_{i,t}})\) . This measure reflects the economic output generated per unit of carbon emitted. In China’s industrial context, where fossil fuels account for the dominant share of primary energy consumption, carbon emissions are closely tied to energy inputs. As a result, carbon emission efficiency and energy efficiency are highly correlated at the firm level, making the former a valid proxy for the latter, particularly in sectors where fossil fuel combustion is the primary energy source. This proxy is most applicable to such industries; for firms in low-emission service sectors, where the link between carbon output and energy input is weaker, the interpretation should be treated with caution, and the two concepts should not be regarded as interchangeable in that context. As a robustness test, total energy efficiency of the factors obtained using the SBM-DEA approach [ 32 ] is used as an alternative dependent variable to examine the sensitivity of findings to measurement methods. The CRD , using the text-mining methodology mentioned above, is included in the regression equation with a one-period lag. The lag is applied because of the time taken for the behavioral response after the disclosure process and to avoid reverse causation issues. The CRD index contains two dimensions: PCRD and TCRD . As for the mediators, GI is defined as the natural logarithm of the number of green patents applications plus one. For Financing constraints ( SA ), it is measured as the absolute value of the SA Index obtained using firm size and firm age. Higher values indicate more severe financing constraints. In the mediation pathway, a negative coefficient of CRD on SA indicates that disclosure reduces financing constraints, while a negative coefficient of SA on EE indicates that more severe constraints lower energy efficiency; the product of these two negative coefficients yields a positive indirect effect, meaning that disclosure improves energy efficiency by easing access to finance. The SA index also has the distinct feature that the individual elements included in this measure have low vulnerability to endogeneity from financial choices, leading to stronger exogeneity in panel regressions. Because firms’ industry classification is relatively stable during the course of the sample period, firm fixed effects control for time invariant variables that are specific to an industry, but no industry fixed effects are included. The standard errors are clustered by firm. The coefficient \({\beta _1}\) is the parameter of primary interest; a significantly positive estimate would support H1. In the heterogeneity analysis, CRD is replaced by PCRD and TCRD in separate regressions, and the difference between the two coefficients is tested to evaluate H2. Mediation effects are tested using the bootstrap method. Taking the GI pathway as an example, the following two equations are estimated: $$G{I_{i,t}}=\alpha +a \cdot CR{D_{i,t - 1}}+\gamma {X_{i,t}}+{\mu _i}+{\lambda _t}+{\varepsilon _{i,t}}$$ (2) $$E{E_{i,t}}=\alpha +c^{\prime} \cdot CR{D_{i,t - 1}}+b \cdot G{I_{i,t}}+\gamma {X_{i,t}}+{\mu _i}+{\lambda _t}+{\varepsilon _{i,t}}$$ (3) The indirect effect \(a \times b\) is evaluated by constructing a 95% confidence interval through 1,000 bootstrap replications. If the confidence interval excludes zero, the indirect effect is statistically significant. The same process is followed when it comes to evaluating the path through financing constraints. As compared to the sequential testing method [ 2 ], the bootstrap analysis does not rely on the assumption that the indirect effect is normally distributed. Endogeneity is addressed using two-stage least squares (2SLS). The first instrument is the annual mean temperature anomaly ( TempAno ) in the city where the firm is registered. The higher degree of deviation from past temperatures in a particular city is related to the greater frequency and intensity of climate risk discussion in the annual reports of firms [ 26 ], thus fulfilling the relevance criterion. Regarding the exclusion restriction, the key concern is whether temperature anomalies could directly affect energy efficiency through channels such as increased cooling or heating demand. Several features of the research design mitigate this concern. First, the CRD variable is lagged by one period, so the temperature anomaly in year t-1 is linked to CRD in year t-1, while EE is measured in year t. Any direct effect of temperature on energy consumption operates contemporaneously rather than with a one-year delay. Second, year fixed effects absorb nationwide climate trends in any given year, and firm fixed effects absorb location-specific systematic temperature differences. What remains as identifying variation is the city-level deviation from its own historical mean in a given year, which represents idiosyncratic short-term weather fluctuation rather than structural changes in energy infrastructure or production technology. The second instrument is the mean CRD of peer firms operating in the same industry and province. This is based on the notion of peer effect: firms in the same institutional environment tend to imitate each other’s disclosure practices, creating a correlation between a firm’s own CRD and the average disclosure level of its peers. The relevance of this instrument is confirmed by a strong first-stage F-statistic. Regarding the exclusion restriction, the concern is that firms in the same industry and province may face common policy shocks or regulatory campaigns that directly affect energy efficiency. Firm fixed effects absorb time-invariant industry and regional characteristics, while year fixed effects control for common temporal shocks. However, province-level time-varying policy interventions cannot be fully ruled out, which is why each instrument is used in a separate just-identified 2SLS estimation rather than combined in a single overidentified model; the consistency of second-stage results across the two separate estimations strengthens the overall identification by providing independent cross-validation. Based on the rule of thumb stating that an F-statistic for the first stage exceeding 10 indicates strong instrumental variables [ 29 ], the Kleibergen-Paap rk LM statistic is also reported to assess model identification. 4 Empirical results 4.1 Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis The descriptive statistics of the main variables are presented in Table 2 . The mean value of EE is 8.30 with a standard deviation of 1.58 and values ranging between 4.25 and 12.21. High variability means that there is a significant disparity in the carbon emission efficiency of high-carbon emitting industries like electric power and steel as compared to low-carbon industries such as information technology and pharmaceuticals. This indicates that Chinese listed firms disclose issues related to policies, regulations, and the low-carbon transition in their annual reports more than climate-induced physical risks. GI shows an average value of 1.284 and a minimum value of zero. There are many companies that did not file any green patents during the study period. Innovation distribution is highly concentrated. The distributions of the control variables are broadly consistent with those reported in comparable studies, with Size averaging 22.347 (corresponding to total assets of approximately 5 billion RMB), Lev averaging 0.423, and ROA averaging 0.041. In the full specification in column (4), the coefficient is 0.843 (t = 2.61, p < 0.01). The association between CRD and energy efficiency is robust across all specifications. Given that the dependent variable is expressed in natural logarithmic form, the economic magnitude is obtained via exponential transformation: a one-standard-deviation increase in CRD (0.042) shifts ln(EE) by 0.843 × 0.042 = 0.035, corresponding to approximately 100 × [exp(0.035) − 1] ≈ 3.6% increase in the level of energy efficiency. H1 is supported. In the case of sub-dimensional regressions, there is a distinct contrast evident between the two forms of disclosure. The PCRD coefficient in column (5) is 0.497 and significant only at the 10% level (t = 1.76) while the TCRD coefficient in column (6) is 1.108 and significant at the 1% level (t = 3.48), roughly twice the magnitude in unstandardized terms. A cross-model Wald test (suest) confirms that the difference between the two coefficients is statistically significant (χ 2 = 4.63, p = 0.031). The unstandardized coefficients alone do not fully capture the relative importance of each disclosure dimension, as PCRD and TCRD differ in their dispersions (SD = 0.018 and 0.031, respectively). In standardized terms, a one-standard-deviation increase in PCRD corresponds to a 0.009-unit increase in ln(EE) (0.497 × 0.018), whereas the corresponding figure for TCRD is 0.034 (1.108 × 0.031), approximately 3.8 times larger. This reinforces the conclusion that transition risk disclosure has a substantially stronger association with energy efficiency than physical risk disclosure. H2 is supported. The positive relationship between firm size, profitability, and energy efficiency is supported by the negative relationship between leverage and energy efficiency and the positive relationship between institutional ownership and energy efficiency ( p < 0.05). The coefficients on ownership type, firm age, board size, and board independence are all statistically insignificant. The coefficient on CRD is 0.791 (t = 2.38, p < 0.05), slightly lower than the baseline estimate but still significant, indicating that the results are not driven by industry-level temporal confounders. Under the log-linear specification, a one-standard-deviation increase in CRD corresponds to approximately a 3.6% increase in energy efficiency. This figure captures the marginal contribution of information disclosure alone, net of carbon pricing, environmental regulation, and other policy instruments that jointly shape energy management. This finding raises a more fundamental question beyond statistical significance. The question is to what extent disclosure can actually be associated with changes in the behavior of firms. Previous studies have focused mainly on market reactions to climate disclosures, including stock pricing efficiency, financing costs, and investor portfolio decisions. The negative association between carbon emission levels and the level of disclosure seems more consistent with firm practice [ 34 ]. However, carbon emission levels incorporate the effects of different variables, making it difficult to identify the isolated contribution of disclosure to changes in emissions. Energy efficiency, which more directly reflects operational adjustments, allows a better approximation of the marginal association between disclosure and firm practices. The effect size is broadly in line with related studies; for instance, Wang et al., [ 34 ] report coefficients of a similar order of magnitude when examining climate disclosure and carbon emissions. A 3.6% annual improvement attributable to a single channel is far from negligible, particularly given that disclosure-driven adjustments tend to unfold gradually through organizational processes and may compound over multiple reporting cycles. The degree of attenuation reflects the friction that occurs when signals transmitted through disclosure translate into operational actions. This degree of attenuation is not fixed. It varies depending on the degree to which the signals provided by disclosure are actionable. This difference in effect between transition risk disclosure and physical risk disclosure provides a clear example. Physical risk disclosures provide information about past and future events involving severe weather, natural disasters, and other shocks outside the firm’s control. Firms can hardly be expected to react proactively when receiving this information. Transition risk disclosure deals with carbon pricing, emission rights, technological replacement, and other future factors that would directly impact the cost and competitiveness of the firm. In terms of valuations, firms exposed to high levels of transition risk without any active response have faced valuation discounts in recent years, and the market reaction to transition risk has been more pronounced than to physical risk [ 17 ]. The findings indicate that the relationship established between valuations and actionability applies to operational practice as well. Actionability determines the speed with which a signal translates into action. With China’s adoption of its dual carbon targets in 2020 and the establishment of its carbon market in 2021, transition risks changed from being viewed as vague policy targets into concrete issues that need to be addressed, and the focus shifted toward relevant policies. This institutional shift likely amplified the actionability of transition risk signals during the later portion of the sample period. Whether a signal is actionable depends not only on its content but also on how the firm’s institutional context translates the signal into either a cost or a benefit. This is particularly true when comparing carbon trading pilot areas and non-pilot areas: there is a clear statistical significance and magnitude of disclosure effect in pilot areas, while in non-pilot areas there is no such significance. Carbon markets create institutions that monetize climate risk [ 38 ]. Carbon price signals turn the transition risks discussed in the disclosure into quantifiable operating restrictions. Province-level research also indicates that carbon markets could increase energy efficiency through innovation and optimization of energy structure [ 6 ]. The findings reported in the current study corroborate the earlier research but highlight the additional dimension of information disclosure in the process. With respect to a well-functioning carbon market, there is an economic imperative for businesses to translate their disclosures into actual energy conservation initiatives. Furthermore, the risk of non-compliance due to the disparity between stated intentions and behavior is much higher in such a case. In places where pilot projects have not been initiated, there is no such mechanism, and so their disclosure activity primarily amounts to information transfer and legitimation. The time heterogeneity results reinforce this interpretation: the coefficient on CRD nearly doubles in the post-2021 period, coinciding with the launch of the national carbon market that extended institutional pressure beyond the original pilot regions. Without such institutional backing, climate risk disclosure risks functioning as a legitimation tool rather than a driver of substantive improvement, consistent with growing evidence of ESG greenwashing in the Chinese context (K. Li et al., [ 16 ]; Niu, [ 22 ]). The credibility of disclosure may therefore depend on complementary factors such as third-party assurance, analyst scrutiny, and regulatory enforcement strength. The effect of uncertainty about carbon prices on business behavior further supports this claim [ 24 ]. The detection of relatively stronger effects for state-owned enterprises and firms in highly polluting industries is based on the same reasoning: the accountability processes within the government and the enforcement effectiveness of environmental regulations serve as additional institutional pressure that strengthens the link between disclosure and operational outcomes. Furthermore, the positive effect of climate governance at the government level on disclosure effectiveness has been established (Zhang, [ 42 ]). Price mechanisms facilitate carbon trading, whereas administrative mechanisms ensure government accountability. This combination forms the institutional basis that allows climate risk reporting to be associated with changes in firms’ activities. These findings also carry implications for the comparison of disclosure regimes. Whereas the TCFD framework and ISSB IFRS S2 operate primarily in voluntary or comply-or-explain settings, China’s evolving requirements are moving toward a semi-mandatory model. The results suggest that the effectiveness of disclosure depends less on whether it is mandatory or voluntary per se, and more on whether it is embedded within a broader institutional framework that assigns tangible costs to the gap between stated commitments and actual performance. The separately estimated indirect effects through GI and the alleviation of financing constraints sum to about 20% of the total effect, while the direct effect continues to be dominant. Since the two channels were assessed in independent models rather than jointly, these shares are approximate, but the dominance of the direct effect is what one would expect on theoretical grounds. The direct effect captures the immediate association between institutional pressure and managers’ decisions, so the relatively modest share of the indirect effects is reasonable. Regulations can create coercive pressures that directly shape the company’s environmentally oriented conduct without having to pass through any intermediary factors [ 1 ]. However, corporate actions to address climate risks do not necessarily follow a consistent path from awareness to innovation to performance. Climate risks can lead to a decoupling of green innovations from ESG performance [ 20 ], and it remains unclear whether the green innovation and financing constraint channels captured here represent long-term organizational transformation or shorter-term responses to external expectations. Organizations motivated to maintain legitimacy may make changes to their energy management processes, such as developing energy management systems, setting internal goals for energy savings, and altering purchasing standards. These behavioral changes do not rely on any intermediate process involving green patents or improvements in the financing environment. In other words, the institutional pressure resulting from the disclosure process can completely bypass these two intermediary steps found in this research and influence energy efficiency through other decision-making mechanisms. This also means that the transmission mechanisms captured here are partial rather than exhaustive. This research has several limitations owing to factors relating to both data issues and the methodology. The coverage of data regarding carbon emissions is much less in the beginning part of the time span (2012 to 2016). The companies retained in the data set might be those that are proactive in disclosing information related to the environment. The survivorship effect can lead to an upward bias in the measure of the estimated association, whereas the instrumental variable technique helps to deal with the endogeneity problem associated with CRD but cannot handle sample selection bias. The dependent variable, carbon emission efficiency, serves as a proxy for energy efficiency. This approximation is strongest in fossil-fuel-intensive sectors where carbon output and energy input move closely together. For firms in low-emission service sectors, the link between the two concepts is weaker, and the results for such firms should not be read as direct evidence of energy efficiency gains. A separate data concern is that companies may have varying degrees of coverage for Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions, and there is no evidence that the two databases completely align in these scopes when datasets are integrated. This might lead to estimation errors biased towards zero. Furthermore, the TF-IDF-based disclosure index captures the quantity and intensity of climate-related language rather than the quality or credibility of disclosure content. Firms may use climate-related terminology extensively without committing to substantive operational changes, and the current measure cannot distinguish between genuine risk management communication and superficial or boilerplate disclosure. The data set spans from 2012 to 2024, during which the context of climate reporting in China underwent a significant change, moving from a completely voluntary setting to a semi-mandatory one. The baseline regression assumes that the relationship between the independent variable and dependent variable stays unchanged throughout this time period. Although the time heterogeneity analysis provides some evidence that the relationship strengthened after 2021, the model does not fully capture how evolving disclosure norms and incentives may have altered the nature of the relationship over the sample period. In terms of the methodology, the dictionary method depends on the manual selection of initial keywords. Although the selection process starts from the word list generated from dictionaries found in the literature and is followed by several rounds of screening, the problem of insufficient vocabulary coverage and ambiguity cannot be completely ruled out. Pre-trained language models can learn contextual and semantic relations that are not possible through dictionary approaches [ 25 ]; such models could also help distinguish between substantive climate disclosure and superficial greenwashing language, an important limitation that this study does not address. Instruments with greater sensitivity to Chinese semantics still require further refinement. The development of measurement techniques can lead to significant advances in analysis [ 3 ]. This becomes especially relevant in disciplines where there is ambiguity surrounding conceptual demarcations and variability in language use. 6 Conclusions The policy implications of this study center on the design logic of climate information disclosure regimes. The insignificant disclosure effect in non-pilot areas implies that the mere act of information disclosure alone cannot bring about meaningful change in firms’ energy-management practices. Therefore, mandatory disclosures need to be supported by carbon markets, environmental enforcement, and other complementary policies. The expansion of the national carbon trading scheme would provide further institutional incentives for companies to put their commitment to disclosure into practice through energy conservation actions. At the regulatory level, the unique structure of the disclosure rule is such that encouraging companies to discuss transition risk issues would be more effective than requiring physical risk information. More detailed reporting requirements relating to transition risks, such as requiring that firms disclose the precise effect of carbon costs on their cost structure or provide efficiency targets together with an update on meeting those targets, would increase the value of the disclosures made. The heterogeneity findings also suggest that differentiated approaches may be warranted. State-owned enterprises and firms in heavy-polluting industries, which already show stronger responses to disclosure, could be subject to more granular mandatory reporting requirements. For firms in non-pilot regions, complementary policy support such as environmental subsidies or low-carbon credit incentives may help offset the absence of carbon market mechanisms. At the firm level, the approach firms take to assessing climate risks should not be solely about compliance. Incorporating the risks discovered during the disclosure process into energy management decisions and translating them into practical retrofitting solutions and upgrades is what makes the disclosure process operationally valuable. These conclusions should be interpreted in light of the study’s limitations, particularly the limited ability of the TF-IDF measure to distinguish substantive disclosure from superficial climate-related language, and the evolving nature of China’s disclosure regime over the sample period. Future research can extend this study in several directions. Applying pre-trained language models to Chinese annual report texts may help improve the semantic accuracy of the measure for climate risk disclosures and distinguish substantive disclosure from greenwashing language. These models would help address the limitations of dictionary-based methods. The relationship between climate risk disclosure and its spillover effects on the energy efficiency of upstream and downstream firms is an unexplored issue, yet one that is relevant from a policy perspective. A third direction involves comparing the effectiveness of mandatory versus voluntary climate disclosure regimes across different institutional contexts, which would help clarify the conditions under which disclosure translates into real operational change.
