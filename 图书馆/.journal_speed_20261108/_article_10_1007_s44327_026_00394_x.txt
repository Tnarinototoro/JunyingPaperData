This article examines stakeholder participation and decision-making in the implementation of BRT in Dar es Salaam. Using a qualitative case study design, it draws on policy and project documents, stakeholder mapping, repeated field observations across three corridors and 84 semi-structured interviews with vendors, paratransit workers, commuters and BRT officials. The findings reveal exclusionary stakeholderism, in which formal recognition and consultation coexisted with unequal influence over decisions. Corridor improvements reduced congestion and created mobility and livelihood opportunities for some stakeholders, while others experienced displacement, restricted access to passenger flows, insecure operating spaces and uneven enforcement. These outcomes arose from interactions among BRT-specific design choices, implementation practices and wider urban governance conditions. Inclusive transport governance therefore requires ongoing, consequential participation rather than information provision alone. Urban Policy Urban Politics Public Policy Postcolonial STS Religion and Sustainability Socio-Economic Policy Informal Transport Systems in Urban Development 1 Introduction Dar es Salaam’s public transport system has long been shaped by rapid urbanisation and policy changes that expanded (and normalised) informality as the central feature of everyday mobility. Following economic liberalisation and transport-sector deregulation in the 1980s, privately operated daladala minibuses became the backbone of everyday urban mobility, filling the institutional gap created by the decline of state-led UDA bus services. However, daladala operations developed within a weakly regulated environment characterised by persistent concerns regarding safety, congestion, service quality, and labour conditions [ 26 ]; 2014; [ 25 ]. These challenges intensified as the city’s population increased from approximately 1.4 million in 1988 to more than 5.7 million in 2022, considerably outpacing the expansion of transport infrastructure and increasing congestion and commuting burdens [ 15 , 17 ]. At the same time, the city’s largely informal spatial development, including the growth of informal settlements and organically formed transport nodes, markets and roadside trading spaces, strengthened the relationship between mobility corridors and informal livelihoods. Introduced in 2016, the Dar es Salaam Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) system was presented as a modern and efficient response to the city’s long-standing mobility challenges. Its development involved a blended financing and governance arrangement comprising international development financiers and Tanzanian state institutions [ 32 , 33 , 34 , 11 , 1 ]. Consistent with broader representation of BRT as a mechanism for transport modernisation, the system introduced dedicated bus lanes, controlled stations, modified stopping arrangements, route restructuring, and large interchange facilities [ 4 ]. These interventions have altered not only transport operations but also the spatial and regulatory environments surrounding major mobility corridors. They therefore raise important questions about who participates in transport planning, whose interests shape design and implementation decisions, and how the resulting benefits and burdens are distributed among formally and informally positioned stakeholders. Although BRT systems are commonly evaluated in relation to efficiency, travel, time, passenger capacity, and environmental performance, their governance and distributive implications require greater attention [ 9 ]. It remains insufficiently understood how stakeholder recognition, representation, engagement, and decision-making power interact with spatial design regulation and implementation practices to shape mobility and livelihood outcomes. This issue is of importance to cities such as Dar es Salaam, where informal vendors, micro-transport operations and commuters depend on transport corridors not only for movement but also for employment, customer access, and economic survival. Formal consultations may recognise these groups as stakeholders without necessarily giving them meaningful influence over decisions that affect their livelihoods and mobility. This article introduces the concept of exclusionary stakeholderism to examine this tension. Exclusionary stakeholderism denotes governance arrangements in which affected groups may be formally identified, informed, or consulted but remain weakly represented in consequential decision-making and are unevenly protected from the costs of implementation. The article contributes to transport-governance scholarship by linking three dimensions that are often examined separately: the distribution of stakeholder representation and decision rights; the spatial and regulatory mechanisms through which BRT is implemented; and the resulting distribution of mobility opportunities, livelihood effects, conflict, and exclusion. It contributes to debates on inclusive and sustainable urban transport and the broader objective of developing accessible and socially equitable cities. Accordingly, the article is guided by three research questions. First, how are decision-making power, participation, and representation distributed among state agencies, development partners, transport operators, informal workers and vendors, and commuters throughout the financing, design, and implementation of the BRT? Second, how do the BRT’s spatial design, regulatory arrangements, and implementation practices reshape access to livelihood spaces and mobility opportunities for informal actors and low-income users? Third, how do existing forms of stakeholder engagement contribute to or mitigate conflict, resistance, and exclusionary outcomes, and what do these patterns imply for meaningful stakeholderism beyond formal consultation within the wider urban-governance context? This concern is especially salient given evidence that transport interventions lacking stakeholder agreement often face resistance and operational underperformance, which can potentially weaken their long-term sustainability [ 16 ]. 2 BRT Governance, Stakeholderism, and exclusion BRT systems are frequently promoted as instruments of urban mobility transformation, particularly in rapidly growing cities facing congestion, unreliable public transport services and rising demand for mobility. Their introduction is often justified in terms of improved efficiency, reduced travel times, enhanced passenger capacity and more sustainable urban mobility [ 4 ]. However, BRT systems are not merely technical transport interventions; they are also governance projects involving decisions about financing, infrastructure design, route allocation, land use, regulation, enforcement, and the distribution of public space. These decisions affect state institutions, development partners, formal and informal transport operators, commuters, vendors, residents, and other groups whose activities are connected to transport corridors. Debates on BRT governance in cities of Low-and-Middle-Income Countries (LMIC) frequently present “stakeholder engagement” as an inherently positive feature of transport planning. In practice, however, engagement may be limited to consultation without influence, information provision without feedback, or symbolic inclusion without institutionalised voice. Formal actors, including state agencies, private operators, and international financiers, generally possess clearer mandates and more continuous access to decision-making processes than informal transport workers, vendors and low-income commuters. The central analytical question is therefore not simply whether engagement occurred, but who was recognised, who was represented, who possessed decision-making authority and through which institutional channels participation could influence project outcomes ([ 21 ] a, [ 22 ] b). In this article, we treated stakeholderism as more than a normative call for “inclusion.” It is used as a governance framework for examining how participation is structured or denied, how stakeholder voice is translated into authority and how accountability operates across multi-actor transport systems. Stakeholderism draws attention to the difference between inclusive language and institutionalised influence. It also provides a basis for examining how design and implementation decisions distribute mobility benefits, livelihood opportunities, regulatory burdens and adjustment costs among differently positioned groups. 2.1 Stakeholder recognition, representation and decision rights Stakeholder recognition concerns the identification of individuals, groups and institutions that affect, or are affected by, a policy, programme, or project [ 3 ]. In BRT governance, recognised stakeholders may include central and local government agencies, international development financiers, regulatory authorities, formal and informal transport operators, commuters, vendors, property owners and communities situated along transport corridors. Recognition is important because groups that are not formally identified may be omitted from consultation, relocation planning, compensation arrangements, mitigation measures or grievance procedures. Recognition alone, however, does not guarantee substantive inclusion. Representation concerns the institutional channels through which stakeholder interests and experiences enter governance processes. Some stakeholders participate directly through legal mandates, contractual responsibilities, financial authority, or technical expertise. Others rely upon associations, committees, public meetings, elected representatives or intermediaries to communicate their concerns. These differences create substantial inequalities in the quality and continuity of representations. State agencies and development partners may have sustained access to planning and financing forums, whereas informal workers and low-income users may be invited only to occasional consultations or encounter decisions principally during implementation. Evidence from Greater Accra illustrates how weak representation can become institutionalised through hierarchical, one-way engagement. Debrah et al., [ 7 ] found that officials primarily transmitted information to transport operators without establishing meaningful feedback channels, contributing to stakeholder apathy and service-related difficulties. This example is relevant to BRT governance because it shifts attention from whether stakeholders attended a meeting to whether they possessed a structured pathway through which their view could shape decisions. Decision rights refer to the extent to which stakeholders can influence, approve, modify, or contest decisions. Participation may occur without any corresponding redistribution of decision-making authority. Stakeholders may be informed of proposed changes, invited to meetings, or asked to comment after routes, stations, access rules, or regulatory arrangements have already been determined. In such circumstances, participation may satisfy procedural requirements without materially influencing project design or implementation. Stakeholderism operates as a governance diagnostic. It examines how voice is converted into authority and whether accountability mechanisms enable affected groups to seek explanations, challenge decisions or secure mitigation. Broader debates on heterodox stakeholderism similarly emphasise that stakeholder protections are shaped by institutional context, regulatory capacity and inequality, particularly in Global South settings [ 13 , 19 ]. These debates are useful for analysing urban transport reforms because they direct attention to the institutional conditions under which externalities are internalised or displaced onto groups with limited political and economic power. 2.2 Engagement quality and implementation mechanisms A central analytical distinction in this article is between public information and dialogic engagement. Public information involves one-way communication through announcements, public notices, updates, or sensitisation campaigns, where the primary purpose is to transmit information. Dialogic engagement, by contrast, creates opportunities for stakeholders to respond, contest proposals and potentially influence decisions [ 14 ]. Manetti et al., [ 14 ] distinguish stakeholder engagement from the narrower practices of stakeholder management. Stakeholder management is principally concerned with identifying and responding to stakeholder expectations or claims, whereas engagement involves interaction, dialogue, shared responsibility, and the involvement of primary stakeholders in decision-making processes. Dialogic engagement may take different forms. A deliberative approach seeks agreement through fair and reasoned communication, whereas an agonistic approach recognises that differences among stakeholders may persist and that legitimate conflict should not necessarily be eliminated [ 14 ]. This distinction is important for BRT governance because disagreement over corridor restructuring, relocation, station access or livelihood effects should not automatically be treated as disruption or resistance to modernisation. Conflict may instead indicate that affected interests were not adequately represented or institutionally incorporated into the decision-making process [ 14 ]; [ 16 ]. The distinction between information and dialogue also clarifies how participatory tools should be evaluated. The existence of public meetings, communication platforms or consultation exercises does not by itself demonstrate meaningful engagement. Engagement quality depends upon when participation occurs, who can participate, what information is made available, whether stakeholders can provide feedback and whether decision-makers explain how that feedback has affected the outcome. Communication may otherwise function primarily as a means of legitimising predetermined decisions [ 14 ]. Engagement quality can be assessed by whether participation establishes a feedback loop capable of influencing BRT design and implementation. This extends beyond consultation to examine whether stakeholder concerns shape consequential decisions. Such decisions include station-area design, access rules, route integration, relocation, compensation and livelihood restoration. They also encompass enforcement practices and grievance procedures (Manetti et al., 2017; [ 7 ]). Engagement is therefore closely connected to the mechanisms through which implementation occurs. Limited representation or influence over decision-making may shift priorities towards efficiency and regulatory compliance. This can reduce attention to affordability, livelihood continuity and access to passenger flows. However, adverse outcomes cannot be attributed solely to inadequate stakeholder engagement. The quality of engagement influences whether potential impacts are identified, negotiated and mitigated during implementation. 2.3 Spatial restructuring and distributive outcomes BRT infrastructure reorganised urban space by separating transport functions, controlling passenger movement, and redefining access to roads, stations and surrounding commercial areas. While these interventions can improve efficiency, reliability, and safety, they also reshape how different groups interact with transport corridors and public space [ 4 ]. Such spatial restructuring is therefore not neutral; it alters existing patterns of mobility and economic activities, particularly for informal workers and low-income users whose livelihoods depend on proximity to passenger flows [ 12 ]. These changes have clear distributive implications. Informal vendors, transport operators and commuters experience BRT interventions differently depending on their access to infrastructure, regulatory protection, and decision-making processes. For example, modifications to station design, pedestrian circulation or route integration may enhance system performance while simultaneously limiting access to customers or increasing travel burdens for certain groups. As a result, transport reforms may generate aggregate benefits while concentrating costs among stakeholders with weaker institutional representation [ 21 ]a; [ 22 ]b; [ 16 ]. The distributive outcomes of BRT governance must also be understood within broader institutional and urban contexts. Pre-existing conditions such as informal economic regulation, uneven enforcement, and limited state capacity shape how project impacts are experienced and managed [ 2 ]. As Pargendler [ 19 ] suggests, where inequality and regulatory constraints are significant, governance arrangements may fail to internalise externalities, shifting burdens onto less powerful stakeholders. Consequently, exclusionary outcomes emerge not only from BRT design itself but from its interaction with wider governance structures and stakeholder engagement processes. 2.4 Analytical framework This article uses stakeholderism as an analytical framework to examine how governance arrangements shape inclusion and exclusion in BRT corridors. The framework begins from the premise that identifying affected stakeholders does not necessarily ensure that their interests, resources, and influence are incorporated into decision-making [ 3 ]. It argues that inequality in stakeholder recognition, representation, and decision rights shapes engagement quality, determining whether participation is limited to one-way information or enables meaningful feedback and influence (Manetti et al., 2017; [ 7 ]). These governance dynamics influence implementation practices such as station design, route restructuring, access regulation, relocation, mitigation, and enforcement. Exclusionary outcomes are therefore understood not as automatic consequences of BRT infrastructure, but as products of interaction between project decisions and wider governance conditions. 3 Methods We adopted a qualitative research design to examine stakeholder dynamics, how participation was structured, and how inclusion and exclusion were produced at the interface of the BRT system. Given the multi-actor nature of BRT governance, we employed a multi-method qualitative strategy. The approach was informed by comparable studies of BRT reforms and their socio-political effects in LMIC settings [ 10 , 20 , 30 ]. In line with research emphasising community engagement in understanding the social impacts of urban transport projects, the design prioritised informal actors and everyday users whose livelihoods and mobility were directly exposed to corridor restructuring [ 27 , 31 ]. 3.1 Data sources and study locations a. Documentary review Data were drawn from three main sources: policy and project documents ([ 24 , 8 , 5 a, 6 b, 23 ] TANROADS, 2024), semi-structured interviews, and repeated field observations. Policy and project documents were selected to link formal BRT rationales and governance structures with lived realities at the mobility–livelihood interface. Following qualitative approaches commonly used to study the socio-political dynamics of BRT reforms in LMICs [ 10 , 20 , 30 ], we reviewed reports principally from 2017 to 2024. They helped to examine how stakeholder roles, consultation processes and implementation responsibilities were formally defined. Earlier documents, including materials published in 2014, were included to capture necessary historical and institutional context for the development of the BRT system. Semi-Structured Interviews For semi-structured interviews, interviews were done with stakeholders occupying diverse positions in the BRT system–including informal transport operators, vendors, and commuters – to reflect on and challenge official narratives. Field observations were conducted between 2022 and 2024 in the three corridors – Ubungo Interchange to Kibamba CCM (Morogoro Road); Mbagala Rangi 3 to Gerezani (Kilwa Road); and Tazara to Gongo la Mboto (Nyerere Road). A total of 84 participants were interviewed: 55 informal vendors, 17 informal transport operators comprising bodaboda riders and daladala operators, 9 commuters who used BRT services, and 3 BRT officials. Participants were recruited through a combination of purposive and snowball sampling. Purposive sampling was used to identify participants with direct experience of BRT planning, implementation, operation, use, or livelihood effects. Snowball sampling helped to identify additional participants with relevant experience who were not readily accessible through formal recruitment channels. The sample was intended to provide diversity of experience and institutional position rather than statistical representation. Semi-structured interviews lasted between 12 and 22 min and were conducted in Kiswahili. Interviews addressed experiences of consultation, access to decision-making, changes in mobility and livelihoods, station-area access, relocation, enforcement, and perceptions of how BRT benefits and costs were distributed. With participants’ consent, interviews were audio-recorded. We also maintained field notes and matters requiring further analytical attention. We translated the interview materials into English. As we are native Kiswahili speakers and regularly use English in our academic and professional work, we were able to interpret culturally specific expressions and contextual meanings directly. The sample size was determined by stakeholder coverage rather than by statistical representativeness. Recruitment continued until all stakeholder categories identified as relevant to the research questions had been included across the study locations. The final sample of 84 therefore represents the total number of participants interviewed and comprised all four principal participant categories. The sample was considered sufficient once these groups were represented and the study had captured variations in institutional position, dependence on livelihood, mobility experience and exposure to BRT-related changes. Rather than seeking statistical representation of the population affected by BRT outcomes, the article aimed to provide a more diverse qualitative snapshot of stakeholders’ experiences and governance relationships. This was appropriate because BRT systems in Dar es Salaam remain in the early stages of implementation, with further phases underway. c. Field observations Repeated non-participant field observations were undertaken between 2022 and 2024 along the three corridors. These locations were selected because they contained important transport nodes, substantial pedestrian movement, informal commercial activity, and differing levels of exposure to BRT-related spatial and operational change. A total of 6 observation visits were conducted across the three corridors. Each visit lasted for 10 h, with observations undertaken in the morning (7:00 to 10:00), afternoon (12:30 to 15:30) and evening (16:30 to 18:30), all on weekdays (Mondays, Thursdays, and Fridays). Conducting observations across different locations and periods in the city enables us to compare corridor conditions, commuter activities, trading practices and enforcement patterns. Observational records were subsequently coded and compared with documentary evidence and interview accounts during thematic analysis. 3.2 Coding and thematic analysis The interview recordings, translated materials, field notes, observational records and documentary evidence were analysed thematically, broadly following Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-phase approach. Analysis proceeded recursively through familiarisation, coding, theme development, review, definition and reporting. Coding combined deductive codes derived from the research questions and analytical framework, including stakeholder recognition, representation, engagement quality, decision rights, spatial restructuring and distributive outcomes. Inductive codes emerged from participants’ accounts and observations and included relocation uncertainty, reduced access to passengers, loss of trading space, informal payments, inconsistent enforcement and livelihood adaptation. Codes were compared across stakeholder groups, study locations and data sources before being organised into broader themes. Themes were selected for their analytical relevance to the research questions rather than their numerical frequency alone. 3.3 Stakeholder mapping To empirically examine stakeholderism and power relations, we constructed a stakeholder map by triangulating data from documents, mandates and formal responsibilities, interviews concerning perceived influence and access to decision-making, and observations concerning spatial control and enforcement. This map structured our analysis of representation and decision rights, distinguishing cases where “engagement” functioned more as one-way information provision than genuine participation [ 7 , 14 , 16 , 19 ]. Where contextual meaning or competing interpretations required clarification, the original materials were revisited, and interpretative subjectivity was managed by comparing evidence across participant groups, study locations and data sources rather than relying on isolated quotations or observations. Field and analytical notes helped distinguish participants’ accounts, direct observations and subsequent interpretations. Contradictory evidence was retained and examined in relation to stakeholders’ institutional positions, interests, corridor conditions, implementation stages and levels of exposure to BRT-related changes. Differences between documentary commitments, interview accounts and observed practices were treated as analytically significant and explored as possible evidence of implementation, accountability or communication gaps. 4 Results 4.1 Patterns of stakeholder participation and decision influence Documentary evidence indicated that consultations, disclosure, and grievance procedures were formally incorporated into BRT planning and resettlement arrangements. The Phase I Resettlement Policy Framework provided for consultation, information disclosure and a grievance-redress mechanism, presenting displacement and compensation as processes that should be managed through agreed procedures rather than unilateral instruction (PMO-RALG and Dar es Salaam City Council, 2007). Later resettlement planning similarly committed implementing institutions to engaging project-affected persons and communities, involving stakeholders in relocation and compensation planning, establishing grievance mechanisms and publicly disclosing relevant information (TANROADS, 2024). Interview evidence nevertheless showed that informal stakeholders frequently experienced engagement as the communication of decisions rather than as an opportunity to influence them. Vendors described receiving information about relocation or access restrictions without clear explanations of where they would move, how alternative spaces would be allocated or whether their concerns could alter implementation decisions. One vendor stated: “They keep saying we must move , but we don’t know where to go.” (Vendor 55, Mbagala Rangi Tatu, 2023). This account reflects uncertainty not only about relocation but also about the institutional channels through which affected vendors could seek clarification or influence the proposed arrangements. Although formal documents referred to consultation and grievance procedures, participants did not consistently describe continuing feedback processes through which their concerns were acknowledged and incorporated into decisions. Uncertainty was also evident in the allocation of replacement trading spaces. Some vendors considered newly constructed markets possible alternatives but reported that access to these spaces was unclear or unequal. One participant explained: “I planned to move to the newly built market , but people said all the spaces were taken even before the construction was finished. Wealthier individuals occupied them first.” (Vendor 37, Mbagala Rangi Tatu, 2023). This account suggests that exclusion may occur not only through removal from established trading locations but also through limited access to mitigation measures intended to address displacement. Vendors reported little involvement in determining the location, affordability or allocation of alternative commercial spaces, leaving them uncertain about whether proposed remedies would accommodate their livelihood needs. Similar uncertainty was evident among informal transport operators whose activities depended on access to passengers around BRT facilities. A bodaboda operator described using a location near pedestrian movements that had not been formally designated as a motorcycle stand: “This is not the actual motorcycle park; it is just a place to look for customers. Many passengers cross the pedestrian area , heading to the market.” (Informal transport operator (Bodaboda) 7, Kimara Temboni, 2023). The quotation shows that the operator’s access to station-generated passenger flows depended on an informal spatial arrangement. It does not establish whether BRT authorities had consulted the operator, but it demonstrates that the operator’s position within the station-area transport system lacked formal recognition and a designated operating space. A bajaji operator Footnote 1 similarly described uncertainty about the future location of informal feeder services once BRT operations became fully established: ““This is not an official Bajaj stop; we are using it temporarily until DART begins operations. The designated stop is for daladala , so we avoid competing there. Once DART starts , we will decide where to move.” (Informal transport operator (Bajaji) 3, Zakhiem area, 2023). This account indicates that the operating arrangement was provisional and that the operator expected to be relocated without knowing what alternative space would be available. It also highlights differences among transport modes: daladala services had a designated operating area, whereas the bajaji operator used an unofficial site and expected to negotiate or adapt once BRT operations began. As with the boda boda account, the evidence indicates uncertainty about integration and operating space rather than proving that consultation did not occur. Stakeholders differed markedly in their access to decision-making and implementation. State institutions, development partners and formal operators participated through statutory mandates, financing responsibilities and operational roles. By contrast, informal vendors and transport operators encountered BRT decisions through relocation instructions, provisional arrangements, public information or changes on the ground. Recognition did not guarantee influence over corridor design, operating locations, market-space allocation, relocation or livelihood mitigation. The findings distinguish formal consultation from consequential participation: although documents outlined engagement and grievance procedures, interviews revealed uncertainty about whether stakeholders’ concerns influenced decisions, indicating unequal representation and influence. 4.2 Corridor design, access, and livelihood changes Interview and observational evidence showed that BRT-related road improvements, station development and changes in passenger circulation produced differentiated mobility and livelihood effects across the study corridors. Some participants associated the upgraded infrastructure with reduced congestion and improved movement, whereas others described displacement, restricted access to passengers and increased operating costs. These differences depended on stakeholders’ locations, livelihood activities, transport modes and ability to access station-generated passenger flows. Informal transport operators identified some operational benefits. A daladala driver reported that the improved infrastructure had reduced congestion while emphasising the continuing role of daladala services for passengers unable to afford regular BRT travel: “The world-class infrastructure … has reduced traffic jams , but we serve passengers who can’t afford the rapid bus fares.” (Informal transport operator (Daladala driver) 3, Kimara Mwisho, 2023). The account suggests that BRT and daladala services operated partly as complementary rather than wholly substitutive modes. A passenger similarly anticipated that further corridor development would improve urban travel: “I think the construction of the Mwendokasi will simplify travel in the city , especially the extension to the Mbezi–Kibamba area.” (Passenger 6, Kibo bus station, 2023). Infrastructure development nevertheless altered established trading locations and access to customers. Field observations conducted between 2022 and 2024 documented vendors operating along pedestrian lanes, road edges and other high-footfall areas near stations and interchanges. These locations provided customer access but also exposed traders to congestion, insecurity and possible enforcement. One vendor explained: “I was in front of a shop , but when the road was expanded , I had to move. Now , I operate in a pedestrian space because it’s where customers are.” (Vendor 19, Kimara Butcher, 2023). Relocation also created additional costs. A participant at Mbagala Rangi Tatu reported: “I was chased away. Now I must rent a space for 30 , 000 TZS per month. But customers are here , so I must stay close.” (Vendor 23, Mbagala Rangi Tatu, 2023). These accounts indicate that displacement did not necessarily remove vendors from transport corridors. Instead, traders moved to less secure or more expensive spaces that preserved access to customers. Station design also affected direct interaction with passengers. A vendor operating near a median-lane station stated: “We cannot communicate with passengers who are waiting to board or those who are off-boarding.” (Vendor 11, Mbagala Rangi Tatu, 2022). Livelihood access therefore depended not only on passenger numbers but also on whether station configuration and access rules allowed interaction between passengers and surrounding commercial activities. The effects were not uniformly adverse. In some locations, BRT stations and pedestrian facilities generated new concentrations of potential customers. A bodaboda operator explained: “Most of our customers are from the BRT … there are more BRT passengers compared to daladala passengers.” (Informal transport operator (Bodaboda) 7, Kimara Mwisho, 2023). The operator nevertheless used an unofficial location near pedestrian movements rather than a designated motorcycle stand. This enabled access to customers but left the activity spatially insecure. Vendors also benefited from BRT-generated passenger flows. A fruit vendor reported that many customers were passengers disembarking from BRT buses, while a second-hand clothes vendor described benefiting from pedestrian movement across an overpass: “After the BRT pedestrian overpass bridge was constructed , I seized the opportunity. I cater to passers-by here.” (Second-hand clothes vendor 27, Magufuli Terminal interchange, 2023). These accounts demonstrate that BRT infrastructure could create livelihood opportunities by concentrating passenger and pedestrian movements. However, the capacity to benefit depended on proximity to stations, exits, crossings and interchange facilities. Vendors separated from these movements by station design or access restrictions were less able to capture the resulting commercial opportunities. The commercial occupation of pedestrian infrastructure also created tension between mobility and livelihood needs. One vendor acknowledged that trading obstructed part of the pedestrian route but presented such occupation as necessary for survival: “We know that we have blocked it , but we provide services nearby … they are all looking for survival.” (Second-hand clothes vendor 42, Zakhiem area, 2023). Vendors recognised the conflict between commercial activities and pedestrian movement. However, the use of pedestrian space represented an adaptation to limited access to secure and commercially viable trading locations. The evidence documents obstruction and commercial use of pedestrian facilities but does not establish pedestrians’ own experiences of safety, accessibility or convenience. Documentary materials formally recognised livelihood disruption as a potential consequence of BRT implementation. The Phase I Resettlement Policy Framework stated that affected persons should not be left worse off following displacement, while subsequent resettlement plans committed implementing institutions to minimising livelihood impacts and supporting restoration where adverse effects could not be avoided (PMO-RALG, 2014; TANROADS, 2024). However, interview and observational evidence revealed gaps between these commitments and some participants’ experiences of relocation, customer loss, restricted access to passengers and higher operating costs. Observations at Ubungo Interchange also indicated that faster, more channelled traffic flows reduced prolonged stopping and informal interaction in some established commercial areas. Although these changes supported the corridor’s movement function, they weakened the conditions on which some informal businesses depended. Corridor development produced uneven mobility and livelihood outcomes. It reduced congestion and generated new customers for some operators and vendors, while road expansion, median-station design and access regulations displaced others or separated them from passenger flows. Benefits and burdens therefore varied according to location, affordability, livelihood dependence and access to commercially valuable passenger flows. 4.3 Enforcement, uneven impacts and stakeholder responses Documentary evidence showed that responsibility for implementing and regulating BRT-related changes was distributed across several institutions. DART oversaw aspects of system operation, TANROADS was responsible for infrastructure development and supervision, and municipal and local authorities managed public space, traffic control and enforcement. Resettlement planning also established grievance structures at district, ward and street levels, indicating that disputes over compensation, relocation and access were anticipated within the formal governance arrangements (TANROADS, 2024). Interview and observational evidence nevertheless showed that regulations were applied unevenly across stakeholder groups, locations and stages of implementation. Informal transport operators frequently used spaces near BRT stations that were not officially designated for their activities. A bodaboda operator explained: This is not the actual motorcycle park; it is just a place to look for customers. (Informal transport operator (Bodaboda) 41, Kimara Korogwe, 2023). The operator’s access to passengers therefore depended on informal use of station-adjacent space. A bajaji operator similarly described temporary tolerance by traffic officers where no alternative operating area was available: “Until DART starts running , we will keep using this area. Even the traffic officers do not bother us because they know there is nowhere else , we can park our Bajaji.” (Informal transport operator [Bajaji] 38, Kimara Mwisho, 2023). This tolerance allowed operators to continue serving passengers but did not provide secure operating rights. The respondent expected the arrangement to change once BRT services became fully operational, illustrating how informal access could be accepted temporarily while remaining institutionally uncertain. Vendors reported a more punitive form of enforcement involving confiscation, temporary withdrawal and informal negotiation. One participant stated: “Sometimes city rangers take our goods , but if we give them some money , we can get them back. If we see them coming , we just run away and return when they leave.” (Vendor 41, Mbagala Rangi Tatu, 2023). This account shows how vendors adapted through avoidance, temporary relocation and informal payments. Their continued presence reflected the economic value of access to passenger and pedestrian flows, but their position remained insecure because they lacked predictable permission to operate and reliable procedures for contesting confiscation. Field observations showed that informal actors adjusted their activities in response to enforcement. Vendors moved their goods, returned after officers had left or relocated to nearby high-footfall areas, while bodaboda and bajaji operators continued to use unofficial pick-up points. These practices sustained livelihoods but also perpetuated uncertainty and repeated negotiation over access to transport space. Uncertainty was particularly pronounced in corridors where BRT operations were incomplete. Participants anticipated further relocation or stricter enforcement but reported limited information about timing or alternatives. One vendor explained: “They moved us before , and I lost many customers. If they do it again , I don’t know where I’ll go.” (Vendor 14, Mbagala Rangi Tatu, 2023). The burden of implementation therefore varied according to activity, location, resources and capacity to negotiate with authorities. Some informal operators benefited from temporary tolerance and access to BRT-generated passenger flows, whereas vendors in other locations faced confiscation, repeated displacement and interruption of commercial activity. Official documents framed BRT implementation around system performance, regulatory compliance, participation, compensation and livelihood restoration (PMO-RALG, 2014; World Bank, 2015; TANROADS, 2024). However, some informal actors experienced it mainly through operating restrictions, relocation warnings and uncertain access to mitigation. Although insecure access to public space and discretionary enforcement pre-dated the project, corridor restructuring and station regulation created new settings in which these practices operated. Implementation therefore produced uneven effects, including temporary tolerance, punitive enforcement, informal negotiation and continued uncertainty over access to commercially valuable transport spaces. 5 Discussion 5.1 Rethinking stakeholder engagement in practice The findings show that formal recognition of stakeholders did not consistently translate into sustained representation or influence over consequential BRT decisions. Although project and resettlement documents included commitments on consultation, disclosure, grievance procedures and livelihood mitigation, vendors and informal transport operators often encountered decisions through relocation notices, operating restrictions and changing spatial arrangements. The central problem was therefore not the complete absence of engagement, but the weak connection between participation and decision-making authority. This gap was evident in uncertainty over relocation, allocation of market spaces and future operating locations. Vendors reported limited clarity about where they would move, while bodaboda and bajaji operators relied on unofficial spaces near BRT facilities without clear integration arrangements. These findings support the distinction between one-way communication and engagement that enables stakeholders to respond and influence outcomes (Manetti et al., 2017). Information may be disclosed, and consultations may occur, yet participation remains limited where affected groups cannot shape mitigation, relocation, route-integration or station-access decisions. The Dar es Salaam case also resembles the hierarchical engagement identified in Greater Accra, where information was transmitted without effective feedback channels [ 7 ]. However, the present findings extend this concern by showing that limited influence had spatial and distributive consequences, affecting access to commercial spaces, passenger flows and operating locations. The evidence therefore reveals a hierarchy of influence: state agencies, development partners and formal operators participated through institutional mandates and implementation roles, while vendors, informal operators and everyday users were more commonly engaged through consultation or local implementation processes. Exclusionary stakeholderism is thus best understood as a relative governance condition in which those most exposed to implementation effects may have the least capacity to modify the decisions producing them. 5.2 Infrastructure, space and the uneven distribution of mobility and livelihood opportunities The findings show that BRT-related spatial restructuring produced mixed outcomes. Road improvements reduced congestion and were associated with easier urban travel, while daladala services continued to meet the needs of passengers who could not fully rely on BRT because of affordability or trip requirements [ 29 ]. This suggests that BRT and informal transport operated partly as complementary elements within the wider mobility system rather than as wholly separate or substitutive modes [ 18 ]. BRT stations, pedestrian crossings and interchange facilities also created new concentrations of passengers from which some informal actors benefited. Bodaboda operators gained customers near stations, while vendors located close to stops and pedestrian overpasses reported increased commercial opportunities. However, access to these benefits was uneven and insecure. Some operators worked from unofficial locations, while other traders were displaced, separated from passengers by median-lane stations or required to rent alternative premises. These patterns show that the distributive effects of BRT cannot be assessed only through travel speed, capacity or system efficiency. Infrastructure design re-organises access to passenger flows, commercial spaces and feeder services. Those able to remain close to stations, exits and transfer points may benefit, whereas those displaced from commercially valuable locations may experience customer loss, higher costs and greater exposure to regulation. The Dar es Salaam findings therefore illustrate how transport externalities are experienced through everyday changes in access to space, customers and mobility opportunities [ 19 ]. The occupation of pedestrian lanes further demonstrates the tension between transport functionality and livelihood survival. Vendors recognised that their activities obstructed movement but continued to use these spaces because secure and commercially viable alternatives were limited. The issue is therefore not whether BRT was uniformly beneficial or harmful, but how design and regulatory arrangements distributed opportunities and burdens among differently positioned stakeholders [ 28 ]. Where livelihood dependence remains unresolved, enforcement alone is unlikely to produce sustainable management of transport space. 5.3 Implementation, enforcement and everyday adaptation The findings show that BRT-related space was governed through fragmented institutional responsibilities and uneven enforcement. Although formal mandates and grievance procedures existed, affected stakeholders experienced regulation through a mixture of temporary tolerance, restriction, confiscation and informal negotiation. Bajaji operators were sometimes allowed to use unofficial spaces where no alternatives were available, while vendors reported more coercive encounters, including confiscation and informal payments. Enforcement therefore varied by activity, location, implementation stage and official discretion. These practices shaped everyday adaptation. Informal actors moved temporarily, returned after officers had left, negotiated access or occupied alternative high-footfall spaces. Such responses were not simply resistance to regulation; they reflected uncertainty, limited livelihood alternatives and weak access to formal decision-making. Unresolved conflicts over station access, relocation and operating-space allocation were therefore displaced into everyday implementation, revealing a gap between formal commitments and local practice. The findings support Moslem et al.,’s [ 16 ] argument that weak stakeholder agreement can complicate transport implementation, but they do not show that resistance undermined the BRT system as a whole. Rather, they indicate that unresolved concerns generated recurring negotiation, non-compliance and enforcement around stations and corridors. Implementation should therefore be understood as an ongoing governance process, requiring continued participation, clearly designated operating areas and accessible mitigation procedures after infrastructure decisions have been made. 5.4 Scope, limits and pathways for meaningful participation Exclusionary stakeholderism is supported by the findings as a governance configuration in which affected groups may be recognised, informed or consulted but remain weakly represented in consequential decision-making. The concept captures the coexistence of formal participation with unequal influence, uncertain mitigation and uneven exposure to implementation costs. It links limited conversion of stakeholder voice into decision authority with spatial and regulatory arrangements that shape access to transport space, livelihood opportunities and mobility benefits. Exclusionary stakeholderism was not experienced uniformly. Some vendors and informal transport operators benefited from BRT-generated passenger flows, while others faced displacement, restricted access or enforcement. Outcomes varied by corridor, stakeholder activity, proximity to stations and implementation stage. Moreover, the BRT did not create contested governance of informal activity in Dar es Salaam. Rather, corridor expansion, median-lane stations, pedestrian facilities and station-area regulation interacted with pre-existing conditions of insecure tenure, fragmented authority and discretionary enforcement, reconfiguring and sometimes intensifying established conflicts over public space and livelihoods. Meaningful stakeholderism should not be equated with complete consensus or direct participation by every individual in every decision. A more realistic benchmark is whether affected groups have transparent, continuing and representative channels through which they can raise concerns, receive responses, contest decisions and influence mitigation and implementation. This requires moving beyond sensitisation towards documented feedback procedures, representation for informal actors, transparent allocation of relocation spaces, integration of feeder transport and commercial activities into station planning, credible grievance mechanisms and clearly assigned institutional responsibilities. Such measures would not eliminate disagreement but could shift conflict from discretionary street-level encounters into more predictable governance processes. The qualitative case-study design limits the scope of the conclusions. The study identifies governance mechanisms and explains how stakeholders experienced BRT-related change, but it cannot estimate the prevalence or population-level magnitude of mobility and livelihood effects. Findings may also vary across corridors, implementation phases and levels of operational maturity. Future research could combine longitudinal qualitative analysis with corridor-level measures of affordability, accessibility, travel burdens, livelihood displacement and stakeholder participation. Exclusionary stakeholderism is therefore presented as an analytical explanation of how engagement quality, decision influence and implementation practices can generate uneven outcomes, not as a claim that all stakeholders or locations experienced the BRT in the same way. 6 Conclusion This article examined how stakeholder participation, decision-making authority and BRT implementation shaped mobility and livelihood outcomes in Dar es Salaam. Drawing on documents, interviews, field observations and stakeholder mapping across three corridors, it used stakeholderism to assess whether recognition and consultation led to representation, feedback and influence over key decisions ([ 7 ]; Manetti et al., 2017; [ 16 , 19 ]). The findings reveal exclusionary stakeholderism: formal engagement coexisted with unequal influence, particularly for informal vendors and transport operators, who often experienced BRT-related changes through relocation notices, operating restrictions and enforcement. Although corridor improvements reduced congestion and created opportunities for some stakeholders, others faced displacement, separation from passenger flows, higher costs and insecure access to transport spaces. BRT impacts therefore depended on how design, access rules and implementation practices distributed benefits and burdens. The article defines exclusionary stakeholderism as the recognition of affected groups without corresponding influence over decisions or adequate protection from implementation costs. In Dar es Salaam, this pattern reflected the interaction of BRT implementation with existing informality, fragmented institutional responsibilities and discretionary enforcement. More meaningful participation requires ongoing feedback, representative channels for informal actors, designated operating and trading spaces, transparent relocation procedures and accessible grievance systems. These measures could reduce reliance on informal negotiation, repeated displacement and non-compliance [ 16 ]. Although the qualitative case study limits statistical generalisability, the findings show that inclusive transport governance requires affected groups to have credible opportunities to influence decisions affecting their mobility and livelihoods.
