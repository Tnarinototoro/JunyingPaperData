ABSTRACT
Farmers in the French Alps increasingly rely on Livestock Guardian Dogs (LGDs) to protect herds in mountain pastures and valleys from a growing wolf population. 
However, rising sport and recreational activities challenge the coexistence between LGDs and outdoor users. Since 2019, pastoral services in four French departments 
have conducted surveys – collecting quantitative and qualitative data – to understand recreationists encounters with LGDs.
This research paper examines which factors influence the perception of encounters between recreationists and LGDs. Based on 1587 questionnaires, perceived LGD 
threats and emotional experiences during and after encounters significantly influenced users’ perceptions, both positively and negatively. Awareness of LGDs played 
a modest mediating role, slightly shifting perceptions towards a good stance, underscoring the importance of awareness campaigns.
Personal experiences also influenced perceptions; first-time encounters and professional visits led respondents to describe encounters as “neutral” rather than 
“bad”, while older users described them as “neutral” rather than “good”. Gender and age also played a role: women reported a higher number of “good” encounters. 
Surprisingly, respondents crossing flocks described encounters as “good” rather than “neutral”, challenging recommendations against flock crossing and highlighting 
adaptation needs.
A key tension lies in contrasting views of mountain areas as non-excludable common land versus excludable resources. Recreationists often see these landscapes as 
open-access, yet flocks and LGDs introduce barriers, creating exclusion. This reflects three themes: legitimacy (privatisation perceptions), responsibility (account-
ability conflicts), and socio-ecological issues within herding, recreation, and predator management systems.
Management implications
● Strengthen awareness campaigns led by pastoral services, local 
authorities, tourist offices, environmental educators, media-
tors, and eco-guards, as implemented in several French mu-
nicipalities and protected areas, in order to reduce the pressure 
on livestock farmers and shepherds.
● Ensure that these awareness campaigns take into account the 
diversity of pastoral practitioners as well as the variety of 
contexts in which encounters take place.
● Promote mutual understanding and dialogue between pastoral 
actors and recreationists.
1. Introduction
Over the past few decades, the recovery of large carnivores (LCs) 
such as wolves (Canis lupus) across Europe has sparked renewed debates 
about the coexistence of humans and wildlife (Chapron et al., 2014; 
Pooley et al., 2017). While supportive conservation policies have facil-
itated wolf recolonisation in regions like the French Alps, this expansion 
has reactivated and intensified social conflicts, particularly in areas 
characterised by extensive pastoral systems (Dorresteijn et al., 2015; 
Redpath et al., 2015). Pastoralism – encompassing sheep, goat and cattle 
grazing – plays a pivotal role in maintaining High Nature Value (HNV) 
farmland, sustaining biodiversity, and preserving cultural landscapes 
(Beilin et al., 2014; Plieninger et al., 2016). However, the same land-
scapes that benefit from grazing practices also harbour concerns related 
to livestock depredation and economic vulnerability tied to the presence 
of predators (Eklund et al., 2017). Consequently, livestock protection 
measures – most notably Livestock Guardian Dogs (LGDs) – have gained 
traction across Europe and beyond as a means to reduce depredation and 
support coexistence (Gehring et al., 2010; Landry et al., 2020).

However, these interventions can introduce new social frictions. 
LGDs, typically large and assertive dogs bred or trained to protect herds, 
not only confront wolves but also interact with people crossing the same 
landscapes. LGDs generally enjoy more public support than lethal con-
trol measures (Mosley et al., 2020) and have been shown to reduce 
depredation in various contexts (Andelt, 1992; Gehring et al., 2010; 
Urbigkit & Urbigkit, 2010; VerCauteren et al., 2011). However, in 
mountainous regions such as the French Alps – where tourism and rec-
reation are economically significant – encounters between LGDs and 
hikers, mountain bikers and other recreationists can create novel forms 
of conflict (Landry, 1999; Lescureux & Linnell, 2014). While many 
perceive LGDs as a critical strategy for limiting wolf attacks on livestock, 
some visitors experience fear or irritation when confronted by protective 
dogs. Fear of dogs is quite common in the general population, and being 
female, having previously been threatened by a dog and being older are 
all strong predictors of dog-related fear (Boyd et al., 2004). The emotion 
of “fear” can be felt in the body and in relationships with others and 
spaces (Tuan, 2013). Fear during encounters can lead to negative atti-
tudes towards pastoral activities and carnivore conservation (Young 
et al., 2015). Existing research suggests that maintaining spatial buffers 
between livestock and recreational areas can help reduce such incidents 
(Mosley et al., 2020). However, those recommendations often originate 
from large-scale grazing contexts (e.g. in the western United States) and 
may not translate easily to more spatially constrained European settings. 
In a context where recreationists are increasing in Europe (Melo et al., 
2020), understanding these social dimensions is vital because successful 
LC management depends not only on technical solutions (e.g., dog 
training, fencing) but also on public acceptance, perceptions of fairness, 
and the willingness of different user groups – such as farmers and rec-
reationists – to share a landscape with wildlife (Pooley et al., 2017). A 
study conducted by Ribert et al. (2024) shows that despite reduced 
territories and attempts to share mountain spaces, a fragmentation of 
these areas occurs. More or less fixed and restrictive boundaries (vege-
tative cover, fences, modifications to trails, etc.) are established and 
used by shepherds to separate their movements from highly frequented 
areas (Ribert et al., 2024). The conclusions of this research indicate that 
recreationists and pastoralism struggle to coexist since the reintro-
duction of livestock protection dogs in the mountains.
In France, there are currently around 5000 LGDs, reflecting their 
official status as one of three major herd protection measures – alongside 
the presence of a shepherd and nighttime enclosures – mandated for 
eligibility for government compensation (DREAL, 2020). The relation-
ship between shepherds and flocks has now expanded to encompass 
living with wolves and LGDs, as well as navigating increased in-
teractions with recreationists. This complexity is heightened by the fact 
that, in the event of an LGD attack on recreationists, legal responsibility 
may fall on dog owners; following an evaluation, the owner may be 
asked to euthanise their dog and pay a fine (Mounet, 2007). However, it 
is often unclear whether the LGD's behaviour or the behaviour of rec-
reationists – or both – drives these encounters. Likewise, it remains 
difficult to ascertain what determines whether a recreational user per-
ceives the encounter as good or bad, be it the dog's behaviour or indi-
vidual factors such as prior experience with dogs and fearfulness.
In this context, various organisations representing pastoral interests 
collaborated on a survey to assess interactions between recreationists 
and LGDs in the French Alps. We analysed the data from this survey 
using a mixed-methods approach (quantitative and qualitative) to 
identify the factors that influence the quality of encounters between 
LGDs and recreationists, including socio-demographic variables, 
knowledge about LGDs and pastoralism in general, LGD behaviour, and 
shepherds’ behaviour.
This research paper examines which factors influence the perception 
of encounters between recreationists and LGDs. this study makes theo-
retical contributions to people-nature studies and offers practical guid-
ance for encouraging meaningful coexistence between pastoralists, local 
communities, recreationists and large carnivores.
2. Study area and methods
“My Experience with Livestock Guardian Dogs” was a quantita-
tive and qualitative survey carried out between 2019 and 2021 by the 
pastoral service team of four French Alpine departments followed by 
collaborative work with researchers in social sciences.
2.1. Instrument
The survey instrument (Supplementary material A) was divided into 
six sections to examine various facets of people's encounters with Live-
stock Guardian Dogs (LGDs): 1. the encounter; 2. the person's reaction, 
including their emotional and behavioural reaction; 3. the LGD's 
behaviour towards the people encountered; 4. the context of the 
encounter, including the weather, the presence or absence of accom-
panying persons, and the availability of information on the presence or 
absence of LGDs; 5. information about the respondents, including socio- 
demographic data, their use of the mountains, and their level of comfort 
with LGDs; 6. the consequences of the encounter, such as changes in the 
person's subsequent behaviour, residual emotions, repercussions on 
their perception of LGDs, etc.
Two open-ended questions – “Can you describe the encounter?” and 
“Can you tell us more about the encounter?” – allowed respondents to 
add detail and nuance, although 4.3% of participants chose not to 
answer them, and some only answered one of them.
2.2. Data collection and sample
The questionnaire was distributed by the pastoral services in four 
departments of the Auvergne-Rhˆone-Alpes region (Savoie, Haute- 
Savoie, Is`ere and Drˆome) between 2019 and 2021. It was available in 
paper version (notably in tourist offices) and in digital form accessible 
via a QR code. The pastoral services, as well as tourism professionals and 
public stakeholders, published the survey via social networks and the 
media. In the field, nature educators approached all hikers they 
encountered to invite them to complete the survey, although this 
method remained limited due to insufficient human resources. Since this 
was a non-interventional study and participants’ anonymity was pro-
tected, no ethical approval was required under national regulations. 
However, consent was obtained from all participants before the survey 
was administered, and the data were anonymised such that no in-
dividuals could be identified. In total, 1603 questionnaires were 
returned, and 16 surveys (1.2%) were excluded, as they described en-
counters occurring when LGDs were indoors rather than in a typical 
outdoor grazing context. The final dataset therefore included 1587 
participants.
2.3. Data analysis
a. Multinomial logistic regression
A multinomial logistic regression model was used with R software to 
determine the factors that influenced the way individuals perceived 
their encounters with LGDs. The dependent variable in this model was 
the respondent's evaluation of the encounter, divided into three cate-
gories: “good”, “neutral” (neither good nor bad in the questionnaire) and 
“bad”.

made according to certain criteria associated with specific questions (see 
supplementary material B entitled “Transformation of variables”).
