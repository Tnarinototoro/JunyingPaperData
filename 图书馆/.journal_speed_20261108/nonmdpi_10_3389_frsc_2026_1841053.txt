Manchester Institute of Innovation Research, Alliance Manchester Business School, The University of Manchester, Manchester, United Kingdom Article metrics View details Abstract Inroduction: Electric vehicle (EV) diffusion depends not only on whether consumers recognize individual economic, performance, and environmental attributes, but also on how those evaluations are translated into an overall attitude and ultimately purchase intention. Drawing on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), this study examines a bounded belief-attitude-intention mechanism in Bahrain by integrating three economic evaluations (price, perceived economic benefits, and policy incentives), three functional-performance evaluations (driving range, acceleration, and charging time sensitivity), and environmental values. Methods: A cross-sectional survey produced 366 valid responses, which were analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) with 10,000 bootstrap resamples. Results: Policy incentives, acceleration, environmental values and consumer attitudes showed positive and significant direct relationships with EV purchase intention, whereas perceived economic benefits operated primarily through attitude. The mediation pattern was selective rather than universal, as attitudes provided indirect-only mediation for economic benefits, complementary partial mediation for policy incentives, acceleration, charging time sensitivity and environmental values, but not significant mediation for price or driving range. Additional robustness analyses materially clarified three apparently contradictory results. Price was negatively associated with intention in the simple model but became small and positive only after simultaneous adjustment for correlated determinants. Driving range was positively associated with intention at the simple level but lost unique significance in the full model. Charging time sensitivity was interpreted according to its reverse-coded measurement, distinguishing weaker endorsement of faster charging as an adoption inducement from any preference for longer charging durations. The significance pattern remained stable across alternative composite-score estimations, inclusive and restrictive measurement specifications, a non-owner EV subsample, demographic controls, and an independent bootstrap mediation analysis. Discussion: The study contributes by showing that attitude is a determinant-specific transmission mechanism rather than a universal mediator and by demonstrating how integrated EV adoption models can distinguish total associations from unique multivariate effects in an emerging Bahraini market. Practically, the findings highlight the importance for policy support, performance communication, charging convenience, and environmental value in encouraging EV purchase intentions in Bahrain. 1 Introduction Decarbonizing road transport has become a central component of climate and urban sustainability policy because transport remains a major source of greenhouse-gas emissions and local air pollution. The transport sector accounts for approximately 23% of global energy-related carbon dioxide emissions, with road transport responsible for roughly 70% of the sector’s emissions (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), 2023). At the vehicle level, a typical gasoline-powered passenger vehicle in the United States emits about 4.6 metric tons of carbon dioxide per year, while ambient air pollution is associated with an estimated 4.2 million premature deaths annually worldwide (Environmental Protection Agency, 2025a, 2025b; World Health Organization, 2024). These environmental and public-health pressures have intensified policy efforts to shift road mobility away from internal-combustion-engine vehicles (ICEVs) toward lower-emission alternatives. The Paris Agreement established a global framework for limiting temperature increases, while the European Union has legislated a pathway toward zero carbon dioxide emissions from new cars and vans by 2035 (European Parliament, 2022, 2023; United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, 2026). Battery electric vehicles (EVs) are central to this transition because they eliminate direct tailpipe emissions and can reduce life-cycle emissions when electricity systems progressively decarbonize (Bastida-Molina et al., 2020; Hawkins et al., 2012; Wei et al., 2023). Their environmental advantage is nevertheless conditional rather than automatic, as battery production, resource use, electricity-generation mix, and vehicle use all influence life-cycle outcomes (Dolganova et al., 2020; Hawkins et al., 2012). Even so, technological progress has substantially improved vehicle range, charging capability, and model availability, while declining battery costs and expanding policy support have contributed to rapid market growth (Forsythe et al., 2023; International Energy Agency, 2025; Qadir et al., 2024). Global EV sales reached 16.6 million units in 2024, an increase of around 22% from 2023, yet EVs still represented less than 4% of the global vehicle fleet, indicating that diffusion remains uneven despite rapid annual growth (International Energy Agency, 2025; Ritchie, 2024). The unevenness of adoption is particularly relevant in the Gulf, where fuel-pricing structures, urban geography, infrastructure development, and policy environments differ from many mature EV markets. Bahrain provides a theoretically informative setting because several contextual conditions may alter the relative importance of established EV-adoption determinants. Subsidized gasoline can weaken the perceived operating-cost advantage of electrification, the country’s compact geography may reduce the incremental importance of very long driving ranges for routine travel, and the developing charging network can increase the importance of charging convenience and policy support (Alsabbagh and Alnaser, 2022; Shareeda et al., 2021). Bahrain has also committed to carbon neutrality by 2060 and has pursued renewable-energy, efficiency, and EV-infrastructure initiatives as part of its wider sustainability agenda (Bahrain News Agency, 2023; Bahrain SDGs 2030, 2026). Recent operations-research evidence focused specifically on Manama further shows that charging station location, accessibility, and queueing have become practical urban mobility questions within the country (Albinali et al., 2026). Bahrain is therefore examined not because geographical specificity alone constitutes theoretical novelty, but because this combination of economic, spatial, infrastructural, and policy conditions provides a useful context in which to test whether established EV adoption determinants operate through the same attitudinal mechanisms observed or assumed elsewhere. Consumer adoption research shows that no single variable explains the transition to EVs. High acquisition prices can deter purchase, but total cost of ownership, policy incentives, charging access, range, performance, environmental values, social influence, perceived usefulness, trust, and risk can also shape intention (Adnan et al., 2016; Bryła et al., 2023; Coffman et al., 2017; Pamidimukkala et al., 2024; Rezvani et al., 2015). Recent studies increasingly combine multiple antecedents through the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), value-based adoption perspectives, and other integrated frameworks (Fu, 2024; Goel et al., 2023; Gupta et al., 2024; Kottala et al., 2025; Nazir and Tian, 2022; Rahma et al., 2024; Shalu and Pandey, 2025). The theoretical challenge is therefore no longer to claim that EV adoption has multiple determinants. Rather, it is to explain how distinct classes of determinants operate, whether they retain direct influence when considered simultaneously, and whether their effects are transmitted through the same psychological mechanism. Consumer attitude is a particularly important mechanism because it represents an overall evaluation of the target behavior and is a proximal antecedent of behavioral intention in the TPB (Ajzen, 1991). EV studies repeatedly find that favorable attitudes predict stronger adoption intention and may mediate the influence of economic, technological, environmental, or policy-related beliefs (Ali and Naushad, 2022; Chaturvedi et al., 2022; Khurana et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021). Yet, the empirical role of attitudes is unlikely to be uniform, as some antecedents may require attitudinal internalization before they affect intention, while others may influence intention both directly and indirectly, and some that may have little incremental effect once correlated evaluations are considered together (Dau et al., 2025; Rehman, 2025; Zhao et al., 2010). Treating attitude as a universal mediator can obscure meaningful differences between economic trade-offs, functional vehicle attributes, policy support, and environmental values. Against this background, this study examines whether attitudes function as a selective transmission mechanism across seven EV-related factors in Bahrain. The model organizes price, perceived economic benefits, and policy incentives as economic evaluations; driving range, acceleration and charging time sensitivity as functional-performance evaluations; and environmental values as a value-based antecedent. These variables were selected because they capture salient ownership sacrifices, benefits, policy signals, functional capabilities, and personal values that recur throughout EV adoption research and are directly relevant to an emerging Gulf market (Bansal et al., 2021; Degirmenci and Breitner, 2017; Shareeda et al., 2021; Thananusak et al., 2017). The study does not attempt to reproduce every construct used in TPB, TAM, UTAUT, or value-based models, but instead isolates a theoretically bound belief-attitude-intention mechanism and tests whether heterogeneous EV evaluations converge on attitude in the same way. This study makes three main contributions. First, it reframes integration as a question of mechanism rather than variable accumulation by testing whether attitudes provide indirect-only, partial, absent, or otherwise determinant-specific mediation across heterogeneous EV evaluations. Second, it disaggregates functional performance into range, acceleration, and charging time sensitivity rather than treating vehicle performance as a single undifferentiated construct, allowing their unique and overlapping effects to be compared. Third, it subjects the primary PLS-SEM findings to a broad set of diagnostic and robustness assessments, including nested coefficient models, alternative composite-score estimation, inclusive and restrictive measurement specifications, non-owner and demographic-controlled models, an independent 5,000 resample mediation bootstrap, a common-method diagnostic, and HTMT inference. Accordingly, the study’s contribution does not rest on the claim that combining multiple EV adoption determinants or studying Bahrain is itself unprecedented. Rather, Bahrain is treated as a theoretically informative context in which the study examines whether heterogeneous economic, functional-performance, and environmental evaluations are transmitted through attitude in determinant-specific ways, while the robustness analyses distinguish lower-order associations from unique conditional effects. 2 Literature review and hypothesis development 2.1 TPB-informed belief-attitude-intention framework The TPB provides the principal theoretical foundation for this study because it explains behavioral intention as the product of evaluative, normative, and control-related processes, with attitudes representing the individual’s favorable or unfavorable evaluation of performing the behavior (Ajzen, 1991). In TPB, salient beliefs about likely outcomes contribute to attitude formation, and attitude in turn influences intention. This belief-attitude-intention logic has been widely applied to sustainable consumption and EV adoption, where consumers evaluate economic sacrifices, expected benefits, functional characteristics, environmental consequences, and policy conditions before forming an overall orientation toward adoption (Adnan et al., 2016; Khurana et al., 2019; Kristiono et al., 2023; Moons and De Pelsmacker, 2015; Rahma et al., 2024; Thwe et al., 2025). Recent belief-elicitation work using TPB also reinforces the importance of identifying the specific beliefs consumers associate with EV adoption rather than assuming that a generic attitude emerges independently of concrete product and policy evaluations (Thwe et al., 2025). The wider technology adoption literature provides complementary perspectives for understanding the different dimensions of consumer adoption decisions. TAM emphasizes perceived usefulness and ease of use, UTAUT highlights performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions, while diffusion of innovation (DOI) theory focuses on innovation characteristics and the process through which innovations spread (Rogers, 1962; Venkatesh et al., 2003, 2012). Prior EV studies have productively extended these models with environmental concern, risk, trust, policy support, price value, and infrastructure readiness (Fu, 2024; Nazir and Tian, 2022; Noor et al., 2025; Shalu and Pandey, 2025). For example, TAM-based evidence shows that usefulness and ease of use may influence purchase intention directly and through attitudes, while UTAUT-based work highlights social and facilitating conditions (Udiyana and Yasa, 2025; Venkatesh et al., 2003, 2012). Taken together, these perspectives demonstrate that EV adoption is shaped by a combination of product evaluations, contextual conditions, and psychological responses. Within this broader literature, the present study concentrates specifically on the evaluative mechanism through which consumers assess salient economic, functional-performance, and environmental attributes and translate those assessments into an overall attitude toward EV adoption. The selection of constructs reflects this emphasis on decision-relevant evaluations. Economic considerations are represented through price, perceived economic benefits, and policy incentives; functional-performance considerations are captured through driving range, acceleration, and charging time sensitivity; and environmental values represent the value-based dimension of the adoption decision. Together, these constructs capture three substantively distinct classes of considerations that feature prominently in EV adoption research and that are especially relevant to consumer decision-making in emerging markets where affordability, product practicality, infrastructure development, and sustainability considerations coexist (Adnan et al., 2016; Bryła et al., 2023; Coffman et al., 2017; Pamidimukkala et al., 2024). Perceived usefulness, perceived risk, trust, social influence, facilitating conditions and environmental concerns represent other important pathways identified in the broader adoption literature. Perceived usefulness and ease of use are central to TAM-based EV studies, while perceived risk and trust help capture uncertainty and credibility, social influence and facilitating conditions represent normative and control-related mechanisms, and environmental concern is treated as a prominent ecological factor (Acar and Taşkın, 2025; Kottala et al., 2025; Nazir and Tian, 2022; Udiyana and Yasa, 2025). These constructs therefore complement rather than duplicate the evaluative pathway examined in this study. Environmental values are included because they capture a relatively enduring orientation toward environmental responsibility that can shape evaluations of environmentally beneficial products, whereas environmental concern is generally more issue-specific and may operate through distinct cognitive or behavioral pathways (Barbarossa et al., 2017; Bouscasse et al., 2018; Rehman, 2025; White and Sintov, 2017). Accordingly, the framework applies TPB to the evaluative pathway linking salient EV-related beliefs and values with attitude and purchase intention. Economic, functional-performance, and environmental factors are modeled as determinants of both attitude and purchase intention, allowing their effects to be expressed directly, indirectly through attitude, or through both pathways. This specification is consistent with contemporary mediation theory, which recognizes that indirect effects may exist in the presence or absence of significant direct effects, and that different factors may operate through distinct combinations of mediated and unmediated mechanisms (Zhao et al., 2010). Within the proposed framework, attitudes therefore represent the central evaluative mechanism through which consumers integrate multiple considerations surrounding EV adoption, while the simultaneous direct paths capture the possibility that some attributes may also influence purchase intention beyond their contribution to overall attitude. 2.2 Economic factors 2.2.1 Price Upfront purchase price is among the most persistent barriers identified in EV adoption research because consumers compare the immediate acquisition sacrifice of an EV with familiar ICEV alternatives. Early studies found that high initial cost and uncertain economic value reduced adoption interest, while continuing to show that price sensitivity remains important even as battery prices decline and vehicle choice expands (Egbue and Long, 2012; Larson et al., 2014; Rezvani et al., 2015). Survey evidence from India and Thailand shows that consumers trade price against range, charging speed, operating savings, and other attributes, while recent integrated models continue to identify price value, maintenance cost, and affordability as meaningful components of EV purchase intention (Bansal et al., 2021; Hasan et al., 2024; Kant et al., 2024; Lashari et al., 2021; Noor et al., 2025; Thananusak et al., 2017). Research on U.S. households likewise indicates that adoption intention cannot be reduced to cost alone, but affordability remains a material part of the purchase decision (Kim et al., 2025). The influence of price is conditional on the wider value proposition. Consumers may accept a higher acquisition price when they expect lower operating costs, stronger performance, environmental benefits, or policy support, and conversely, cheap gasoline can reduce the economic attractiveness of switching (Degirmenci and Breitner, 2017; Li et al., 2017; Rehman, 2025). In Bahrain, subsidized fuel and an immature EV market can weaken the importance of long-run operating savings while leaving the purchase premium highly visible (Shareeda et al., 2021). The direct expectation is therefore that stronger perceptions of EV price burden reduce purchase intention. Thus, it is hypothesized: H1: Higher perceived EV price negatively influences consumers’ intention to purchase EVs in Bahrain. Price can also influence intention indirectly through the consumers’ overall evaluation of EV adoption. In TPB, expected financial sacrifice is a significant outcome belief that can contribute to a favorable or unfavorable attitude (Ajzen, 1991). EV research shows that price perceptions often interact with perceived value, environmental concern, and government support, and that attitudes can transmit part of the effect of economic evaluations to intention (Acar and Taşkın, 2025; Ali and Naushad, 2022; Higueras-Castillo et al., 2019). When price is interpreted as an unacceptable sacrifice, it can weaken attitude. Therefore, when consumers judge the wider bundle of economic, technological, and environmental benefits as worthwhile, the attitudinal penalty may be reduced. Thus, it is hypothesized: H1A: The relationship between perceived EV price and purchase intention is mediated by consumer attitudes. 2.2.2 Perceived economic benefits Perceived economic benefits capture the expected financial advantage of EV ownership after purchase, including lower fuel or energy expenditure, reduced maintenance requirements, and total cost of ownership savings. This construct is conceptually distinct from upfront price, as a consumer can simultaneously perceive EV as expensive to buy, but economical to operate. Comparative total-cost studies from the U.S., U.K., Japan, and Europe show that lower energy and maintenance costs can offset part of the initial price premium over the ownership period, although the timing and magnitude of the advantage depends on the vehicle segment, mileage, energy prices, taxation, and depreciation (Liu et al., 2021; Palmer et al., 2018; Soszynska et al., 2024). Consumer studies similarly find that expected operating savings and perceived value strengthen EV attractiveness (Bansal et al., 2021; Gupta et al., 2024; Higueras-Castillo et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2025; Rehman et al., 2026). However, the strength of this relationship is context dependent. In markets where gasoline is expensive and electricity is relatively cheap, running cost savings can be highly visible, while in subsidized fuel environments, the comparative advantage may be less immediate (Li et al., 2017; Shareeda et al., 2021). Bahrain’s compact geography may reduce absolute annual driving expenditure for many consumers, while fuel subsidies can narrow perceived savings from electrification (Alsabbagh and Alnaser, 2022). Nevertheless, lower maintenance complexity and long-run operating economies can still provide a positive value signal, particularly when consumers evaluate ownership costs rather than purchase price alone. Thus, it is hypothesized: H2: Perceived economic benefits positively influence consumers’ intention to purchase EVs in Bahrain. Economic benefits may be especially likely to operate through attitude because savings are often not experienced at the moment of purchase. Research on EVs and other sustainable technologies show that perceived value and expected financial benefits can improve attitudes, which then strengthen intention (Ali and Naushad, 2022; Goel et al., 2023; Khurana et al., 2019; Kottala et al., 2025). Value-attitude-behavior evidence likewise suggests that utilitarian and economic value can be internalized into a favorable evaluation before becoming purchase intention (Dau et al., 2025; Fu, 2024). Accordingly, the economic benefit pathway is expected to include an attitudinal mechanism. Thus, it is hypothesized: H2A: The positive relationship between perceived economic benefits and purchase intention is mediated by consumer attitudes. 2.2.3 Policy incentives Policy incentives reduce adoption barriers by altering the private cost and convenience of EV ownership. Financial measures include purchase subsidies, tax credits, registration fee exemptions, and preferential financing, while non-financial measures include parking privileges, toll exemptions, lane access, and charging support (Anilan and Vij, 2024; Hardman, 2019; Martins et al., 2024; Qadir et al., 2024). Evidence from the U.S., Norway, Europe, and China generally shows that well-designed incentives can accelerate adoption, although effectiveness varies with market maturity, policy mix, consumer awareness, and the persistence of non-financial barriers (Jenn et al., 2018; Langbroek et al., 2016; Martins et al., 2024; Mersky et al., 2016; Sierzchula et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2024). The former U.S. Clean Vehicle Credit, which provided qualifying buyers with up to $7,500 purchase-side support before its termination in 2025, illustrates how purchase subsidies were used to reduce the initial acquisition burden, while European and Chinese evidence increasingly emphasizes coordinated policy packages rather than isolated subsidies (Alternative Fuels Data Center, 2026; Anilan and Vij, 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Incentives also carry informational and symbolic effects. Consumers may interpret policy support as evidence that EV technology is socially desirable, institutionally supported, and likely to receive future infrastructure investment. Empirical studies show that policy perceptions can influence both purchase intention and the psychological evaluations underlying intention (Bian and Panyagometh, 2023; Wang et al., 2021). In China, psychological value has been shown to transmit part of the effect of incentive policy on purchase intention, while more recent research finds mediated effects through usefulness, ease of use, or perceived value (Chen et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2023). Fleet evidence also demonstrates that the effect of incentives is conditioned by the wider economic environment rather than mechanically constant across time (Alali et al., 2022). Bahrain is at an earlier policy stage than mature EV markets with limited support for EV adoption (Shareeda et al., 2021). National sustainability initiatives, the first EV forum, and announced charging station development indicate increasing institutional attention, but comprehensive purchase incentives remain limited relative to leading markets (Al-Helou, 2022; Bahrain Insurance Association, 2024; Bahrain News Agency, 2023; Bahrain SDGs 2030, 2026). Studies suggest that targeted subsidies, reduced import tariffs, and exemptions from registration fees could help reduce cost disparities between EVs and ICEVs, particularly for Bahrain’s subsidized gasoline market (Alsabbagh and Alnaser, 2022; Shareeda et al., 2021). The direct expectation is therefore that stronger perceived policy support increases purchase intention. Thus, it is hypothesized that: H3: Perceived financial and non-financial policy incentives positively influence consumers’ intention to purchase EVs in Bahrain. Policy incentives can also shape attitudes by reducing perceived sacrifice, improving expected convenience, and signaling institutional commitment. Evidence from China and other markets indicates that policy incentives can operate through perceived value and attitudinal evaluations rather than only through a direct price effect (Bian and Panyagometh, 2023; Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2021; Xue et al., 2023). In Bahrain, the absence of comprehensive packages has reinforced skepticism, contributing to negative consumer attitudes toward EV affordability (Shareeda et al., 2021). Yet, targeted financial measures, such as VAT-free purchases and waived registration fees, combined with non-financial perks like priority parking or dedicated lane access, could significantly improve attitudes in heavy-traffic and limited infrastructure markets like Bahrain (Alsabbagh and Alnaser, 2022). This indirect pathway may be especially important because incentives help consumers interpret a still-unfamiliar technology as more feasible and legitimate. Thus, it is hypothesized that: H3A: The positive relationship between policy incentives and purchase intention is mediated by consumer attitudes. 2.3 Functional-performance factors 2.3.1 Driving range Driving range is a core functional attribute because it determines how far an EV can travel before recharging and is closely related to range anxiety. Limited range was one of the earliest barriers identified in consumer EV research and continues to shape perceptions even as battery capability improves (Egbue and Long, 2012; Graham and Brungard, 2021). Experimental, survey-based and simulation studies show that additional range increases vehicle utility and willingness to pay, although the marginal value of additional kilometers may decline once routine travel needs are comfortably met (Bansal et al., 2021; Hackbarth and Madlener, 2016; Kim et al., 2017; Neubauer and Wood, 2014). Real-world travel data also show that diffusion models could consider actual driving patterns rather than assuming uniform range requirements (Plötz et al., 2014). Range is therefore both a technical capability and a perception problem. Consumers can demand more range than their daily travel requires because occasional long trips, uncertainty about charging, and unfamiliarity with EV operation increase the value of a safety buffer (Carley et al., 2013; Coffman et al., 2017). Technology advancement is gradually reducing this constraint, but range remains intertwined with charging infrastructure and vehicle price (Forsythe et al., 2023). Bahrain’s compact geography suggests that many routine journeys can be served by contemporary EV ranges, yet limited charging familiarity may preserve concern beyond objective daily-distance requirements (Shareeda et al., 2021). Thus, it is hypothesized that: H4: Perceived adequacy of driving range positively influences consumers’ intention to purchase EVs in Bahrain. Adequate range may also improve adoption indirectly by reducing anxiety and strengthening the overall evaluation of EV practicality. Studies of EV attitudes show that perceived usability and functional adequacy contribute to favorable attitudes, while experience can correct exaggerated concerns (Rezvani et al., 2015; Roberson and Helveston, 2020). Because range is evaluated relative to personal travel needs and charging confidence, its effect may be absorbed into a broader attitude rather than remaining entirely direct (Ali and Naushad, 2022; Khurana et al., 2019). Thus, it is hypothesized that: H4A: The positive relationship between perceived driving range adequacy and purchase intention is mediated by consumer attitudes. 2.3.2 Acceleration Acceleration represents a different dimension of performance from range and charging. EV powertrains can deliver instant and rapid response, making acceleration a noticeable experiential advantage rather than merely a technical specification (Bai and Zheng, 2020; Forsythe et al., 2023). Although EV literature has historically concentrated more heavily on price, range, and charging, consumer studies in the U.S., Thailand and Spain show that perceived performance and driving experience can improve adoption evaluations (Higueras-Castillo et al., 2019; Roberson and Helveston, 2020; Thananusak et al., 2017). Recent Gulf-region evidence from Oman also identifies performance-related expectations as relevant to sustainable urban mobility adoption (Al-Maamari et al., 2025). Acceleration may matter particularly because it can challenge the stereotype that environmental technologies require a sacrifice in driving quality. When a consumer experiences or anticipates responsive performance, the EV can be evaluated not only as an environmentally responsible product but also as an attractive automobile. This hedonic-utilitarian combination is consistent with evidence that both functional and experiential value contribute to EV adoption intentions (Fu, 2024; Higueras-Castillo et al., 2019). Thus, it is hypothesized that: H5: Perceived EV acceleration performance positively influences consumers’ intention to purchase EVs in Bahrain. Acceleration can also operate through attitudes because performance experience changes the consumers’ overall evaluation of the technology. Consumers who experienced EV acceleration firsthand are also shown to develop more favorable attitudes toward adoption (Pamidimukkala et al., 2024; Roberson and Helveston, 2020). Cultural preferences for performance attributes may also contribute to generating more positive evaluations of EVs (Shareeda et al., 2021). The mediation hypothesis therefore tests whether acceleration retains a direct appeal while also strengthening purchase intention through a more favorable attitude. Thus, it is hypothesized that: H5A: The positive relationship between acceleration and purchase intention is mediated by consumer attitudes. 2.3.3 Charging time sensitivity Charging time and charging availability shape EV convenience because refueling an ICEV and recharging a battery involve different temporal and infrastructural routines. Long charging duration, uncertainty about access, and limited public fast charging can reduce perceived usability, whereas fast-charging access can improve adoption intentions (Carley et al., 2013; Coffman et al., 2017; He et al., 2022). Consumers are willing to pay for faster charging and improved infrastructure, and the value of charging capability is often interdependent with range and home charging access (Bansal et al., 2021; Coffman et al., 2017; Egbue and Long, 2012; Neubauer and Wood, 2014). Regional evidence similarly identifies infrastructure readiness as a central condition for EV diffusion in the Middle East (Cj et al., 2023). The present construct is interpreted as charging time sensitivity, and whether faster charging and the availability of fast-charging stations would encourage adoption, where higher scores represent lower sensitivity to charging time constraints. This orientation is important because a positive coefficient indicates that consumers who are less constrained by charging time concerns report stronger purchase intentions and not implying that they prefer slower charging. In Bahrain, the relevance of this construct is reinforced by the underdeveloped charging network, announced station deployment, and recent modeling of station location and queueing in Manama (Albinali et al., 2026; Bahrain News Agency, 2023; Shareeda et al., 2021). Thus, it is hypothesized that: H6: Lower sensitivity to charging time constraints positively influences consumers’ intention to purchase EVs in Bahrain. Charging concerns may also influence intention through attitude because convenience and perceived feasibility contribute to an overall evaluation of EV ownership. Research on charger accessibility and adoption shows that infrastructure perceptions affect intention, while mediation studies indicate that functional convenience can be internalized into attitude or perceived value (He et al., 2022; Pamidimukkala et al., 2024; Permana and Wisnalmawati, 2025). Accordingly, lower charging time sensitivity is expected to support a more favorable attitude, and ultimately stronger purchase intentions. Thus, it is hypothesized that: H6A: The positive relationship between charging time sensitivity and purchase intention is mediated by consumer attitudes. 2.4 Environmental factors 2.4.1 Environmental values Environmental values reflect relatively enduring beliefs about the importance of environmental protection and responsible consumption. They differ from a momentary assessment of vehicle usefulness or cost because they provide a value-based lens through which consumers evaluate the environmental consequences of mobility choices (Barbarossa et al., 2017; White and Sintov, 2017). EV research consistently shows that pro-environmental identity, environmental values, and perceived ecological benefits can increase adoption intention, although their influence varies with price, infrastructure, and beliefs about electricity generation and battery impacts (Bouscasse et al., 2018; Degirmenci and Breitner, 2017; Rehman, 2025; Rezvani et al., 2015). Life-cycle concerns are relevant because environmentally motivated consumers may also consider battery production, resource use, and the carbon intensity of electricity (Dolganova et al., 2020; Hawkins et al., 2012). Recent value-based studies strengthen the expectation that environmental orientation contributes to EV purchase intention. Value-attitude-behavior research finds that pro-environmental values can shape attitude before translating into intention, while green self-identity and self-expressive benefits can make EV adoption consistent with personal identity (Barbarossa et al., 2017; Dau et al., 2025; Mustafa et al., 2026; Qian and Yin, 2017). Acar and Taşkın (2025) further show that environmental concern and government incentives can influence perceived value and automobile adoption, while Rehman (2025) finds that environmental values and related ecological factors affect EV purchase intention through attitudinal and normative processes. Bahrain’s national sustainability commitments may increase the significance of such values, but fossil-fuel-based electricity also makes it important to avoid portraying EVs as environmentally impact-free (Alsabbagh and Alnaser, 2022; Bahrain SDGs 2030, 2026). Thus, it is hypothesized that: H7: Environmental values positively influence consumers’ intention to purchase EVs in Bahrain. The TPB-informed framework further predicts that environmental values can shape purchase intention by improving the consumer’s overall evaluation of EV adoption. Evidence from green consumption and EV studies shows that attitudes can mediate the influence of environmental concerns, values, identity, and ecological beliefs on intention (Bouscasse et al., 2018; Dau et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2024; Kristiono et al., 2023; Rehman, 2025). The attitudinal pathway is therefore expected to be particularly important for translating broad environmental values into a product-specific intention. Thus, it is hypothesized that: H7A: The positive relationship between environmental values and purchase intention is mediated by consumer attitudes. 2.5 Mediating role of consumer attitudes Consumer attitudes represent the overall favorable or unfavorable evaluation of purchasing or using an EV. Within TPB, attitude is a proximal determinant of intention, but it is formed from beliefs about expected outcomes rather than existing independently of them (Ajzen, 1991). EV studies consistently identify attitude as one of the strongest predictors of adoption intention and often show that it transmits the effects of technological, policy, and environmental factors (Ali and Naushad, 2022; Chaturvedi et al., 2022; Gupta et al., 2024; Khurana et al., 2019; Kristiono et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021). More recent integrated models continue to demonstrate that attitude links perceptions of value, usefulness, environmental consequences, and policy support to purchase intention (Dau et al., 2025; Rahma et al., 2024; Shalu and Pandey, 2025; Udiyana and Yasa, 2025). The present study does not assume that attitude mediates every factor equally, as mediation theory distinguishes indirect-only mediation from complementary mediation and from relationships in which the indirect effect is absent (Zhao et al., 2010). The key theoretical proposition is therefore selective mediation: attitude is central, but its mediating role is expected to depend on the nature of the factor and the decision context. H8: Consumer attitudes positively influence consumers’ intention to purchase EVs in Bahrain. 2.6 Conceptual model Figure 1 summarizes the proposed model based on the literature review. Economic evaluations (price, perceived economic benefits, policy incentives), functional-performance evaluations (driving range, acceleration, charging time sensitivity), and environmental values are modeled as factors of purchase intention and consumer attitudes, while attitude is modeled as a direct predictor of purchase intention. The resulting model permits simultaneous assessment of direct effects and specific indirect effects through attitude, thereby testing whether attitudinal mechanism is indirect-only, complementary, absent, or otherwise determinant-specific. Figure 1 Conceptual model. 3 Methodology 3.1 Research design A quantitative, cross-sectional survey design was used to examine the hypothesized relationships among latent constructs. Survey methods are widely used in EV adoption research because many relevant constructs, including attitudes, perceived benefits, policy perceptions, environmental values and purchase intentions, are psychological or perceptual variables that cannot be observed directly (Bansal et al., 2021; Higueras-Castillo et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2022). The analytical model includes multiple latent variables, simultaneous direct and indirect paths, and a central mediation mechanism. Partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) was selected as the primary analytical approach to assess the measurement model and estimate the proposed direct and indirect relationships (Hair et al., 2022; Sarstedt et al., 2020). The model was estimated using SmartPLS4, while Stata/BE 17.0 was used for supplementary regression, sensitivity, and diagnostic analyses. 3.2 Data collection and sampling procedure Data were collected using a structured questionnaire containing 32 construct measurement items, alongside demographic and consent questions. The survey targeted adult residents of Bahrain aged 18 years and above. Before accessing the questionnaire items, respondents were presented with an introductory participant-information and consent statement identifying the study as an MBA thesis conducted at the American University of Bahrain and explaining its purpose. The statement informed respondents that participation was entirely voluntary, that no personally identifiable information would be collected, that responses would remain anonymous and confidential, and that participants could withdraw at any point before submitting. Participants were also informed that there were no anticipated risks or direct benefits from participation and that their anonymized responses would be used for academic research and could contribute to future research. Informed consent was then obtained through a mandatory first questionnaire item requiring respondents to affirm that they agreed to participate under the stated conditions. The questionnaire used five-point Likert scales ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Measurement items were adapted from prior EV and sustainable technology studies, as shown in Table 1. The use of established multi-item measures supports content validity and comparability with prior adoption research (Adnan et al., 2016; Bansal et al., 2021; Degirmenci and Breitner, 2017; Egbue and Long, 2012; Gupta et al., 2024; Hackbarth and Madlener, 2016; Higueras-Castillo et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2022; Kristiono et al., 2023; Lashari et al., 2021; Rezvani et al., 2015; Thananusak et al., 2017). The questionnaire assessed respondents’ perceptions of electric vehicles at the general product- and technology-category level rather than anchoring responses to a single EV brand, model, or vehicle segment. The constructs therefore capture respondents’ general evaluations of EV price, economic benefits, policy support, driving range, acceleration, charging time sensitivity, environmental values, attitudes, and purchase intention. This specification is consistent with the study’s objective of examining general EV adoption beliefs rather than preferences for a particular vehicle. A non-probability convenience sampling approach was employed. Convenience sampling is common in behavioral EV research where a complete sampling frame of potential adopters is unavailable, but it does not provide known selection probabilities and therefore cannot support design-based claims of population representativeness (Bryła et al., 2023; Pamidimukkala et al., 2024). For survey planning, Cochran’s (1977) formula produced a conventional benchmark of approximately 384 cases under a 95% confidence interval (CI) and 5% precision assumption. Because the realized sample was non-probabilistic, this calculation is treated only as a planning benchmark rather than as evidence that the final sample has a design-based 5% margin of error. Sample adequacy was evaluated primarily in relation to the complexity of the PLS-SEM model and the stability of mediation estimation, for which the final sample of 366 provides substantial information relative to common minimum recommendations (Hair et al., 2022; Memon et al., 2020). A total of 404 questionnaires were initially recorded. After data cleaning, 38 cases were removed because of missing data or indicators of insufficient-effort responding, including highly invariant response patterns. Procedures for identifying careless responding are important because low-effort answers can distort covariance structures and reliability estimates in self-administered surveys (Huang et al., 2012; Schell et al., 2022). Reverse-coded items were checked carefully because inappropriate use or processing of reversed items can introduce measurement bias (Suárez-Álvarez et al., 2018). The final analytical sample consisted of 366 respondents. As shown in Table 2, 331 respondents (90.44%) had never owned an EV and 35 (9.56%) reported current or previous EV ownership, making the sample primarily one of potential rather than experienced adopters. The PLS-SEM analysis proceeded in two stages. First, the reflective measurement model was evaluated using indicator loadings, Cronbach’s Alpha (), Composite Reliability (), and average variance extracted (AVE). Indicator loadings above 0.70 are generally desirable, while indicators between 0.40 and 0.70 can be retained when their removal does not materially improve construct reliability or convergent validity, and additionally when content remains theoretically meaningful (Hair et al., 2022). Internal consistency estimates were interpreted jointly rather than relying on Cronbach’s alpha alone, with values above 0.70 considered sufficient (Hair et al., 2022). Convergent validity was assessed using AVE, with values of 0.50 or above indicating that a construct explains at least half of the variance in its indicators (Hair et al., 2022). Discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell-Larcker criterion (Fornell and Larcker, 1981), and the Heterotrait-Monottrait ratio (HTMT). HTMT values below 0.85 provide a conservative indication of discriminant validity, while 0.90 is commonly used as a more liberal threshold when constructs are conceptually related (Hair et al., 2022; Henseler et al., 2015). Values exceeding these thresholds were therefore treated as potential discriminant validity concerns rather than being declared acceptable solely on the basis of other criteria. Bootstrap inference was additionally considered. Under the original HTMT-inference formulation, a CI excluding 1.00 provides evidence that two constructs are not empirically indistinguishable (Henseler et al., 2015), whereas stricter threshold-based approaches evaluate whether the upper CI bound remains below a predefined value such as 0.90 (Franke and Sarstedt, 2019). Where these approaches produced different conclusions, the evidence was treated as mixed and interpreted cautiously. Finally, potential common-method bias was considered through both procedural features of the original survey design and post-hoc diagnostic assessment. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, respondents were informed of the research purpose, and established multi-item measures were used to reduce ambiguity and evaluation apprehension. These procedural features can reduce some sources of method-related response bias but cannot eliminate common-method variance in a single-source, cross-sectional survey (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Harman’s single factor diagnostic was therefore used only as a limited post-hoc assessment of whether a single factor dominated the covariance structure and was not interpreted as definitive evidence that common-method bias was absent. Conventional VIF statistics were examined separately as predictor-collinearity diagnostics rather than as proof against common-method bias. Because the original questionnaire did not include a theoretically unrelated marker variable, a marker-variable analysis could not be introduced retrospectively without altering or misrepresenting the original research design. Figure 2 Importance-performance map analysis for EV purchase intention. 5 Discussion 5.1 Economic evaluations The price result is the clearest example of why conditional multivariate coefficients must be distinguished from unconditional consumer preference. A large body of EV research identifies high acquisition cost as a barrier, and the present data are consistent with that literature at the simple-association level where price was negatively related to purchase intention (Bansal et al., 2021; Egbue and Long, 2012; Larson et al., 2014; Noor et al., 2025; Rezvani et al., 2015). The sign became small and positive only after other economic, performance, environmental, and attitudinal variables were controlled. Such reversals can arise when correlated predictors share explanatory variance, producing a suppression or reversal pattern in the conditional coefficient (Tu et al., 2008). The findings should not be interpreted as evidence that Bahraini consumers prefer more expensive EVs or that acquisition price has ceased to be a barrier. The negative association in the simple model is consistent with the established literature on upfront cost, whereas the small positive coefficient in the full model represents a conditional relationship after adjustment for correlated economic, performance, environmental, and attitudinal factors. Price therefore remains a relevant structural constraint, even though its unique association changes once other adoption considerations are included. This distinction also addresses the absence of significant attitudinal mediation for price. Price was not a significant predictor of attitude in the primary mediation model, and its indirect CI included zero. In a market with subsidized gasoline and low prior EV ownership, price may remain an important external sacrifice without being consistently absorbed into a generalized evaluation of EVs once perceived benefits, policy support, performance, environmental values, and attitude itself are modeled simultaneously (Bansal et al., 2021; Hasan et al., 2024; Kant et al., 2024; Lashari et al., 2021; Shareeda et al., 2021). Research increasingly shows that EV intention is “beyond the cost” in the sense that cost remains relevant but competes with infrastructure confidence, environmental benefit, policy expectations, and product attributes (Kim et al., 2025). The present result should be read in that conditional sense, not as evidence that cost ceases to matter. Perceived economic benefits produced a different mechanism. The direct path to intention was insignificant, but the indirect path through attitude was significant. This is consistent with the distinction between immediate acquisition sacrifice and expected long-run value. Total cost advantages are prospective and cognitively evaluated, as consumers must believe that lower energy or maintenance costs make the EV a worthwhile proposition before those benefits translate into purchase intention (Liu et al., 2021; Palmer et al., 2018; Soszynska et al., 2024). The indirect-only pattern therefore supports an attitudinal internalization process similar to prior evidence on perceived value and EV adoption (Ali and Naushad, 2022; Dau et al., 2025; Higueras-Castillo et al., 2019). In Bahrain, this pathway is plausible because subsidized gasoline can reduce the immediate visibility of operating savings, making favorable interpretation of long-run economic value more important (Shareeda et al., 2021). Policy incentives showed both a direct and indirect effect. This complementary pattern is consistent with research showing that incentives can alter the objective purchase environment while also improving perceived value, legitimacy, and feasibility (Bian and Panyagometh, 2023; Wang et al., 2021; Xue et al., 2023). Directly, financial or non-financial support can reduce cost and inconvenience. Indirectly, visible policy commitment can strengthen the consumer’s attitude toward EV adoption. Evidence from the U.S., Norway, Europe, China, and fleet adoption likewise suggests that incentive effects depend on design, market maturity, and the wider policy mix rather than on one universal subsidy coefficient (Alali et al., 2022; Jenn et al., 2018; Langbroek et al., 2016; Martins et al., 2024; Mersky et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2024). For Bahrain, the finding implies that policy development may matter not only because an incentive changes private cost, but also because it can reshape confidence in the emerging EV ecosystem. 5.2 Functional-performance evaluations Driving range illustrates a different form of conditionality. The simple relationship with purchase intention was positive and significant, consistent with decades of research showing that inadequate range and range anxiety can deter EV adoption (Egbue and Long, 2012; Kim et al., 2017; Neubauer and Wood, 2014). The coefficient remained positive in the performance domain model but became small and insignificant after the full set of correlated determinants was included. The appropriate conclusion is therefore not that driving range is irrelevant in Bahrain, but rather that range has clear lower-order relevance but contributes limited unique variance once acceleration, charging time sensitivity, economic evaluations, environmental values, and attitude are considered together. This is theoretically plausible because range is intertwined with charging access, travel needs, vehicle price, and broader perceptions of practical usability (Forsythe et al., 2023; Graham and Brungard, 2021). Bahrain’s compact geography may reduce the unique incremental value of additional range for routine travel, but that contextual interpretation must remain secondary to the empirical evidence of overlapping explanatory variance (Shareeda et al., 2021). Acceleration showed one of the strongest direct effects and a significant indirect effect through attitude. This result supports the view that EV adoption is not solely a sacrifice-based environmental decision. Performance itself can create value, as the immediate torque and responsive acceleration provides a functional and experiential advantage, thus creating favorable driving experiences to drive positive attitudes toward EV technology (Bai and Zheng, 2020; Higueras-Castillo et al., 2019; Roberson and Helveston, 2020). Recent evidence that technological improvement is driving EV diffusion and that performance expectations matter in Oman further support the relevance of the result in the Bahraini context (Al-Maamari et al., 2025; Forsythe et al., 2023; Pamidimukkala et al., 2024). The partial mediation pattern suggests that acceleration has both an immediate product-attribute effect and an attitudinal route. Consumers may value responsive performance directly while also using it to form a more favorable overall evaluation of EV ownership. The charging result requires careful interpretation in relation to the retained indicators. CT3 and CT4 assessed whether faster charging options and fast charging availability would encourage EV adoption, and as both items were reverse coded, higher scores indicated weaker endorsement of these charging improvements as adoption incentives. The positive direct and indirect relationships therefore suggest that respondents who placed less emphasis on these inducements also reported stronger purchase intentions and more favorable attitudes. This should not be interpreted as a preference for longer charging durations or as direct evidence of greater tolerance for charging delays. The finding remains relevant to Bahrain’s developing charging infrastructure, where convenience, charger accessibility, and uncertainty surrounding charging arrangements may shape consumer evaluations (Albinali et al., 2026; Cj et al., 2023; He et al., 2022; Qian and Yin, 2017). 5.3 Environmental values and the central role of attitude Environmental values had significant direct and indirect effects, and the alternative bootstrap produced the largest indirect effect among the factors. This pattern is consistent with research showing that environmental orientation, green self-identity, and perceived ecological value can motivate EV adoption while also shaping favorable product attitudes (Barbarossa et al., 2017; Bouscasse et al., 2018; Degirmenci and Breitner, 2017; Rehman, 2025; Rezvani et al., 2015; White and Sintov, 2017). Value-attitude-behavior evidence likewise predicts that relatively enduring values become behaviorally relevant when they are translated into an object-specific evaluation (Dau et al., 2025). The direct effect suggests that environmental values retain motivational force beyond the generalized attitude measure, while the indirect effect shows that part of their influence is internalized into a favorable evaluation of EV purchase. In Bahrain, this dynamic is reinforced by increasing climate awareness and national commitments to carbon neutrality, with younger generations particularly receptive to sustainable mobility solutions (Alsabbagh and Alnaser, 2022; Shareeda et al., 2021). Cultural framing further shapes this relationship, as cross-country differences suggest that ecological responsibility is gradually becoming a social norm in Bahrain (Qian and Yin, 2017). Consumer attitudes was the strongest direct predictor of purchase intention in the model. This is consistent with TPB and EV studies in which attitude is a proximal determinant of intention (Ajzen, 1991; Ali and Naushad, 2022; Gupta et al., 2024; Khurana et al., 2019; Kristiono et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021). Recent research using TPB, TRA, TAM, and value-based frameworks likewise finds that attitude connects beliefs, perceived value, or environmental evaluations to EV intention (Chaturvedi et al., 2022; Rahma et al., 2024; Shalu and Pandey, 2025; Thwe et al., 2025; Udiyana and Yasa, 2025). However, the present mediation results refine this general proposition. Attitude was not a universal mechanism, as it provided indirect-only mediation for economic benefits, complementary partial mediation for policy incentives, acceleration, charging time sensitivity, and environmental values, and no significant mediation for price or driving range. This heterogeneity is theoretically meaningful because different factors enter the decision process in different ways. The selective mediation pattern supports a more precise interpretation of integrated adoption models. Economic benefits appear to require evaluative internalization, whereas policy incentives, acceleration, environmental values, and charging tolerance have both attitudinal and non-attitudinal routes. By contrast, price and range show no significant specific indirect effects after the full model is estimated. This does not make them unimportant but indicates that their unique contribution to attitude is weak once overlapping determinants are controlled. Mediation theory explicitly allows such differences and cautions against classifying mechanisms solely from the significance of a direct effect (Zhao et al., 2010). The findings therefore support attitude as a central but context-dependent mechanism rather than a universal mediator applied mechanically to every EV determinant. 5.4 Theoretical implications The first theoretical implication is that the contribution of an integrated EV adoption model should be judged by the mechanism it clarifies rather than by the novelty of a particular list of predictors. Economic, technological, social, environmental, and policy variables have already been combined in many forms across TPB, TAM, UTAUT, DOI, perceived value, and related models (Adnan et al., 2016; Bryła et al., 2023; Nazir and Tian, 2022; Rezvani et al., 2015). The present contribution is more specific, showing that the examined EV evaluations differ in their direct and indirect associations with purchase intention. The model shows empirically that the same mediator can be indirect-only for one factor, complementary for another, and absent for others. This pattern adds to the belief-attitude-intention literature by identifying where attitudinal mediation appears more or less prominent across the examined determinants. Second, the disaggregation of performance matters, as range, acceleration, and charging time sensitivity are all performance variables that behave differently. Range had stronger lower-order relevance but weak unique full-model explanatory power, while acceleration retained strong direct and indirect paths, and charging time sensitivity showed significant direct and indirect effects once its orientation was interpreted correctly. Treating these attributes as a single performance construct would conceal these differences. The result supports more micro-theorizing about functional attributes in sustainable technology adoption (Forsythe et al., 2023; Higueras-Castillo et al., 2019). Third, the study demonstrates the theoretical importance of distinguishing zero-order association from unique multivariate effect. The price sign reversal and weakening of range are not minor statistical details, but change the substantive claims that can legitimately be made. A coefficient in a multivariate structural model represents the predictor’s unique association conditional on the remaining model, not the consumer’s unconditional preference for the attribute. This distinction is especially important in integrated adoption models containing strongly related beliefs and evaluations (Tu et al., 2008). Robustness analysis should therefore be used not merely to confirm significance but to improve theoretical interpretation. 5.5 Policy and managerial implications The results suggest that EV diffusion in Bahrain is unlikely to be accelerated by a single intervention. Policy incentives have both direct and attitudinal effects, indicating that a credible policy package can simultaneously improve purchasing conditions and consumer confidence. International evidence favors coordinated combinations of financial support, charging investments, and non-financial convenience rather than isolated measures (Anilan and Vij, 2024; Hardman, 2019; Qadir et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Bahrain’s emerging initiatives provide a foundation, but the policy environment remains less mature than leading EV markets (Bahrain Insurance Association, 2024; Bahrain News Agency, 2023). Any new incentives such as VAT exemptions, reductions in import tariffs, or priority parking and lanes should therefore be evaluated alongside infrastructure readiness, fiscal sustainability, and consumer awareness rather than treated as a stand-alone solution (Alsabbagh and Alnaser, 2022; Anilan and Vij, 2024; Shareeda et al., 2021). For manufacturers and dealers, the findings argue against marketing EVs only as environmentally responsible products. Acceleration was a strong predictor, and environmental values also mattered directly and through attitude. Communications can therefore combine performance experience, credible cost of ownership information, and environmental value. Test drives may be particularly useful because direct experiences can correct misconceptions and make performance advantages tangible (Roberson and Helveston, 2020). Moreover, price communication must remain transparent, as the sign-reversal finding does not justify premium pricing as a demand strategy. The IPMA findings provide further insight into the practical priorities emerging from the structural model. As Figure 2 shows, consumer attitudes, environmental values, acceleration, and policy incentives were among the most influential factors associated with purchase intention. Acceleration exhibited comparatively lower performance than other highly important constructs, suggesting opportunities for manufacturers and dealers to strengthen consumer awareness through practical demonstrations and test drives. Driving range, by contrast, showed the highest performance but relatively low importance. This pattern is consistent with its limited unique contribution in the full structural model and suggests that emphasizing longer driving ranges alone may offer less value than addressing broader consumer evaluations. In Bahrain’s compact geographic setting, communication focused on the adequacy of existing EV ranges for routine journeys may therefore be more useful than emphasizing maximum range specifications. Although charging time sensitivity showed the lowest performance, its reverse coded measurement and comparatively modest importance require cautious interpretation. Similarly, the low price performance score should not be interpreted as evidence that affordability is unimportant, particularly given the conditional nature of the estimated price coefficient. Overall, the findings support a coordinated approach combining policy support, performance communication, and improved consumer understanding of EV ownership. Charging-related communication should also account for differences in consumers’ practical requirements. Potential adopters may be comfortable with home charging or short routine travel, whereas others require visible public fast-charging availability. Infrastructure planning in Manama and across Bahrain should therefore consider not only the number of chargers but also spatial access, waiting time, reliability, and expected demand (Albinali et al., 2026). Policy and industry actors can reduce uncertainty by publishing clear information on charger locations, charging times, installation options, and future network development. Lastly, because 331 respondents were non-owners, the findings are especially relevant to pre-adoption communication. Prospective adopters may be evaluating EVs through incomplete information and imagined use rather than lived ownership experience. Evidence from non-user studies and policy research suggests that knowledge, trial experience, and credible information can influence adoption interest (Peng et al., 2025; Samarasinghe et al., 2024). Bahrain’s emerging market may therefore benefit from demonstration programs, fleet trials, public test driving events, and transparent total cost tools that allow consumer to evaluate EVs on practical rather than abstract terms. 5.6 Limitations and directions for future research While this study provides important contributions, several limitations are acknowledged that shape the interpretation of the findings and point toward future research opportunities. First, the convenience sample does not provide known selection probabilities and cannot be assumed to represent the demographic or behavioral distribution of Bahrain’s population, all current vehicle buyers, or all potential EV adopters. The non-owner and demographic-control robustness analyses show that the main coefficient pattern is not driven solely by prior EV owners or the observed demographic composition, but these analyses do not convert a non-probability sample into a representative one. Future research should use probability-based or carefully stratified sampling where feasible and should compare Bahrain with other Gulf markets to determine which mechanisms are context-specific. Second, the data are cross-sectional and self-reported. The temporal sequence implied by the theoretical model is grounded in prior theory but cannot be established causally from one survey wave. Common-method bias also cannot be eliminated completely when predictor, mediator, and outcome measures are collected from the same respondents at the same time. The Harman diagnostic did not indicate dominance by a single factor; however, this remains a limited assessment and cannot rule out common-method bias. Longitudinal, experimental, field, and simulation studies would provide stronger evidence on how attitudes and intentions change with policy interventions, test drives, ownership experience, or charging network expansion. Third, policy incentives and driving range exhibited weaker internal consistency than conventionally preferred, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.621 and 0.532, respectively. Although both constructs met the commonly applied thresholds for composite reliability and AVE, the low alpha values introduce uncertainty into their measurement. Among the policy indicators, POL3, which concerns discouragement arising from insufficient financial support, had the lowest retained loading (0.653). For driving range, DR2 assessed range adequacy for daily needs with a comparatively low loading (0.617), while DR1 was excluded during measurement refinement. The retained range indicators also address different aspects of perceived adequacy, including daily travel, longer journeys, and comparison with conventional vehicles. These results do not establish that any single item caused the reliability problem, but they identify areas for further refinement. Future studies should use quantitative item development, cognitive pretesting, and additional scale validation to improve consistency while preserving the relevant dimensions of each construct. Fourth, consumer attitudes and purchase intention were highly related, with HTMT = 0.933. The bootstrap CI [0.868, 0.990] excluded 1.00 but did not satisfy the stricter 0.90 criterion, leaving a substantive discriminant validity concern. This overlap is particularly relevant because attitude and purchase intention occupy separate theoretical positions in the proposed mediation framework. Although the constructs are conceptually distinguished, their empirical proximity introduces uncertainty into the interpretation of the estimated indirect relationships. Future research should develop more clearly differentiated attitude and intention measures, particularly by separating general evaluations of EV adoption from recommendation intentions and specific future purchasing commitments. Temporally separated measurement and observed adoption behavior would provide stronger evidence of the proposed distinction. Fifth, the questionnaire measured EV perceptions at the general product-category level and did not anchor respondents to a standardized vehicle model, brand, price point, or segment. This was consistent with the study’s objective of examining general EV adoption beliefs, but it also means that respondents may have evaluated different internal reference vehicles when considering price, range, acceleration, charging requirements, and perceived value. Such reference-object heterogeneity may increase measurement variability and limit vehicle-specific interpretations of the findings. Future research can address this limitation through standardized vehicle profiles, experimental vignettes, stated-choice designs, or analyses stratified by vehicle class, price segment, or brand familiarity. Lastly, the bounded theoretical model deliberately excludes variables such as perceived usefulness, perceived risk, trust, social influence, facilitating conditions, and environmental concern. This focus permits a clearer test of the economic-performance-environmental belief-attitude intention mechanism, but omitted constructs may explain additional variance or moderate the observed relationships (Acar and Taşkın, 2025; Nazir and Tian, 2022; Udiyana and Yasa, 2025). Future models can extend the framework in theoretically targeted ways rather than adding variables indiscriminately. 6 Conclusion This study examined how economic, performance, and environmental evaluations shape EV purchase intention in Bahrain, with consumer attitudes modeled as a potential mediating mechanism within a PLS-SEM framework. The results show that attitude is central but not universal. Economic benefits operated primarily through attitude, while policy incentives, acceleration, charging time sensitivity and environmental values operated through both direct and indirect pathways, and price and driving range did not exhibit significant specific indirect effects through attitude. This determinant-specific pattern supports a selective mediation perspective in which different EV evaluations enter the decision process through different psychological routes. The robustness analyses also materially refine the interpretation of the most unusual coefficients. Price was negatively related to intention in the simple model and became small and positive only after adjustment for correlated determinants, so the full-model coefficient cannot be interpreted as a preference for expensive EVs. Driving range was positively associated with intention but lost unique significance in the complete model, indicating overlapping rather than absent relevance. The positive charging coefficient reflects the reverse coded measurement of faster charging inducements and should not be interpreted as a preference for slower charging. These distinctions make the substantive conclusions more conservative and defensible. For Bahrain, the findings indicate that EV diffusion will require a combination of policy credibility, performance experience, economic value communication, charging confidence, and environmental positioning. For theory, the study demonstrates that integrated adoption models are most informative when they explain how determinants operate rather than simply how many determinants are included. Future research should test these mechanisms with representative, longitudinal, experimental, and revealed-behavior designs across Bahrain and the Gulf.
