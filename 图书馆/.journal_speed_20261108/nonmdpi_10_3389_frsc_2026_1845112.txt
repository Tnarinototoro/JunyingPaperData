Fachbereich V – Life Sciences and Technology, Berliner Hochschule für Technik, Berlin, Germany See more Article metrics View details Abstract Trees sequester and store carbon in their biomass and are therefore often part of cities’ plans to become carbon neutral. However, nursing and management of trees result in emissions. In order to quantify carbon sources and sinks along the lifecycle of street trees in Germany, a life cycle inventory study was performed in this research. The inventory was based on interviews with nurseries, municipal green space managers, and arborist experts as well as insights from published studies. The life cycle inventory study was performed for three common tree species and three emission scenarios. The emission scenarios illustrate how individual processes such as the irrigation requirement, pruning, and pest management can impact the overall CO2 balance. Within 26 years at the urban site, all three tree species in all scenarios reached net zero. In the medium emissions scenario, it took 4 years for Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’, 12 years for Corylus colurna, and 15 years for Tilia cordata. The urban site management was associated with more emissions than the nursery and end-of-life stage. If pest management at the urban site was required, this became the process with the highest emissions, followed by pruning, irrigation, and felling. This study highlights the importance of favorable growth conditions, short transport distances, and optimized urban tree management for minimizing emissions and maximizing sequestration. (2026) https://BioRender.com/b3db6tg. 1 Introduction In urban areas, large amounts of anthropogenic CO2 are emitted, which contributes to rising global temperatures and changed weather patterns (IPCC, 2023). In their aim to move towards carbon-neutrality, planting trees is a small, yet popular tool in the toolkit of cities (Ariluoma et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025), because trees store carbon in their biomass, besides providing several additional ecosystem services (Roy et al., 2012). While the amount of carbon stored in trees varies based on the soil, site (Sonti et al., 2025), species, or tree dimensions (Nowak et al., 2002), all urban trees sequester and store carbon as long as they grow. However, planting and managing trees in urban areas are associated with carbon emissions from vehicles and machines (Nowak et al., 2002; Strohbach et al., 2012; Petri et al., 2016; Nicese et al., 2021; Dowtin et al., 2023; Lind et al., 2023). A deeper understanding of the overall CO2 balance of urban trees is therefore necessary to more accurately quantify the ecosystem services they provide and to reduce emissions accumulating throughout their lifecycle. Life cycle inventory analysis (LCI) is the part of life cycle assessments where inputs and outputs of a product are assessed and is therefore a valuable tool for understanding the overall CO2 balance. Several LCI related to urban trees, urban green space or tree production have been conducted in recent years (e.g., Kendall and McPherson, 2012; Strohbach et al., 2012; McPherson et al., 2015; Lind et al., 2023). Emissions, usually expressed as carbon dioxide equivalents (CO2e; ISO, 2006), are associated with all life stages of urban trees. Tree production, e.g., is a specialized process with trees commonly being produced in two or more nurseries (Kendall and McPherson, 2012; Lind et al., 2023), causing emissions from transport (Lind et al., 2023). For the urban management practices, arboricultural measures include planting, irrigation or maintenance during lifetimes of several decades (Nowak et al., 2002; Strohbach et al., 2012; McPherson et al., 2015; Lind et al., 2023). The end-of-life and re-use of urban trees has been studied by Alanya-Rosenbaum et al. (2022); Speak et al. (2020), and usually comprises felling, shredding and disposal. Urban green spaces can be carbon sinks (e.g., Konopka et al., 2021; Manzini et al., 2025), but if construction has involved machinery and required transport, green space elements such as trees are already associated with emissions at the moment they are planted. Depending on growth and management, trees may offset these emissions by growing and sequestering carbon into their biomass. A good way to understand this dynamic is the “net zero” point, i.e., the point in time after planting when emissions from previous production steps, planting and maintenance are finally offset. The time it takes to reach net zero is influenced by several factors, including planted species, maintenance and growth rates (Nowak et al., 2002; Strohbach et al., 2012; Petri et al., 2016; Lind et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2023). Net zero points of 4 to over 20 years have been reported (Strohbach et al., 2012; Petri et al., 2016; Lind et al., 2023; Manzini et al., 2025), and in some extreme cases, the net zero point may not be reached during the lifetime of urban trees (McPherson et al., 2015). To evaluate when net zero is reached, i.e., when emissions from planting and maintenance have been offset by tree growth, many studies rely on unspecific growth rates and allometric equations. Commonly used tools are the i-Tree software suite from the U.S. Forest Service (Ingram, 2012; Lind et al., 2023; Antenucci et al., 2025) or the Center for Urban Forest Research Tree Carbon Calculator (Petri et al., 2016). This study adds to the existing literature by conducting a LCI of three species planted as street trees in Germany, focusing on CO2 stored in biomass and CO2e emissions from processes. This LCI builds and expands previous studies by including emissions from nursery production and pest management in addition to the previously studied aspects of planting, maintenance, tree growth, as well as felling and removal. We quantify how distances travelled, irrigation and pest management influence the CO2 balance. For the calculation of the net zero point, we used growth rates based on German street trees, calculated from a tree inventory. We focused on three tree species: Corylus colurna, Tilia cordata, and the cultivar Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’. Our estimates are based on interviews with practitioners in combination with a literature review and reflect typical conditions in northern Germany. The study aims to answer the following questions: • When do the investigated urban trees reach their net zero point? • Which phases and processes of an urban tree life cycle have the greatest impact on the LCI? • Which influence do the transport distances have? With our study, we can show which leverage points exist for reducing carbon emissions associated with street trees, thereby showing paths to more sustainable practices and supporting cities’ efforts to become carbon neutral. 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Standards, goal, scope, and functional unit The conducted LCI study is based on the ISO 14044:2006 (ISO, 2006) and the Hortifootprint Category Rules (Helmes et al., 2020). The ISO 14044:2006 identifies four sections of a life cycle assessment: the goal and scope definition phase, the LCI phase, life cycle impact assessment (LCIA) phase and the interpretation phase. In this paper we focused on the following phases: (i) the goal and scope definition phase, (ii) the LCI, (iii) limitation of the LCI, and (iv) the interpretation phase. We defined the functional unit as a street tree belonging to the species Tilia cordata (small-leaved lime), Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ (upright English oak, pyramid oak) or Corylus colurna (Turkish hazel). We identified four management stages: young tree nursery, street tree nursery, urban site, and end-of-life, of which only the last three are included in the LCI. The net zero point is reached when there is a balance between the human-caused greenhouse gas emissions and the human-led removals out of the system (ISO, 2022). In this study, net zero is the point in time when a tree has sequestered more carbon than was emitted during the production and/or maintenance. 2.2 Data acquisition, data quality, and system boundaries The primary data consist of expert interviews with employees of three tree nurseries, the municipal green space departments from the cities of Braunschweig (Lower Saxony, Germany) and Brandenburg an der Havel (Brandenburg, Germany), and arborists from two urban forestry companies. The main criteria for the chosen companies were that they delivered trees to or worked with the mentioned cities of Braunschweig and Brandenburg an der Havel. One urban forest company had an expertise in tree care, the other in tree care and removal services. The interviews were conducted in 2024 and 2025. The respondents wished to remain anonymous. Based on the answers, we divided the LCI into four stages (Figure 1): (i) young tree nursery, (ii) street tree nursery, (iii) the urban site, and (iv) end-of-life. Each stage consists of different processes further described in section 2.3 Production system and assumptions. The young tree nursery phase was not included in the LCI; this limitation is further described in 2.4 Limitations. The presented data is representative of practices aligning with German standards. However, the chosen nursery companies differed in size and standard operating procedure and hence represent a wide range of practices. The urban site and end-of-life data are based on interviews from one medium-sized and one large city (73,939 and 254,469, respectively; Amt für Statistik Berlin-Brandenburg, 2025; Stadt Braunschweig Politik und Verwaltung, 2025). However, the distances and handling of urban tree management cannot be generalized to small towns or very large cities, as they were not covered by the interviews. As in previous studies e.g. Ingram, 2012; Strohbach et al., 2012, emissions from the production of machines and infrastructure were excluded. The emissions of the machines varied due to their duration of use, power output of the engine, and fuel type. In order to make the results comparable, we used reference values from the Association for Technology and Structures in Agriculture in Germany (KTBL, 2021) and the Austrian Council for Agricultural Engineering and Rural Development NPO (ÖKL, 2012, 2025) as secondary data whenever possible. The reference values are not published with an uncertainty degree. To address this limitation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis on the fuel use and the duration of machine running times of different distances in the nursery and in the cities, further described in 2.5 Sensitivity analysis. 2.3 Production system and assumptions A detailed description of the stages and processes of the LCI can be found in the Supplement. Factors like the water requirement of plants and pest management were associated with great uncertainties, as they depend on the annual weather conditions and occurrence of pathogens and pests. Therefore, based on the answers collected in the interviews, we developed different scenarios corresponding to a minimum (min), maximum (max), and mean value of measures or amounts used for calculating carbon emissions (Table 1). We will refer to these scenarios hereinafter as min scenario, max scenario, and mean scenario, respectively. The min and max represented the outer spectrum of the answers given by our interview partners. The mean scenario constitutes the central estimate between the min and max assumptions. Only the processes that differ across the three scenarios are presented here. The scenarios are based on interviews with three tree nurseries, two green space agencies, and two urban forestry companies. Transport to nursery and arrival. The 8/10 cm stem girth trees were transported from a young tree nursery to the street tree nursery. The transport distances varied according to the scenario (Table 1). Growing conditions in the street tree nursery. The 8/10 cm stem girth trees arrived at the street tree nursery, where they grew for 3 years until they reached an 18/20 cm stem girth and were sold (stage 2 in Figure 1). The trees grew in native soil without added substrate. To facilitate further calculations, the following assumptions were made, taking the data from the interviews into account: the distance between the field and the machine depot was set to be 3.5 km. One field was assumed to be 2000 m2 with space for 402 trees. From the arrival of the street tree nursery gate to transplanting, a mortality rate of 2% is expected. Preparation of the soil. Before planting, the soil was loosened and fertilized and a drip irrigation was installed in the entire field. The inorganic N-fertilizers per field differed, based on the scenario (Table 1). Irrigation. Irrigation was applied between April and September, depending on weather conditions. The number of days on which watering was necessary varied (Table 1). Each field received a consistent 1,600 L of water per day and the pump could deliver that entire daily volume within an hour. Pruning. Pruning during the nursery stage was done once per year, but the pruning duration differed between the species and year. In the first year, the pruning duration for Tilia cordata was 3.5 min/tree, and in the second and third years it was 6 min/tree. Pruning Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ took 1 min/tree in year one, and in years two and three, 1.5 min/tree. Corylus colurna had the shortest pruning times, with 1 min/tree in all 3 years. Mowing. Mowing was done between three and seven times a year; in Table 1 the scenarios are summarized. Pest management. Only Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ required pest management. Following the answers of the interview partners, powdery mildew (Erysiphe alphitoides) and oak processionary moth (Thaumetopoea processionea) had to be treated. Transplanting. When the tree had reached a DBH of 18 to 20 cm it was excavated and packaged for transportation to the city. Transport to city. After 3 years, the trees were sold and transported to the cities (stage 3 in Figure 1). The transport distance between the nursery and the cities, and the transport distance of the soil varied depending on the scenario (Table 1). The wooden poles required for the urban plantings were purchased from nearby horticultural businesses and a transport distance of 30 km was assumed. Condition in the city. Brandenburg an der Havel and Braunschweig are midsize cities, with several depots (aka building yards or Bauhof). Therefore, distances of 2.5 km were assumed as an average distance between the depot and the planting site. Preparation of the tree pit. Before the tree was planted, the site was inspected, parking restriction signs were placed, a substrate exchange was done, and wooden poles were inserted. Planting of the tree. During the planting, the tree was placed in the substrate, watered and secured by the wooden poles. Irrigation. The watering interval varied based on the scenario, but the amount of water remained the same. In the first year, 200 L water/day/tree were assumed, in the second year 150 L water/day/tree, and in the third year 100 L water/day/tree. After that, trees are rarely irrigated according to our interview partners. Tree control. The tree’s health was regularly monitored by expert arborists. Each arborist was assumed to look at around 90 trees per day. Pruning. Street trees have to be pruned to ensure that vehicles and people can pass safely underneath. Depending on the scenario, we assumed that pruning was required between twice and ten times in 50 years (Table 1). Pest management. The interview partners stated that only Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ required pest management, namely of the oak processionary moth. The occurrence of the oak processionary moth requires an intervention in residential areas for public safety in Germany (Sobczyk, 2014), in most cases by mechanical removal. Depending on the scenario, pest management was assumed to be conducted zero to twenty times in 50 years (Table 1). Tree growth and carbon storage. To assess when net zero is reached, we determined city-adapted allometric equations and growth rates for each species. We calculated allometric equations for all three species based on terrestrial laser scans and volume estimates of 19 Corylus colurna, 17 Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ and 35 Tilia cordata. The scans were carried out with a Riegl VZ400i terrestrial laser scanner on windless conditions in the leaf-off season, and raw data can be found in a repository (Strohbach, 2026). Using quantitative structure models (Markku et al., 2015) with twig correction (Morales and MacFarlane, 2025) and wood densities (Zanne et al., 2009), we fitted equations of type: Where Dw is the dry weight of woody biomass [kg], DBH is the diameter at breast height [cm] and a and b are species-specific parameters (a = −2.108099 and b = 2.376905 for Corylus colurna, a = −0.684975 and b = 2.004488 for Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’, a = −3.479559 and b = 2.718865 for Tilia cordata). Dry weight was converted to CO2 by multiplying it by the factor of 1.854 (using molecular weight for conversion and assuming that 47% of wood is carbon; McGroddy et al., 2004). For tree growth, species-specific growth rates were calculated from the tree inventory of Brandenburg an der Havel. The cadaster of Braunschweig did not contain enough entries with planting date. We used the cadaster from 2023, which contains 33,558 trees. From the cadaster, we selected only trees with the identifier for street trees and a standing age below 60 years. Then we selected the species in focus of this study, which resulted in 117 Corylus colurna and 1,247 Tilia cordata. Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ was not common enough to allow for the selection of only street trees and we used all entries, which totaled 21. The growth pattern for all three species was linear, so we determined growth from the slope of a linear regression. For Corylus colurna and Tilia cordata, we determined low, medium and high growth rates using a boot strapping approach where we randomly sampled 50 trees and extracted the slope of a linear model for 1,000 times. The lowest, median and highest observed growth rates were used for the min, mean and max scenarios (0.51 cm/a, 0.65 cm/a, 0.77 cm/a for Corylus colurna, 0.56 cm/a, 0.69 cm/a, 0.84 cm/a for Tilia cordata). The number of Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ trees was too low for the bootstrap approach, so we simply fitted a linear model and used the slope for the mean scenario, and the lower and upper bounds of the 95% confidence interval for the min and max scenarios (0.47 cm/a, 0.71 cm/a, 0.94 cm/a). To calculate net zero, we combined the max scenario with low growth rates to simulate an unfavorable condition, the medium growth rates with the mean scenario, and min scenario with high growth rates to simulate favorable conditions. All calculations were carried out in R 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024). Felling preparation. In this step, parking restriction signs were placed on the site. Felling. The urban site stage was assumed to end with the felling of the tree after a standing age of 50 years. This lifespan was consistent with our interviews and previously conducted studies by Strohbach et al. (2012); Lind et al. (2023); Manzini et al. (2025). The felling depends on the size of the tree and the site, and we evaluated three different scenarios (Table 1). Further use. Here, the last stage (stage 4 in Figure 1) is entered. About 5% of the shredded biomass was assumed to be distributed in planting sites and parks; the remaining 95% were transported to a local heating plant 5 km away. 2.4 Limitations This study does not include the production stage from the seedling to the two times transplanted tree with a girth of 8/10 cm (stage 1 in Figure 1). We reached out to several nurseries, but none were willing to give an interview; hence, we could not include this stage. In stage 2, the street tree nursery phase, we did not account for maintenance of fences and field margins, nor did we include the manufacturing of machines or small items (plastic connection to bamboo cane, jute, and wire for rootball wrapping). Further, we did not consider the waste disposal or the construction of buildings. Work-related breaks and ways (food breaks, shortened working days, etc.) were not taken into account. Land use changes that may have been related to the expansion of the tree nursery were also not included in the balance sheet, nor was underground carbon storage. The urban site phase (stage 3) features uncertainties, and in particular, the future need for pest management or irrigation are impossible to predict. This uncertainty is reflected in the relatively large bandwidth chosen for these activities in the min, mean and max scenarios. During the street tree nursery stage, important pests in the here analyzed tree species were powdery mildew and oak processionary moth. Other pests were not considered. During the urban site stage, based on the interviews, no pesticides were used, and only oak processionary moth was removed if infestations were detected. The substrate used in urban areas does not contain peat and emissions due to peat extraction were not evaluated. Further, tree pit cover is highly location-dependent and some are bare, some are covered with mulch, and some have grass or other plants growing on them and some even have little guerilla gardens maintained by residents. Maintenance varies according to cover but also according to location in the city. We were unable to include these practices in the carbon footprint calculation. The same holds true for waste disposal of used wooden poles, tree trimmings and leaf litter. 2.5 Sensitivity analysis A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of the machine fuel use and running time for different distances. The mean Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ scenario was used as the baseline scenario. To assess the impact of the distances covered, a 10% decrease and increase in the nursery and city roadways was implemented. The impact of a 10% decrease and increase of machine fuel use was also assessed. 3 Results 3.1 Total carbon emissions per urban street tree Total carbon emissions from all three tree species and emission scenarios are plotted in Figure 2. Here it becomes evident that the required management had a large impact on the CO2e per tree. The max emission scenario for Tilia cordata was 220.3% higher than the min emission scenario. Similarly, the max scenario for Corylus colurna was 219.9% higher than the min scenario. For Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ this difference was even higher (389.7%). Species-specific management, such as pruning and pest management, significantly impacted the CO2 balance. Included are all three life-cycle inventory stages: the street tree nursery production, urban site management, and the end-of-life stage. 3.2 When do the investigated urban trees reach their net zero point? The CO2 balance starts in the negative, i.e., with emissions, for the majority of the scenarios and species (Figure 3, Supplementary Figure S1). This is because the emissions that were associated with the soil preparation, planting, and management during the first years exceeded the sequestered CO2. The only tree that sequestered more CO2 by the time it was planted in the city than was emitted during nursery production and plantation, was Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ in the min scenario. In their min scenarios, Corylus colurna required 6 years to reach net zero; Tilia cordata required 9 years. The net zero in the mean scenario for Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ was reached after 4 years, for Corylus colurna after 12 years, and for Tilia cordata after 15 years. In the max emission scenario, Tilia cordata reached net zero after 26 years at the urban site; for Corylus colurna it took 22 and for Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ 16 years to reach net zero. Our analysis revealed that the majority of the emissions were associated with the stage at the urban site (Figure 4; Supplementary Figures S2, S3). During the end-of-life stage, the second-highest emissions were caused. The nursery stage had a very small impact on the LCI. Transplanting generated roughly half of the emissions in the nursery phase in the mean scenario (Table 2). In all three emission scenarios for all species, the irrigation at the nursery stage made up less than 0.1% of the total emission. The processes that resulted in the most emissions were pruning, felling, and irrigation (see Figure 4; Supplementary Figures S2, S3). In the case of Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ (Figure 4), pest management of the oak processionary moth was assumed in the mean and max scenario. If pest management was needed, it became the process generating the highest emissions, constituting 29.7% to 34.6% of the total emission (84.8 kg CO2e per tree in the mean scenario and 169.6 kg CO2e in the max scenario). In comparison, the emissions associated with pest management in the street tree nursery made up a maximum of 0.1% of the total emission over the full lifetime. The pruning of urban trees resulted in 33.7 kg CO2e per tree in the min scenario (around 32% of the total LCI) and 84.4 kg CO2e per tree in the mean scenario, which was 42% for Tilia cordata and Corylus colurna and around 29.6% of the Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ LCI. In the max scenario it was 168.7 kg CO2e per tree, which made up 34.5% to 52.7% of the LCI. For all three species, the irrigation process in the urban area resulted in 13.75 kg CO2e per tree, which corresponds to 12.9% of the total carbon footprint in the min emission scenario. In the mean emission scenario, it was 34 kg CO2e per tree, which translates to 11.9% of the total Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ emissions, 17% for Tilia cordata and Corylus colurna. The irrigation led to 50.97 kg CO2e per tree in the max emission scenario, which was 10.4% of the total Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ emissions and 15.9% for Tilia cordata and Corylus colurna. 3.3.3 End-of-life The felling and shredding processes caused the biggest emissions in the end-of-life stage. In fact, both processes together made up around 21.7% of the total LCI (23.14 kg CO2e per tree) in the min emission scenario, in the mean emission scenario depending on the species 12.6% to 17.9% and in the max scenario 8.2% to 12.6% (40.34 kg CO2e per tree). 3.4 Which influence do the transport distances have? In the LCI, we calculated three transport ways: (i) the distance between the young tree nursery and the street tree nursery, (ii) the distance between the street tree nursery and the urban site, and (iii) the distance for soil delivery. The distance between the young tree nursery and the street tree nursery varied between 10, 150, and 300 km, which caused 0.02, 0.32, and 0.64 kg CO2e emissions per tree. To deliver the trees and the soil to the urban site, the trees were assumed to be transported either 30, 200, or 380 km and the soil 30, 100, or 200 km. The tree transport from the nursery to the urban site caused emissions of 0.25 (min), 1.6 (mean) and 3.1 (max) kg CO2e per tree. The transportation and unloading emissions combined resulted in 5.35, 13.85, 25.46 kg CO2e per tree. All the distances, loading and unloading steps combined made up between 5.02% and 8.16% of the LCI for Tilia cordata and Corylus colurna. For Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ this is 4.97% to 5.33% of the LCI. 3.5 Sensitivity analysis The sensitivity analysis revealed that, of the two factors examined, the amount of fuel that the machines used had a higher impact on the LCI than the distances covered in the nursery and urban area. Increasing the fuel use by 10% lead to an increase of the LCI of 9.82%. However, when the distances in the nursery and urban site are 10% longer, then this results in a 5.8% increase in the LCI. 4 Discussion The presented study highlights how much the management practices and environmental conditions (need for irrigation and pest management) influence the CO2 emissions released during the lifetime of a tree. The emissions for Tilia cordata ranged from 107.0 to 320.3 CO2e per tree, for Corylus colurna from 106.5 to 319.9 CO2e per tree. For Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’, which required the most management, the emissions ranged from 106.8 to 489.7 kg CO2e per tree. However, when standing for at least 26 years, all three species reached net zero. With a 50-year growth period, a tree can store up to 2677.4 kg CO2. The results presented are based on assumptions derived from expert interviews, local cadaster data and laser scans of city trees. Any change in the underlying assumptions could have a significant impact on the CO2 balance and the net zero point. The sensitivity analysis makes it evident that fuel consumption is a key factor in the LCI. This is particularly relevant when one considers that a shift to electric vehicles could reduce emissions much further (Wüstenhagen and Kirschstein, 2024; Fairuz et al., 2026). However, this exceeds the scope of this paper. 4.1 Net zero of urban trees In our study, net zero was reached for Tilia cordata after 9–26 years, for Corylus colurna after 6–22 years, and for Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ after 0–16 years. The biggest influence on net zero is the growth rate, which influences the overall carbon sequestration in a non-linear way, but is also linked to high uncertainty (see 4.2.4 Growth at urban sites). Overall, net zero is comparable to other studies. Fast-growing species such as Salix alba reached their net zero after 4 to 8 years and Quercus rubra after 4 to 10 years at an urban site (Lind et al., 2023). Looking at a pocket park and not street trees and without including nursery production, Strohbach et al. (2012) found net zero points of 4 to 6 years for a mix of species. Pinus sylvestris reached net zero after 12 to 16 years (Lind et al., 2023). According to Manzini et al. (2025) a newly established urban forest takes 13 years to reach net zero. Other studies indicate that reaching net zero can require a much longer period. Acer rubrum reached carbon neutrality after between 26 and 35 years in a study by Petri et al. (2016). Some studies even conclude that the urban practice can result in emissions that cannot be mitigated by carbon sequestration in trees (McPherson et al., 2015), thus, net zero is never reached. This is unlikely in a setting like ours. The interview partners were asked for peat use in substrates or city plantings and did not report on the use of peat. However, it is still a common component of substrates in nurseries and in urban planting (Lind et al., 2023; Moinel et al., 2024; Levinsson et al., 2025) and affects the overall CO2 balance of urban greening systems. The integration of peat in the planting substrate can delay the time when urban trees reach the net zero point. Riikonen et al. (2017) showed that including the soil carbon losses due to peat extraction for planting substrate production would result in street trees reaching a net sequestration only after 30 years. This highlights that whenever peat is used in substrates, it is essential to include it in the CO2 balance. Another often-overlooked process is carbon loss through soil respiration, which is accelerated by the mixing of the soil when planting a new tree (Havu et al., 2022). Previous research has examined the root systems of urban street trees only to a limited extent (Day et al., 2010). The ratio of the root projected area to the crown projected area ranged from 1.38 to 1.51 in urban Acer campestre trees (Cermák et al., 2000). 14.3%–40% of the trees total biomass is estimated to be stored belowground (Harris, 1992; McPherson et al., 1994; Johnson and Gerhold, 2003; McPherson et al., 2013). Taking the carbon stored in the root system into account would mean that the net zero point will be reached sooner. However, the uncertainties about the root-to-shoot ratio and below ground biomass of urban trees are substantial (Jenerette and Herrmann, 2024), which is why below ground carbon storage was not included in this analysis. Since below ground carbon storage is not included in the presented analysis, this study underestimates carbon stored in trees compared to studies using i-Tree such as Lind et al. (2023). Studies that base their analysis of carbon storage on i-Tree assume a root-to-shoot ratio of 0.26 (Nowak, 2020). 4.2 Impact of stages and processes on the LCI The LCI of the three tree species showed that the urban site management had by far the highest impact, followed by the end-of-life stage. The street tree nursery stage had a rather small impact on the LCI. This can be explained by the highly optimized production processes in the tree nurseries with high numbers of individuals per acre and coordinated management procedures. Once planted at the urban site, the trees have to be managed as smaller groups or individuals leading to higher emissions per tree. The importance of the urban stage was also highlighted by Manzini et al. (2025), where the tree planting and maintenance together made up around 80% and the nursery stage only 20%. 4.2.1 Street tree nursery stage The street tree nursery stage of Tilia cordata, Corylus colurna and Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ caused, depending on the species and the scenario, between 3.9 and 5.8 kg CO2e emissions per tree. Lind et al. (2023) report higher CO2e per tree for the nursery production in Sweden. In their study, the nursery phases of Salix alba, Quercus rubra, and Pinus sylvestris resulted in 7.62, 11.18, and 12.19 kg CO2e per tree, respectively (Lind et al., 2023). This is most likely due to the nursery phase being more than twice as long (between 7.5 and 12 years) as in our study (3 years). When taking this into account, the values of kg CO2e per tree are very similar to our calculations. Lind et al. (2023) also evaluated the entire tree nursery production and report that for the young tree nursery, only 0.45, 0.46 and 0.24 kg CO2e per tree were produced for Salix alba, Quercus rubra, and Pinus sylvestris, respectively. The nursery production of a 15 cm girth Acer rubrum ‘October Glory’ tree resulted in 0.84 kg CO2e per tree (Ingram, 2012). Hence, young nursery tree production appears to involve few emissions per tree. 4.2.2 Process analysis during the street tree nursery stage In this study, transplanting of Tilia cordata, Corylus colurna and Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ was the process with the highest emission in the nursery stage. Around 50% of the emissions during the nursery stage were associated with the transplanting and transport preparation of the tree (Table 2). The importance of transplanting was also found by Ingram (2012), where the production of the tree resulted in 0.84 kg CO2e per tree, while transport and transplanting to landscape resulted in 7.37 kg CO2e per tree. These findings highlight that transplanting is the process that should be optimized whenever possible. Following transplanting, soil preparation and mowing was the most emission-intensive processes in our study. Very different mowing intensities and emissions are reported in comparable studies. Mowing rows between Cercis canadensis ‘Forest Pansy’ trees once per year lead to 0.0012 kg CO2e per tree (Ingram and Hall, 2013). The Acer rubrum ‘October Glory’ fields were mowed 16 times, which resulted in that study in 0.28 kg CO2e per tree (Ingram, 2012). In our study, mowing was included 3 to 7 times a year depending on the scenario and led to emissions between 0.24 and 0.56 kg CO2e per tree. A reason for the somewhat higher emissions compared to the above mentioned studies can be the high assumption of the fuel use of 20 L/ha following the ÖKL (2012). The mowing frequency impacts the CO2e per process. During the production of an Acer rubrum ‘October Glory’ tree, the pesticides applied caused 0.201 kg CO2e per tree, the 14 applications of the herbicide caused 0.33 kg CO2e per tree, and the 21 insecticide applications resulted in 0.53 kg CO2e per tree (Ingram, 2012). In our study the pest management of powdery mildew and the oak processionary moth during the nursery phase combined led to 0.28 kg CO2e per Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ tree. Producing a Cercis canadensis ‘Forest Pansy’ tree required the application of herbicides and insecticides. The application of these pesticides results in 0.123 kg CO2e per tree, and the pesticides itself result in 0.135 kg CO2e (Ingram and Hall, 2013). The contribution of the water management to the LCI in our study was low over the three-year nursery period. In the mean scenario, the irrigation led to 0.043 kg CO2e per tree and in the max scenario 0.087 kg CO2e per tree. This is significantly lower than the 0.51 kg CO2e associated with each Acer rubrum ‘October Glory’ tree (Ingram, 2012). Similarly high values are reported by Ingram and Hall (2013), for the production of Cercis canadensis ‘Forest Pansy’ (0.68 kg CO2e per tree). In the same study Acer rubrum ‘October Glory’ was watered weekly, and Cercis canadensis ‘Forest Pansy’ was watered 48 times. None of our interview partners mentioned watering frequencies and amounts this high. Since production also aims at producing robust trees, they are not watered at a maximum according to our interview partners. Another reason for the different irrigation needs are regional climatic differences (Mehta et al., 2024; Bernhardt et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). 4.2.3 Process analysis during urban site stage Our LCI of Tilia cordata, Corylus colurna and Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ showed, that the processes associated with the most emissions at the urban site were pest management, pruning, and irrigation. Currently, oak processionary moth has to be treated in our case study region, as it poses a health risk to the inhabitants, and the damage of foliage eaten by caterpillars impact the trees’ ability to withstand other stressors (Tomlinson et al., 2015; Arnold et al., 2023). Other pest management is currently not common, but may become more relevant in the future. For example, Kim and Jo (2022) reported that pest management is performed on 40% of the trees in South Korea. In Germany, horse-chestnut leaf miner, ash dieback and oak processionary moth are major problems in urban tree management (Raum et al., 2024), but only oak processionary moth is regularly targeted. However, globalized trade and climate change are increasing the risk of new pest infestation in urban trees (Tomlinson et al., 2015; Raum et al., 2023; Paletto et al., 2025), and, for the near future, Xylella fastidiosa, Phytophthora spp., box tree moth, emerald ash borer, Asian long-horned beetle, and the Japanese beetle may become relevant in Europe (Paletto et al., 2025). The identification and management require the green space agencies to adapt alongside these new threats. Paletto et al. (2025) evaluated that ways to prevent or manage tree pests could be to create a public awareness, staff training on tree health, and regular visual inspections and laboratory analysis. Trained staff and visual inspections, could help to detect pest outbreaks early and limit the pest management actions and therefore also the CO2 emissions. We were unable to identify studies that have conducted a comparable evaluation of a LCI of an urban pest management. However, we stress the importance of including pest management in LCIs. The selection and use of equipment and vehicles has a large impact on CO2e emissions and it can result in up to a third of the total CO2e emission for the pruning of trees (McPherson et al., 2015). In urban areas, the pruning safety preparation emitted between 8.5 and 27.9 kg CO2e per tree and the pruning itself emitted between 43.6 and 131.1 kg CO2e (McPherson et al., 2015). These values align with our values, which are 33.74, 84.35, or 168.7 kg CO2e per tree for the min, mean, and max scenario, respectively. McPherson et al. (2015) also showed that the vehicle used for the tree health control can become a large emitter of up to 14.5 kg CO2e per tree. In our case, we did not find emissions to be quite as high (3.21 kg CO2e per tree in the max scenario). One of the municipalities in our study even used e-bikes to conduct the tree health controls. However, tree management (such as pest management and pruning) uses a variety of heavy-duty vehicles. Ways to decrease the emissions during the arboricultural practice are optimizing routes and using scheduling software, speed limits, and driver training (Luck et al., 2014). McPherson et al. (2015) calculated water requirements of 80.9 to 131.4 L for the Platanus racemosa, which caused 10.6% to 17.2% of the CO2e emissions. This is very similar to our findings, where the irrigation added 12.9% to 17.0% of the total emissions. In the cities we studied, irrigation is planned through a rough schedule rather than based on a precise water requirement measurement. Optimizing irrigation by integrating soil moisture sensors, estimating the water use through weather data or modelling of whole-tree transpiration could help reduce CO2e emission. In order to make informed decisions on the path to climate-neutral cities, it is recommended that existing data on urban management practices be expanded (Antenucci et al., 2025) and it would be beneficial to track species-specific and site-specific management frequencies and durations. Particularly irrigation, pruning, pest management, felling practices, and covered distances or routes. 4.2.4 Growth at urban sites Finally, growth rates of the trees have a strong influence on the overall footprint. This influence is non-linear, because of the exponential nature of the allometric equation. Hence, in the case of Tilia cordata, the 19% increase from minimum to mean growth (0.56 cm/a to 0.69 cm/a) increases the stored CO2 by 38%, while the 23% increase from mean to maximum growth (0.69 cm/a to 0.84 cm/a) increase the stored CO2 by 57%. In order to get a realistic impression of growth, we used cadaster data from one of our focal cities, but tree growth is highly variable within cities (Moser-Reischl et al., 2021) and even within streets (Otto, 2024). Adding to the complexity, climate change has likely led to accelerated growth of urban trees in cities worldwide (Pretzsch et al., 2017), so even if past growth is accurately reflected in growth rates, they may not accurately reflect future growth. Taking all this into consideration, growth rates must be understood as a realistic scenario and not as a precise prediction. They are, however, comparable to what others have observed. In Leipzig, Germany, comparable growth rates for mean growth for the standard from Quercus robur (0.7 cm/a) and Tilia cordata (0.61 cm/a) were found (Strohbach et al., 2012). Using tree-rings, Moser et al. (2017) found a growth rate of 0.5 cm/a for Tilia cordata that were between 35 and 49 years in age and growing at a public square in Munich, Germany. Minimum and maximum growth for Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’ was lower and higher in this study, respectively, than reported in Strohbach et al. (2012) but they were not totally out of bounds and similar growth rates that have been documented for standard from Quercus robur elsewhere in Braunschweig with 0.26–1.27 cm/a (Otto, 2024) and in the UK with 0.78–1.49 cm/a (Vaz Monteiro et al., 2017). Growth rates for urban Corylus colurna, recorded during the first 7 years after planting, were higher than the ones used in this study with approximately 1.2 cm/a (Day and Harris, 2008). While growth rates are subject to great uncertainty, providing trees with enough space and good growth conditions is key to healthy development and faster growth (Sanders et al., 2013). 4.3 Influence of transport distances In the study conducted by Lind et al. (2023) the distance between the nursery and the urban site exceeded our distance of 380 km, most likely due to longer distances to cover in Sweden. In the Swedish study, distances of 77, 496, and 1,136 km resulted in 3.24, 20.9, 47.8 kg CO2e per tree. These transportation emissions are far greater than our results, which, depending on the scenario, are 0.24, 1.6 and 3.1 kg CO2e per tree. This highlights the importance of optimizing transportation ways. Distance, however, is not the only factor to consider. Especially the size of the transported trees also is worth considering: in Germany, it is common that trees are transplanted to the urban site when they have a girth of 18 to 20 cm, while the trees in Lind et al. (2023) study have a girth of 30 to 35 cm and are therefore much heavier. 4.4 Influence of the lifespan of urban trees Consistent with other studies, we chose an urban growth period of 50 years (Strohbach et al., 2012; Lind et al., 2023; Manzini et al., 2025). However, data on actual standing times of urban trees are rare (Ma et al., 2021; Kianmehr et al., 2024). A global review found that the planting date was merely available in 8.8% of the tree inventory (Ma et al., 2021). Oftentimes trees are only taken into the tree inventory once they have been established. However, high emissions are created in the first years for the planting, pruning, and irrigation, and therefore, it would be worthwhile to investigate how reduced stand ages or elevated mortality rates influence the CO2 balance at the urban scale. Street trees have a higher mortality rate than non-street trees (Parhizgar et al., 2025). Creating favorable growing conditions is very important in order to maximize the carbon storage and sequestration in urban areas (Ariluoma et al., 2021). 5 Conclusion This study provides three scenarios for Tilia cordata, Corylus colurna and Quercus robur ‘Fastigiata’. These scenarios show that these urban trees can become a carbon sink, and within 26 years all three studied tree species reached net zero. After a 50-year growth period at the urban site, all trees sequestered between 823 and 2677.4 kg CO2. However, urban management was associated with high emissions. When subtracting emissions from sequestration, one tree stored between 347.2 and 2570.6 kg CO2. The urban stage had the highest impact on the CO2 balance for all three species. Particularly, pest management, pruning, and irrigation were processes with significant emissions. Pest management and pruning are associated with the health and safety of the inhabitants of the city, and there is no possibility to cut out those processes. Nonetheless, because pest management, pruning, felling, and irrigation make up such a large share of the CO2 balance, the routes, machine running times, and frequencies should be monitored and optimized. Using EVs with electricity from renewable energy would also positively influence emissions. While this may still not be a viable option for most heavy machinery, EVs or electric bikes could be used for some urban mobility and impacts on the CO2 balance should be assessed. Finally, selecting suitable species and varieties that are well suited for the local conditions, is a key conclusion from the analysis of our scenarios. Trees that grow well, live long and require little care are optimal. Species prone to infestation with pests that may cause human health issues and have to be treated, should be avoided in critical locations such as busy streets or school yards.
