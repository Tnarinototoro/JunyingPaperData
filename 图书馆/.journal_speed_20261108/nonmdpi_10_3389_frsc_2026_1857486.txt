GM Geoffrey Mukwada * Department of Geography, Faculty of Natural and Agricultural Science, University of Free State, Phuthaditjhaba, South Africa Article metrics View details Abstract The objective of this study is to determine how the current urban food systems in African cities can be transformed into sustainable urban food systems. The study contributes to the ongoing discourse on the role of urban agriculture in sustainable cities, which hitherto remains unresolved. This is despite the UN’s SDG2 which calls for attainment of “zero hunger” through the elimination of hunger, food security, nutrition improvement and promotion of sustainable agriculture, a rallying call for stakeholders hoping to improve food security in urban environments. Guided by critical realism and experiential knowledge acquired from participant observation and drawing from a literature review of case studies of African cities, the study investigates the gaps that exist in the sustainability of African urban food systems. The study interrogates the merits of the Green City Index, Global City Indicators Facility and the Global Compact Cities Circles of Sustainability as frameworks for assessing food insecurity in African cities. The results reveal the exclusion of food security in sustainability assessments, lack of detail on conditions characterizing the biophysical environment, as well as aspects of the environment, energy management and civic engagement as the main gaps in the frameworks used for assessing urban food systems. I argue that in order to ameliorate these gaps, Urban Ecological Infrastructure should be included in decisions on urban food systems and conclude that the traditional approach of exclusively focusing on food security and its elements fails to address the reality that urban residents have multiple competing needs apart from food. These competing needs include space for shelter, clean water and sanitation, health, and safety and security. I advocate the adoption of an integrated needs centred framework that meets both food security goals and protect the integrity of the Urban Ecological Infrastructure to provide a gateway to sustainable urban food systems. 1 Introduction Urban development policies should emphasize the importance of agricultural activity in raising awareness among city dwellers and society at large (Tabrez, 2025), hence the sustainability of urban food systems depends on these policies. In Sub-Saharan Africa, sustained urban food systems and food security are ongoing challenges among urban households (Blekking et al., 2020). Though urban farming has been extensively studied in developed nations, in developing countries, opportunities and challenges of urban farming have not yet been fully analysed (Pillai, 2025). Hence, the debate on the role of urban agriculture in the sustainable city discourse remains unresolved (Azunre et al., 2019). However, the discourse on African cities and their food requirements has been growing along many fronts due to rapid urbanization. Despite soaring urbanization, the promotion of food security has tended to receive a rural bias. For instance, Crush and Frayne (2011) note that exclusive attention has been given to rural hunger and rural smallholder farming, thus ignoring urban food systems. Even though some scholars caution that there has been limited evidence showing the contribution of urban agriculture to food and nutrition security (Frayne et al., 2014; Cilliers et al., 2020), the growing body of literature on this subject suggests otherwise. Both the spatial extent and population of African cities have been increasing, particularly during the last 50 years. In Sub-Saharan African cities, decolonization has given impetus to this growth, unlike during the colonial period where most indigenous people were confined to rural areas. Following independence, large numbers of people from rural areas flocked to the city, where they were attracted by better opportunities such as healthcare, education, and prospects for employment. In the global south in general, rapid urbanisation is now becoming a threat to the sustainability of agriculture within the urban periphery (Azunre et al., 2019). This has had two main effects, firstly the incorporation of peri-urban areas into the urban space economy, and secondly the displacement of agriculture in these areas by urban land uses, notably residential spaces and industry. The overall effect of these changes has undermined food and nutrition security in most African cities. Challenges of food insecurity are increasing due to rapid urbanization on the continent (Crush and Battersby, 2016; Tuholske et al., 2020). Unfortunately, many of the rural migrants fail to secure employment. As a survival strategy they open small fields in their residential areas, where they practicing urban farming. At first such practices were resisted by urban authorities, who continued to rely on the draconian top-down planning policies that were inherited from colonial administrators. The perpetuation of colonial policies and legislation does not augur well for urban agriculture. In South Africa, for instance, none of the legislation or policies that should promote agriculture directly supports urban farming. The Spatial Land Use Management Act and Policy on Agriculture in Sustainable Development do not urban agriculture (Cilliers et al., 2020). Consequently, there is limited policy support for urban agriculture. Similarly, in Kenya planning legislation is silent on food systems issues and does not adequately address urban food system issues (Battersby and Watson, 2020). In Zimbabwe, where some legislation was closely modelled on British planning law (Chigumira et al., 2020), planning laws such as the Municipal Act of 1930, Town Planning Act of 1933, Urban Councils Act of 1973, Regional, Town and Country Planning Act of 1976 do not support urban agriculture, but “pathologize” informal activities. In this study I focus on case studies showing the historical developments of urban agriculture in Sub-Saharan using selected cities. I first examine the reasons why urban agriculture has taken root in African cities and then address the role of Urban Ecological Infrastructure (UEI) in urban agriculture and food security, as well as the factors that influence the state of this infrastructure. Urban agriculture has been a major strategy towards improved food security for some major cities the world over and an additive form of economic development that promotes food security at household level (Chaminuka and Dube, 2017). Moyo (2013) notes that the adoption of urban agriculture has arisen from the reality that though urban dwellers often rely on markets for food, scarcity of food has led to a surge in food prices to levels beyond the affordability of most poor urbanites, who have turned to the intensification of urban farming as a coping strategy. also notes that it is the most vulnerable urban poor households, whose sources of incomes are often inadequate and unreliable who turn to urban agriculture. For instance, Moyo (2013) observed that urban agriculture and social security play a complementary role in providing food security in the slums of Kibera in Kenya, where the urban poor have been placed in a precarious position due to rapid urbanization. As characterized by recent literature, household livelihood strategies is a function of different forms of assets or capital types, including natural, social, economic, financial, physical, capital (Gallaher et al., 2013), whose role has been illustrated in copious publications on the sustainable livelihood framework. However, there is still room for assessing the role that each one of these forms of capital play in urban food security. Consequently, I focus the role that natural capital plays in food security in African cities. In doing so, I first examine the importance of Blue-Green UEI in sustainable household food systems, based on the case study of Harare, and then secondly highlight the gaps that exist in different urban sustainability frameworks reported in literature, as well as those that were revealed through my own experiential knowledge before discussing a normative framework that could plug these gaps. 2 Literature review 2.1 Nature of urban food systems The UN’s SDG2 calls for the attainment of “zero hunger” necessitates the elimination of hunger, food security, nutrition improvement and promotion of sustainable agriculture. This goal has since become the rallying call of the World Food Programme and related stakeholders, especially those within the NGO community. However, despite its noble efforts, this goal does not sufficiently acknowledge the reality of urban food insecurity (Battersby and Watson, 2020). Food security is defined as a situation whereby everyone, at all times, has physical and economic access to enough safe and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life (HLPE, 2020). According to HLPE (2020) food security comprises six interrelated elements, namely food availability, access, stability, utilization, agency and sustainability. Food utilization denotes nutrition while stability relates to food resilience. While these elements have been instrumental in shaping policy (Hendriks and Babu, 2024), the attainment of sustainable food security through urban agriculture has remained elusive. Yet, evidence from existing literature indicate that urban agriculture supports the economic, social and environmental sustainability of cities (Azunre et al., 2019), even though the importance of urban agriculture varies considerably between cities and countries in Africa (Crush et al., 2011). Urban agriculture offers numerous advantages, including food and security nutrition, healthy environment and decent livelihoods, employment generation, potential to increase the supply of fresh food to cities, support decent livelihoods, reduce waste, and create employment opportunities, among other benefits, and it has potential to reduce food loss and waste within the post-harvest phase of the food distribution chain (Amao, 2020; Cilliers et al., 2020). However, it is the urban vulnerable poor who pursue urban agriculture as a strategy for coping with their circumstances. Unlike rural communities, which can produce or obtain their own basic needs such as food, water, and shelter, the urban poor become increasingly dependent upon the cash economy to obtain these goods, rendering them more vulnerable (Gallaher et al., 2013). Nuances of practices that promote sustainability in urban areas have come in different hues and shades, including “urban greening, green infrastructure planning, nature-based solutions, and eco-engineering approaches” (Cilliers et al., 2020). The incorporation of Urban Green Spaces and Blue Green Infrastructure in urban planning facilitates urban cooling, efficient water management, and habitat creation, enhances overall urban environmental health, which is pivotal in advancing cities toward carbon neutrality, resilience, and long-term sustainability (Malik and Singh, 2026). Efficient water management is a critical issue in African cities due to their high vulnerability to climate change (Grasham et al., 2019). Urban food supply chain systems depict food flows between cities and their peri-urban hinterlands. These food supply chain systems involve different stakeholders, including both commercial and subsistence farmers, wholesalers, formal and informal retailers, who provide a network of consumers like households, fast foods, restaurants, hotels, and other outlets. One key element of the food supply chain that has generally been overlooked is informal traders. Battersby and Watson (2020) maintain that in African cities, where urban development issues such as poverty, unemployment, and poor health are predominant, informal traders meet the food needs of many poor urban households. One of the biggest threats to informal food traders results from formal trading food supply chains such as supermarkets, which economically undermine and spatially marginalized them. Skinner (2020, p. 104) observed that “despite greater supermarket penetration in many urban areas, informal food retailers – street and market traders, ‘spaza’, or small-scale shops – remain ubiquitous across the African continent”. With informal traders receiving greater attention, the importance of the informal food sector in the urban food system is gaining better recognition (Skinner, 2020). However, food systems are shaped by processes that characterize urban environments, including urbanization. 2.2 Urbanization in Africa While in 1950, only 14% of Africa’s population lived in urban areas, this figure has been growing steadily, and it is estimated that by 2030 50% of Africa’s population will be living in urban areas (Battersby and Watson, 2020). Even though there is no standardized approach to the definition of “urbanization”, this process is generally understood to refer to the increase of the proportion of the population living in urban areas, resulting from urban expansion (Battersby and Watson, 2020), which may result from rural-urban migration. Lack of economic opportunities in rural areas is the primary driver of urbanization. As highlighted below, in Africa, the rapid urbanization that is taking place has not been matched by food systems. 2.3 Sustainable food systems in African cities 2.3.1 The status-quo and existing frameworks sustainability assessment A close analysis of most of the literature on food security in African cities depicts food security as an integral component of the sustainability of these cities. Consequently, this literature is laden with nuances that ingratiate food availability, access, utilization and stability to the extent to which cities are sustainable. Azunre et al. (2019), for instance, has demonstrated the different approaches that have been adopted in assessing the sustainability of cities, including the Green City Index (GCI), Global City Indicators Facility (GCIF) and the Global Compact Cities Circles of Sustainability (GCCCS), each with its own set of indices by which sustainability can be measured. With its set of 30 indicators, which are grouped under 8 categories, the Green City Index (GCI) has been crowned by these authors as the most robust of sustainability assessment framework. The categories include environmental governance, carbon dioxide (CO2), buildings, transport, water, waste and land use, energy and air quality. Huang et al. (2015) maintain that the comprehensiveness of GCI model has led to its wide application across the globe, particularly in Europe, Asia, North America and Africa. Unlike the GCI, the GCIF attempts to cover all aspects of urban life and depicts urban sustainability as a concomitant of strong economic and social structures. It was developed by the World Bank, in conjunction with the Japanese Trust Fund (Azunre et al., 2019). However, a close inspection of GCI and GCIF indicates that both frameworks fail to integrate food systems, flora and fauna. Just like the GCI, the GCCCS has been widely adopted across the world and it has been applied in “Johannesburg, Melbourne, New Delhi, São Paulo, and Tehran by several organisations such as the UN Global Compact Cities Programme, World Vision, and Save the Children to understand and support sustainability efforts” (Azunre et al., 2019, p. 107). It consists of 28 indicators which fall into four categories, namely political, cultural, economic, and ecological categories. The GCCCS has a close resemblance to the GCI in terms of the considerations gives to health, education, gender, technology, infrastructure, waste, water and air quality related indicators. In addition to its robustness its other major strength is that indicators are used in a complementary manner. Consequently, actions or measures taken to improve one component of the environment will also contribute to improvements in other components of the environment. Azunre et al. (2019, p. 107) note, for instance that “actions targeted at reducing emissions and waste are more likely to improve the habitat, settlements, recreation, identity and organisation of an area”. The main pitfall of the GCCCS is its inability to address aspects of the environment, energy management and civic engagement in detail (Azunre et al., 2019). In summing up the main characteristics of the GCI, GCIF, and GCCCS models of urban sustainability assessment one notices some gaps that are evident in these frameworks. Based on the foregoing discussion, it can be concluded that the GCI and GCIF do not give food security sufficient attention, even though the latter attempts to address all aspects of urban life. On the other hand, the GCCCS exhibits weaknesses in its limited scope and detail given to ecological issues. The question that comes to the fore is: How can these gaps be addressed and what analytical framework can be used to achieve this goal? I acknowledge that this is a normative question. It will be addressed in the discussion section of this paper. The remaining part of this paper focuses on the gap linked to this question. 2.3.2 Current state of urban food systems What is the current state of urban food systems? To address this question, I evoke the concept of Urban Ecological Infrastructure (UEI) to highlight the gaps revealed in existing urban sustainability frameworks that were discussed earlier and their failure to create sustainable urban food systems in African cities. UEI as a concept that emerged from UNESCO’s Man and Biosphere (MAB) Project. Li et al. (2017, p. 513) defines UEI as: the organic integration of blue (water-based), green (vegetated), and grey (non-living) landscapes, combined with “exits” (outflows, treatment, or recycling) and “arteries” (corridors) at an ecosystem scale. The UEI plays a fundamental role in sustainably providing the ecosystem services that urban residents require. UEIs include a comprehensive set of functions: water supply and recycling, energy supply and heat dissipation, soil fertility and biodiversity, metabolism or breakdown of materials and regeneration of wastes, runoff reduction and nutrient retention, and the provision of recreational, educational, and cultural services. The purpose of UEI development is to improve a city's ecological integrity and sustainability. There is potential for urban agriculture to function as micro-scale environmental infrastructures, by integrating food production with waste reuse, water management, and microclimate regulation (Tisnawati and Aufelisa, 2026). In line with the above definition, I argue that the nature of UEI available in the African city and how this infrastructure is governed, managed and used determines the extent to which the urban food systems can meet residents’ food requirements. In this vein, I argue that the urban area is a system, whose conditions must be monitored and managed in a manner that ensures food availability, access, utilization, stability and sustainability. In order to attain food security, the city must pursue policies and practices that protect the environment and its attributes, including its UEI. Availability of biophysical resources in the urban and peri-urban environment, access to green and grey infrastructure and proximity to large markets are the main drivers of the current urban food systems. Unfortunately, these conditions often contribute to the degradation of urban environments in several ways. For instance, poor management of the grey landscapes promotes environmental degradation, soil erosion, loss of land’s fertility and ultimately low land productivity and reduced food availability, access, nutrition and resilience. Consequently, poor management of grey landscapes contributes to loss of agricultural revenue for urban and peri-urban farmers. Similarly, the pollution of the grey landscapes through poor sewage disposal can lead to unavailability of clean water and contamination of agricultural produce (Crowder and Reganold, 2015). Likewise, if green infrastructure such as vegetation is not well managed the result will be accelerated soil erosion and accumulation of waste in the urban and peri-urban ecosystem, leading to unavailability of clean water for irrigation. Azunre et al. (2019) cite studies which point to the adverse effects of urban agriculture on the health of the cities due to excessive use of agro-chemicals or untreated water sources. If adequately treated, wastewater can be used to boost food production in the peri-urban environment. Unfortunately, the mindset that prevails in most African citie is that the peri-urban environment should be used as a “sink” for waste and the source of gravel and sand for building materials. The peri-urban area is where landfills and sewage works are often situated. These activities and their impacts are increasing due to urban sprawl (Mishra, 2025). Some impacts are indirect. For instance, the development of thermal islands and occurrence of acid rain, which result from atmospheric pollution can affect cropping and access to clean food. Similarly, overuse of water by urban residents reduces water availability for agriculture. However, the fact that agriculture is a major contributor to greenhouse gases, biodiversity loss, agro-chemical pollution, and soil degradation (Crowder and Reganold, 2015) should not deter cities from practicing it. This means that agriculture should be practiced in a way that minimizes environmental degradation. Thus, cities in Africa need to explore opportunities for practicing smart agriculture and transform themselves into smart cities, i.e., environmentally friendly cities. Cilliers et al. (2020) note that environmental considerations are now an integral part of international spatial planning approaches and decision-making in urban environments. They maintain that urban green spaces are substantially beneficial to urban communities. In line with this argument, I suggest that the most beneficial feasible way of greening the city is through the adoption of smart agriculture, which in turn promotes urban food security. 3 Materials and methods This study is based on critical realism and experiential knowledge drawn from participant observation and reviewed literature on case studies of African cities to investigate the gaps that exist in the sustainability of African urban food systems. Thus, this study is qualitative and guided by a number of questions about the state of urban farming systems in Africa, as well as formative questions about how these systems ought to be, based on both revelations from literature and personal reflections derived from my experiences as an emerging urban farmer. I have been an informal small-scale urban farmer for more than 20 years. Critical realism addresses the ‘what’ questions and provides initial strategies for answering the ‘why’ questions, thus providing exploratory knowledge linked to the existing reality (Archer et al., 1999). An important question guiding my reflections on how urban food systems ought to be shaped relates to prospects for transforming existing food systems into the more desirable sustainable food systems, thereby meeting the needs of both the environment and society. 4 Results 4.1 Importance of Blue-Green UEI in sustainable household food systems: the case of policy, practices and experiences from Harare A key question that arises regarding urban food systems is about whether it is possible to transform the current urban food systems into sustainable urban food systems. To address this question, I draw from personal experiences that highlight how UEI can be used to achieve this transformation. The past two decades have seen the relaxation of policies in urban agriculture in Zimbabwe. In their bid to remain popular with urban residents in an environment characterized by economic decline urban authorities in Zimbabwe no longer openly prohibit the use of open spaces for farming. Urban farming has become one of the major sources of livelihood in Zimbabwe. The failure by urban authorities to enforce their own by-laws in an environment characterized by conflicting demands between urban authorities and the needs of residents, and urban residents’ drive towards self-food reliance has witnessed a boon in urban agriculture, thus highlighting the importance UEI in urban food systems. In my experiences as an urban farmer during the past 20 years, I have discovered that there is an inseparable link between Blue and Green UEI. These two entities are interlocked and interdependent. Because of its tropical environment, Zimbabwe experiences its rainy season in summer, roughly extending from end of October to April. For the majority of urban residents urban farming is rainfed, hence it can only be practiced during this period. However, my farming operations rely on both rainfed and irrigation based agricultural practices. The link between Blue and Green UEI is that both trees and crops play an important role in reducing surface runoff and in enhancing infiltration and percolation, thus promoting groundwater recharge. Groundwater replenishment is an indirect way of harvesting water in the environment. Unlike the conventional way of collecting water and storing it, UEI provides ecosystem-based solution of water storage through natural processes. Green UEI acts as a conduit for groundwater storage. To access this water, I drilled a borehole which supplies water to my farming operations throughout the year, including the dry season and during dry spells. Besides promoting water seepage, the trees and crops that I grow serve as one of my major sources of food, though they also play many other purposes as illustrated in Table 1. Hitherto, I have not been selling the surplus food because my primary goal was to experiment with urban farming as an adaptation to climate change from an academic perspective, thus translating theory into practice. 5 Discussion 5.1 Sustainable urban food systems In this section I focus on the normative question: “What do urban food systems ought to be in order to be sustainable?” This question calls for food system transformation, a process that is rapidly gaining attention the global policy discourse (Sonnino, 2023). As noted in Section 2, the existing urban sustainability frameworks are fraught with gaps. The case study of Harare highlights how these gaps can be closed by incorporating UEI into urban planning to create sustainable urban food systems that are needs driven. Since needs are not confined to food alone, but also include the need for a safe environment, needs should be allowed to shape policies, including those that protect the urban environment. While most literature paints a picture that urban ordinances and planning laws are draconian or oppressive (Chigumira et al., 2020), and tend to limit food production availability, access, nutrition, and stability, it ought to be mentioned that apart from safe chemical free nutritious food urban residents need liveable spaces on which to build decent homes. These spaces are dwindling due to urban sprawl and degradation through poorly practiced urban agriculture and other land-use practices. Water supply in African cities is increasingly becoming scarcer, yet it is one of the most basic needs of every human being. In West Africa, some urban farmers already rely on contaminated water sources such as wastewater due to shortage of clean water (Bougnom et al., 2019). In most African urban areas, there are competing uses for water, including mining, construction, and sewage disposal. Urban agriculture is adding to this toll of degradation. Against this background, it is necessary to ensure that the entire urban food system is environmentally friendly so that it does not aggravate degradation. The surest way of doing so is to develop and use UEI in an integrated way. This can be achieved by promoting smart urban agriculture, through sustainable practices that maintain the Blue-Green UEI that supports urban farming and environmental protection. Based on the existing body of knowledge and experiences gained from my participant observation in urban farming in Harare I propose a framework which could be used to analyze the relationship between UEI and urban food systems. This framework is summarized in Figure 5. As illustrated along the right flank of Figure 5, the framework starts with human needs, including those linked to environmental integrity, as opposed to the traditional approach of managing urban food systems which is supported by most scholars, where food systems are demand driven, as illustrated on the left flank of Figure 5. The argument pursued in most of this scholarly work is that there must be promotion of urban agriculture, regardless of its environmental costs, in order to attain urban food security. Such an approach needs reconsideration. As opposed to this traditional approach, a sustainable approach would start with the needs of urban residents (both for food and the need to live in a safe environment) and strengthen the policies that support these needs, both formally and informally. Such policies will ensure stable availability and accessibility to nutritional needs while promoting environmental protection, as illustrated by the right flank of Figure 5. The integrity of the entire environment, including its biophysical, socio-economic and political components must be protected through effective policy systems that meet both environmental and food needs, at both formal and informal levels. The availability, accessibility, utilization, stability, and sustainability of food can be achieved both formally and informally. The models I experimented with Figures 1–4 are all informal and small-scale. They offer different advantages, depending on household needs, food requirements, and preferences. Most informal food systems involve small-scale farming operations like my own and produce food for subsistence purposes, though they sell surpluses to informal traders such as vendors. Unlike informal food systems, formal food systems include commercial food producers such as urban and peri-urban farms that feed into food chain supply systems like supermarkets and other food outlets. For urban food systems to be sustainable there is need to integrate Brown UEI and Blue-Green UEI. In this context, Brown infrastructure refers to the infrastructure that is introduced by humans, including transport networks, energy transmission systems, buildings, perimeter walls. As discussed below, this integration is associated with both opportunities and challenges. Figure 5 Framework for analyzing the relationship between Urban Ecological Infrastructure and food systems. 5.2 Opportunities and challenges in building sustainable urban food systems Based on my experiences as an urban farmer the integrated approach summarized in Figure 5, coupling food and environmental systems through use of UEI, illustrates how urban environments should be managed. There are opportunities and challenges that should be considered where UEI is an integral component of urban food systems. These are summarized as follows. 5.2.1 Opportunities Integration gives priority to both human and environmental needs, as well as the interaction between them. Household food supply is ensured throughout the year where a diverse mix of plants (and animals too) are raised under irrigation. Improved food supply could reduce malnutrition and child mortality. Development of a circular economy where products from different parts of urban food systems are used in others. For instance, food remains and trimmings from plants can be composted to enrich the soil. Biodiversity conservation is enhanced through the habitats that are created by urban farming. Labour can be supplied at household level, thus reducing unemployment and enhancing physical wellbeing through drudgery. Unemployment is a crisis in Africa (Giller, 2020). Some poor urban residents rely on informal employment to earn a living (Chirisa et al., 2022). The carbon footprint is reduced through carbon sequestration. For instance, planting C4 grasses such as sugar cane can promote carbon sequestration. Promotion of water conservation through reduced surface runoff and conveyance of water into underground storage where its exposure to evaporation is limited. This is particularly important in an environment where global warming and the development of thermal islands are worsening. Thus, opportunities for climate change mitigation are enhanced. Reduced runoff reduces flood risk in the urban environment. Potential for generating income from urban farming (Bisaga et al., 2019), even though my agricultural produce has never been sold. 5.2.2 Challenges Blue-Green UEI interferes with Brown UEI such as buildings, perimeter walls, drainage systems, and energy transmission systems such as electric powerlines. Trees fall over electricity mains while their roots pry into drainage pipes, perimeter walls, and buildings. Individuals responsible for damaging this public infrastructure are often face prohibitive fines. If not carefully managed, fruit trees and composts can be a breeding grounds for rodents and disease vectors such as flies and mosquitoes. In a tropical country such as Zimbabwe, where temperatures are already high the proliferation of these vectors can be disastrous because it contributes to the spread of diarrhoeal diseases and malaria, as climate change worsens. Lack of access to land can limit the productivity of urban farming systems. Teoh et al. (2024) have made similar observations and highlighted challenges of shortage of land as a threat to urban farming in densely populated areas. Urban sprawl is a common problem in African cities, where it has led to critical shortage of land to the extent that open spaces and even cemeteries are being converted into dwelling places (Baeribameng et al., 2020). Proliferation of rodents changes the dynamics of the urban environment and the invasion of homes by predators such as snakes, hawks, and owls, which may pose a threat to small livestock such as rabbits, ducks, and chickens. Where the farmer depends on organic fertilizers sourced from rural farms, for instance manure from free ranging livestock farms, urban farming can introduce new types of pests and weeds into the urban environment. Some weeds are invasive. In one instance I had to deal with Lantana camara, an invasive species which got established on my plot following the application of livestock manure from a rural livestock farm. Small-scale urban farming is labour intensive. While it reduces unemployment it requires investment of time in hoeing, planting, weeding, pruning, and harvesting. Finding storage and markets for products is another challenge, especially where excess amounts of food are produced. Disruption and damage by pets, particularly dogs, which dig or trample on crops or prey on small livestock. Some plant species attract pests. An example is Azanza garckeana, which attracts aphids. Pests and diseases pose a great challenge in urban farming systems where crops are grown close to each other (Teoh et al., 2024). Trees and crops such as bananas, maize, and sugarcane compromise security due to their interference with visibility. This might worsen crime in the urban environment. As demonstrated in the foregoing discussion, urban agriculture promotes food and nutrition security (Cilliers et al., 2020). However, it is evident that the traditional approach of addressing food systems and ecological concerns as separate domains cannot contribute to sustainable food systems in urban environments. The integration of these domains is necessary because food systems are the core of current environmental, social, and economic global challenges, as well as multiple problems such as malnutrition, hunger resource scarcity, social inequalities, climate change, ecosystem degradation and loss of biodiversity loss (Zou et al., 2023). Sustainable food systems could reduce child mortality in African cities. Child nutrition is considerably high in Africa, where it accounts for more than 50% of child mortality (Drammeh et al., 2019). Based on the needs summarized in Table 1, a close analysis of the models that I have experimented with reveals that the most suitable urban farming model is the Agri-silvicultural model. This offers the biggest range of opportunities to households that are engaged in urban farming. Thus, urban farming could address the urban food conundrum if the right policies are put in place to tackle food access barriers in a sustainable manner. I concur with the view that urban planners and policymakers need to “revisit how decisions are made about issues such as residential development, land tenure, transport infrastructure, and the use of space in cities, as these affect the ability of households to produce, sell, and access food” (Davies et al., 2021, p. 2). Since agriculture and food security are vulnerable to climate change (Berhanu and Wolde, 2019) the value of Blue-Green UEI in food systems will become more critical as the impacts of climate change worsen. The integration of Blue-Green UEI into food systems is the missing link that could bring sustainability to food systems in African cities. 6 Conclusion The objective of this study was to determine how the current urban systems in African cities can be transformed into sustainable urban food systems. Different frameworks have been proposed regarding the transformation of urban food systems into sustainable systems. Based on the existing literature on urban food systems in Africa, it can be concluded that UEI plays a critical role in shaping food systems in African cities. The most resilient urban food systems are those that integrate ecological, social and political needs. Such systems will improve the governance of both UEI and food systems. It can also be concluded that the incorporation of UEI in food systems could enhance environmental integrity and the quality of food systems. Policy systems in urban environments should support the incorporation of UEI in urban planning and development due the multiple benefits that they offer to urban residents. Finally, the study concludes that the traditional approach of exclusively focusing on food security and its elements fails to address the reality that urban residents have multiple competing needs apart from food. These competing needs include space for shelter, clean water and sanitation, health, and safety and security. The study advocates the adoption of an integrated need centred focus and recommends the pursuit of food security goals that protect the integrity of the UEI as a gateway to meeting the entirety of human needs. 6.1 Limitations of study This study is a qualitative case study based that relied heavily on the researcher’s experiences as a participant observer. Therefore, it was not easy to draw generalizations from its findings, even though comparisons were made between its findings and those from previous studies. Also, it was not possible to quantify the environmental impacts of the farming models that the researcher experimented with, including the role of plants in biogeochemical cycles and carbon sequestration.
