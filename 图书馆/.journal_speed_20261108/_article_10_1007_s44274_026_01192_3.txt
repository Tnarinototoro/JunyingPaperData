Although the technical potential for valorizing pineapple waste is documented globally, no study has assessed waste flows at the actor level across the entire chain in this region. This study quantifies and characterizes pineapple waste from production on Idjwi Island to collection, processing, retail and consumption in Bukavu. Using purposive sampling and snowball techniques, 110 respondents across five actor categories were surveyed: 30 producers on Idjwi and 20 actors each (collectors, sellers, processors, consumers) in Bukavu. Semi-structured interviews covered production practices, waste management, transport, processing and perceptions. Waste fractions from five-fruit batches per site were weighed and post-harvest losses were quantified by counting and weighing damaged fruits. Results indicated an average yield of 38.9 t ha⁻¹, with 2.2-3 tons of vegetative biomass generated per ton of pineapple produced. Overall, 32.8% of total fruit weight (≈ 0.46 kg per fruit) became waste. Losses were highest at processing (37.7%) and retail (36.2%), compared with production (24.9%). The principal waste fractions were peels (33%), leaves (21.5%) and damaged fruits (17.3%). Most actors (70.8%) reported limited knowledge of valorization technologies and fewer than 10% applied them. Dominant disposal practices included informal sale (27%), open dumping (19%) and burning (8.5%). Over 60% of respondents expressed interest in training. This study provides the first actor-level quantification of pineapple waste flows in South Kivu, revealing that losses concentrate at processing and retail stages. The main barrier to valorization is not technical feasibility but the lack of training, equipment and market structures, underscoring the need for a participatory, integrated circular bioeconomy approach. Fruiting Olericulture Waste Management/Waste Technology Food Production Subsistence Agriculture Crop waste Valorization of Pineapple By-Products for Bioactive Compound Extraction 1 Introduction The pineapple value chain plays an increasingly important role in the fruit sector of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), with national production estimated at approximately 0.19 million tons in 2022 [ 1 , 2 ]. This growth reflects a broader trend across sub-Saharan Africa, where pineapple has become one of the most widely cultivated tropical fruits, driven by rising urban demand and its adaptability to smallholder farming systems [ 3 ]. Within the DRC, South Kivu Province, located in the eastern part of the country, stands out as one of the main production basins due to its favorable agro-ecological conditions. Agriculture forms the backbone of livelihoods in this province, with the majority of the population relying on farming activities for subsistence and income. In South Kivu, pineapple cultivation often intercropped with other food crops represents a key source of income for thousands of smallholder farming households [ 3 ]. Within South Kivu, Idjwi Island has emerged as a particularly important production zone, where pedoclimatic conditions including fertile volcanic soils, well-distributed rainfall and moderate temperatures are highly conducive to pineapple cultivation [ 4 , 5 ]. This has conferred a reputation for high-quality pineapples from Idjwi, with yields often exceeding the national average and fruit characterized by higher sugar content and pronounced flavor [ 5 , 6 ]. Bukavu (South Kivu) and Goma (North Kivu) constitute the major urban markets for this production, with dense concentrations of consumers and numerous intermediaries involved in collection, retail and distribution. Small-scale processing units producing pineapple juice also operate in these urban centers [ 5 , 6 , 7 ]. Despite this dynamic market structure, the value chain remains largely informal and weakly integrated, with limited coordination among actors and minimal investment in post-harvest infrastructure [ 3 , 7 ]. A striking paradox characterizes the pineapple sector: while the fruit generates substantial economic value, a large portion of the plant including leaves, stems, roots, and peels remains an under-utilized agricultural residue [ 8 ]. Post-harvest waste from pineapple production has been estimated at between 90 and 150 t ha⁻¹ in comparable tropical contexts, reflecting the enormous volume of residues generated [ 9 ]. These residues predominantly consist of lignocellulosic biomass with high moisture content and variable biochemical constituents, indicating significant potential for value-added utilization [ 10 ]. Integrated biorefinery studies suggest that from one ton of pineapple waste, it may be possible to extract high-value products such as bromelain and fermentable sugars, underscoring an untapped opportunity for circular bioeconomy development [ 11 ]. Furthermore [ 12 ], demonstrated that these by-products can be incorporated into animal feed thanks to their crude fiber, protein and sugar content, or converted into bioenergy and biomaterials in accordance with circular economy principles. Collectively, these studies converge on the conclusion that pineapple residues represent a valuable resource rather than a waste stream yet this potential remains largely unrealized in most producing regions. From a circular-economy perspective, these residues should therefore be considered potential secondary resources rather than materials destined solely for disposal. Circular economy approaches seek to reduce resource losses and maintain materials in productive use through strategies such as reuse, recycling and recovery [ 13 ]. In agri-food systems, this translates into recovering nutrients, biomass and other valuable compounds from residues and reintegrating them into productive activities, thereby reducing waste while creating additional economic and environmental value [ 14 ]. For pineapple, such approaches include direct reuse as animal feed or soil amendments, composting, extraction of bioactive compounds, and conversion into bioenergy, chemicals and biomaterials [ 15 ]. However, empirical studies indicate that the existence of technically feasible valorization pathways does not necessarily result in their adoption. Pineapple-waste valorization remains constrained by issues such as collection and transportation, processing costs, market linkages, technological requirements and the limited techno-economic evidence available for scaling up many proposed biorefinery processes [ 15 ]. More broadly, agricultural-residue valorization requires coordination among producers, collectors, processors and end users, as well as appropriate infrastructure and institutional support [ 16 ]. Thus, assessing circular-economy opportunities requires not only characterization of the biomass resource but also an understanding of where residues are generated, how they are currently managed, who handles them and what constraints influence their recovery and reuse. The gap between the demonstrated potential of pineapple residues and their actual valorization is particularly pronounced in sub-Saharan Africa. In South Kivu, as in other tropical parts of the region, smallholder farmers’ management of crop residues is often informal, poorly documented and based on traditional practices using local varieties. Research in the region shows that staple crop residues are frequently left unmanaged, used as fodder or compost or burned and that farmers face significant challenges in identifying and adopting profitable valorization options due to weak organization and limited access to technologies and markets [ 17 ]. This lack of organization is not merely a technical constraint but a systemic one: it impedes the identification and exploitation of valorization opportunities and generates environmental externalities such as uncontrolled dumping and greenhouse gas emissions [ 18 ]. Consequently, the valorization of these biomass resources remains limited, resulting in lost economic potential, intensified local environmental impacts and missed opportunities for local value creation. While several studies have documented the technical potential of pineapple waste valorization [ 10 , 11 , 12 ] and others have described the socio-economic constraints facing smallholder farmers in the region [ 13 , 14 ], few have systematically connected these two dimensions by quantifying actual waste flows along the entire value chain and linking them to actor-specific management practices and perceptions. In South Kivu specifically, no study to date has provided a comprehensive assessment of pineapple waste generation, management and valorization potential across all stages of the value chain from production on Idjwi Island to consumption and processing in Bukavu. The present study addresses this gap by systematically assessing pineapple waste in South Kivu with four specific objectives: (i) to quantify and characterize waste flows at each stage of the value chain; (ii) to analyze current management practices and their environmental impacts; (iii) to identify the constraints and perceptions of different actors regarding waste valorization and (iv) to propose locally appropriate avenues for waste valorization within a circular bioeconomy framework. 2 Methodology 2.1 Ethical approval and plant materials collection permissions Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the relevant Ethics Committee of the Université Évangélique en Afrique, South Kivu, Democratic Republic of Congo. The study involved human participants (stakeholders) and all procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical guidelines for social and agricultural research. The collection of plant materials used in this study complied with local and national regulations governing biodiversity conservation and ethnobotanical research in the Democratic Republic of Congo. The necessary permissions for plant collection were obtained from local authorities and community leaders in the study areas. The investigated plant (Ananas comosus) species were not classified as protected or endangered species. 2.2 Study area The study was conducted in two key areas of the pineapple industry in South Kivu, namely the territory of Idjwi and the city of Bukavu. Fig. 1 Full size image Study area The humid tropical climate of Idjwi Island, characterized by average annual rainfall ranging between 1,500 and 1,800 mm and average temperatures varying from 18 to 26 °C, creates favorable agroecological conditions for pineapple cultivation. However, these climatic conditions also accelerate the decomposition of organic residues in the absence of adequate soil and waste management systems, leading to nutrient loss and soil degradation if not properly managed [ 19 ]. Located at approximately 1°57′ South latitude and 29°00′ East longitude, at an average altitude of about 1,460 m above sea level, Idjwi benefits from fertile volcanic soils but remains vulnerable to erosion and unsustainable agricultural practices [ 20 ]. The study specifically targeted pineapple producers from the groupements of Mpene (2°05’19.07"S, 29°03’12.81"E; 1624 m altitude), Mugote (2°12’38.84"S, 29°05’20.11"E; 1536 m altitude) and Nyakalengwa (2°15’21.00"S, 29°02’26.02"E; 1586 m altitude), which are among the main pineapple-producing areas of Idjwi Territory. The city of Bukavu was selected to represent the main urban node of pineapple collection, consumption, processing, and commercialization, thus playing a central role in linking island producers to urban consumers and external markets. In Bukavu, Beach Muhanzi (2°29’31.12"S, 28°50’56.20"E; 1469.16 m altitude) was identified as the principal wholesale purchasing point where collectors, traders and other actors in the pineapple value chain obtain their supplies, while Kamagema (2°32’42.98"S, 28°51’53.26"E; 1566.118 m altitude) constituted one of the major retail sale points. In addition, consumers and processors were interviewed directly at their respective households in different communes of Bukavu. 2.3 Sampling approach and sample size determination Five categories of actors were targeted: producers (located on Idjwi Island), and collectors, processors, sellers, and consumers (operating in Bukavu city). Each category occupies a strategic position in the value chain and contributes, to varying degrees, to the generation and management of pineapple waste, while also representing a potential entry point for recovery interventions. A non-probabilistic sampling design combining purposive selection and the snowball technique was adopted [ 21 ]. This approach was chosen because no comprehensive sampling frame of pineapple value chain actors was available in the study area, particularly given the informal nature of some activities. Purposive sampling was used to identify actors directly involved in pineapple production, collection, processing, marketing, and consumption, while snowball sampling facilitated the identification of additional participants, particularly among less easily identifiable actor categories such as collectors and processors. The approach also facilitated access to actors distributed across the study area under the logistical constraints of the survey. However, because participants were not selected through a probability-based procedure, the sample may be subject to selection and network-related biases, limiting the statistical representativeness and generalizability of the findings to the wider population of pineapple value chain actors [ 22 ]. The process began with a small number of purposively selected initial respondents, identified with the assistance of local agricultural technicians and community leaders. These respondents then referred other relevant actors who met the study eligibility criteria, and recruitment continued through successive waves until informational saturation was reached, defined as the point at which additional interviews no longer generated new categories of pineapple waste or waste management practices. The final sample comprised 110 respondents, including 30 producers from Idjwi Island and 80 actors from the other four categories, namely collectors, processors, sellers, and consumers, in Bukavu City. The snowball recruitment process was described using the general snowball sampling relationship, according to which the theoretical number of participants after k recruitment waves depends on the initial number of respondents, the average number of participants recruited by each respondent, and the number of recruitment waves. $$\:{n}_{x}={\sum\:}_{i=0}^{k}{s}^{i}=\frac{1-{s}^{k+1}}{1-s}$$ In this relationship, nₓ represents the theoretical sample size for actor category x, s represents the average number of participants recruited by each respondent, and k represents the number of recruitment waves. In the present study, the average number of recruits per respondent was approximately two, and recruitment generally extended over four waves for most actor categories. This relationship was used to characterize the expected expansion of the sample through successive recruitment waves, while the final sample size was determined by the recruitment process and informational saturation rather than by a probability-based sample size calculation. For producers, the 30 respondents were distributed proportionally across the three main pineapple-producing groupements of Idjwi, namely Mpene, Mugote, and Nyakalengwa, according to their relative importance in local pineapple production. This sampling approach has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, snowball sampling is inherently subject to selection bias: respondents recruited through referral chains tend to share similar socio-economic characteristics, production practices, and market networks, which may under-represent marginal or isolated actors. Second, the geographic concentration of producers on Idjwi Island (30 out of 110 respondents) reflects the actual spatial distribution of pineapple production in South Kivu, but limits the generalization of findings to other territories of the province. Third, data on waste management practices and perceptions were largely self-reported, which may introduce social desirability bias; this was partially mitigated by direct field observations at production, processing and marketing sites. Fourth, the non-probabilistic nature of the design means that the sample cannot be considered statistically representative of the entire population of pineapple value chain actors in South Kivu. These limitations notwithstanding, the combination of purposive selection and snowball sampling made it possible to obtain a diverse and experienced group of respondents, ensuring the relevance and robustness of the data collected [ 22 , 23 ]. Future studies could build on this work by establishing a formal sampling frame through actor registration or cooperative lists, thereby enabling probabilistic sampling and more robust statistical inference. 2.4 Collection of plant materials Plant materials used in this study were collected from pineapple farms and surrounding agroecosystems located in Idjwi Territory, South Kivu Province, Democratic Republic of Congo (approximately 1°57′ S and 29°00′ E). Additional pineapple residues and plant materials were obtained from collection, processing, and marketing sites in Bukavu city (approximately 2°30′ S and 28°51′ E), particularly at Beach Muhanzi and Kamagema markets. The collection of plant materials complied with local and national guidelines governing biodiversity conservation, environmental protection, and agricultural research in the Democratic Republic of Congo. Prior authorization to access farms and collect plant materials was obtained from local administrative authorities, community leaders, and farm owners before field activities were conducted. The plant species collected during the study were not classified as protected, endemic, or endangered species according to available national conservation regulations. The collected plant materials were identified based on their local and scientific names with the assistance of local agricultural technicians and available botanical references. All plant materials were collected exclusively for scientific research purposes related to pineapple waste valorization and environmental sustainability. 2.5 Conduct of the survey Data collection was carried out using a questionnaire integrated into Kobo-collect application, which was used during semi-structured interviews with the different categories of stakeholders. The questions common to all stakeholder categories covered sociodemographic profiles, perceptions of waste management, related constraints, and the potential for economic and environmental value creation. This process was supplemented by direct observations at production, processing, and sales sites. At the same time, the pineapples were weighed using an HLE (High-precision Laboratory Electronics) scale, model SF-400 (< 1 g accuracy) in order to quantify the proportion of waste generated in relation to the weight of fruit handled by each category of stakeholder. At the production sites in Idjwi and at the markets (Bukavu), whole fruits were weighed first, followed by the parts considered waste (peels, stems, and crowns). For each batch of pineapples ( n = 5 per site), the total weight of the fruit and the waste was recorded. At the collectors’ premises, the proportion of fruit damaged during transport or storage was estimated by counting and directly weighing the damaged fruit in a random sample of batches sold (20 to 50 fruits per seller). For each producer, different types of waste were first identified according to the stage at which they appeared, from cultivation to harvest. The total waste generated (D-total) includes all vegetative biomass removed from the field or generated during cultivation and harvest, namely: leaves cut or fallen during cultivation, small or malformed fruits not harvested, diseased fruits or fruits damaged by pests, fruits dropped during handling at harvest, crowns, stems or cores removed during initial processing and roots or other underground parts when applicable. This total therefore represents the overall plant biomass generated per unit of fruit produced, rather than post-harvest fruit waste alone. Waste types were categorized and summed according to the formula. $$\:{D}_{total}={\sum\:}_{i}^{n}{D}_{j}$$ where D total is the total waste generated by the producer (t), n is the total number of waste types considered for the producer and D j is the amount of each waste type j. Yield per plot was calculated as the ratio of production to cultivated area. $$\:{Y}_{{i}_{\left(\frac{t}{ha}\right)}}=\frac{{Q}_{i}}{{S}_{i}}$$ where Y i is the yield of producer, Q i is the total fruit produced, and S i is the cultivated area of producer. The waste-to-production ratio at the farm level was then calculated to express the proportion of losses relative to total production. $$\:{R}_{d/p}=\frac{{D}_{total}}{{Y}_{i}*{S}_{i}}$$ This approach directly links the types of waste, actual production, and cultivated area, providing a comprehensive measure of farm-level losses. For other actors (collectors, processors, and sellers), the average proportion of waste considered as loss was estimated using. $$\:{P}_{i}=\frac{{W}_{i}}{{W}_{t}}$$ Where: P i represents the proportion (%) of waste type i (peel, crown, uneaten residue) for a given actor x, W i : the weight of the waste, and W t : the total weight of the fruit. For ethical reasons, the resource persons were informed of the objectives of the study and their verbal consent was obtained prior to the survey. The data was treated confidentially and used exclusively for research purposes. 2.6 Data analysis The collected data were analyzed using the R programming language (version 4.5.2) through RStudio. Descriptive statistics, including relative frequencies, means, and standard deviations, were used to characterize stakeholders according to their socio-demographic profiles, the types and quantities of waste generated, and waste management practices. Before inferential analyses, the assumptions underlying each statistical procedure were assessed. For quantitative variables analyzed using ANOVA, normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test and homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. When these assumptions were not satisfied, logarithmic or square-root transformations were considered. One-way ANOVA was performed to compare quantitative variables among groups, followed by Fisher’s Least Significant Difference test at the 5% significance level when significant differences were detected. For the multiple linear regression model, normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, homoscedasticity using the Breusch-Pagan test, and independence of residuals using the Durbin–Watson test. Multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor, with VIF values below 5 indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Cook’s distance was used to identify influential observations. Model fit was assessed using R², adjusted R², the overall F-test, and root mean square error. Explanatory variables were selected based on their relevance to the study objective and their expected relationship with waste generation. Chi-square tests were used for qualitative variables to assess differences among stakeholder categories. Multiple linear regression was used to examine associations between waste types, stakeholder characteristics, and waste weight generated per fruit. Open-ended responses concerning suggestions for improving pineapple waste management and valorization were coded into thematic categories. A frequency matrix of themes by stakeholder category was then subjected to hierarchical clustering to identify groups of stakeholders with similar patterns of suggested measures. The resulting clusters were presented graphically to describe differences in priorities among stakeholder categories. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. 3 Results 3.1 Interactions among stakeholders The operational functioning of the pineapple value chain is illustrated in Fig. 2 , which models the dynamics of exchanges among five key categories of actors especially producers, collectors, processors, sellers (wholesalers and retailers), and final consumers. The figure highlights not only the physical flow of products from harvest to final consumption, but also the downward and upward circulation of information along the chain. This systemic representation provides a clear visualization of the value chain structure and the interdependencies that govern the pineapple market. Fig. 2 Full size image Systemic analysis of functional interactions among actors in the pineapple value chain. Solid arrows represent material flows; dashed arrows represent information flows The analysis of interactions reveals a complex bidirectional structure. Material flows (represented by solid arrows) follow a conventional linear progression. Producers supply fresh pineapples to collectors, who aggregate production to supply either sellers with fresh fruit or processors for transformation into juice, dried products, or canned goods. In contrast, feedback loops (represented by dashed arrows) play a critical role in market regulation. These loops enable the upward transmission of strategic information. Consumers communicate preferences, satisfaction levels, and complaints to retailers, who then relay demand forecasts and quality requirements to wholesalers, processors, and collectors. Ultimately, collectors and processors transmit market signals, such as price trends, quality standards, and optimal harvest timing, back to producers. This continuous flow of information ensures coordination across the value chain and enhances its capacity to respond efficiently to final consumer demand. 3.2 Socio-demographic profile of stakeholders The results presented in Table 1 reveal substantial differences among actors in the pineapple value chain. Gender distribution varies significantly between groups ( p < 0.001), with a majority of men among collectors (74.2%) and consumers (75%), and a majority of women among producers (71.7%), processors (56.7%), and traders (82.5%). Age groups also differ significantly ( p < 0.001). Consumers are predominantly over 45 years old (55.8%), producers are mainly between 36 and 40 years old (27.5%), and processors are mostly between 31 and 35 years old (21.7%). Marital status varies significantly among value chain actors ( p < 0.01). Single individuals are predominant among collectors (59.2%) and processors (63.3%), whereas consumers (90%), producers (75.8%), and traders (75%) are predominantly married. These differences reflect contrasting social structures depending on the role occupied within the value chain. Educational level also varies significantly among actors ( p < 0.01). Collectors and consumers mainly have a secondary level of education (42.5%). Some producers have a primary level of education (32.5%), while others have no formal education (25.8%). Finally, processors stand out with a high proportion of individuals holding higher education degrees (39.2%). Peels are predominant among processors (37.5%) and consumers (37.5%), followed by leaves among producers (32.5%) and stems among collectors (34.2%). Unsold fruits mainly concern traders (30.8%), while residual juice is largely reported by processors (37.5%). Perceived environmental impacts also vary significantly among actors ( p < 0.001). Producers mainly report water contamination and soil degradation, processors perceive air pollution, collectors highlight insect proliferation, and consumers report odor-related nuisances. These impacts are more frequently observed during the rainy season (> 60%), although this difference is not statistically significant ( p > 0.05). Waste management practices differ across actor groups. Collectors and traders primarily favor selling, consumers and producers tend to practice disposal or use waste for animal feed, whereas processors focus mainly on processing activities. The majority of actors are unaware of waste valorization technologies (70.8%, p > 0.05), and their practical application remains very limited (< 10%), mainly due to lack of training and financial resources. Furthermore, perceptions of the potential for waste valorization differ significantly among actors ( p < 0.001). Most respondents acknowledge its benefits for improving soil fertility and reducing pollution. Finally, interest in training remains high (> 60%, p > 0.05), reflecting a shared willingness to adopt more sustainable waste management practices that take into account the specific roles of each actor in the value chain. Producers emphasize sharing experiences, monitoring volumes, and collection cooperatives, and show strong interest in processing units to be set up in the crop production area. Collectors, for their part, prioritize hygiene, sorting, and logistics optimization. Processors focus on compost, biogas, and production optimization. Sellers emphasize artisanal processing, hygienic storage, and customer awareness. Finally, consumers emphasize reuse in the garden, fruit preservation, and community programs. This structure shows that pineapple waste management requires interventions tailored to each class to maximize the effectiveness and ownership of practices. 4 Discussion This study examined the pineapple value chain in South Kivu with four specific objectives: (i) to quantify and characterize waste flows at each stage of the value chain; (ii) to analyze current management practices and their environmental impacts; (iii) to identify the constraints and perceptions of different actors regarding waste valorization; and (iv) to propose locally appropriate avenues for waste valorization within a circular bioeconomy framework. The following discussion addresses each objective in turn, comparing our findings systematically with the existing literature. 4.1 Socio-demographic profile of actors and implications for waste management The results reveal a strongly gendered division of labor along the pineapple value chain: women dominate production (71.7%), processing (56.7%), and selling (82.5%), while men are the majority among collectors (74.2%) and consumers (75%). This profile is consistent with broader patterns observed in horticultural and fruit value chains across East Africa. Previous studies [ 24 , 25 ] similarly report that women are concentrated in handling, processing, and marketing of perishable products but remain underrepresented in higher value-added segments, particularly logistics and collection. This convergence suggests that the feminization of pineapple production and trade in South Kivu reflects structural patterns in regional agricultural value chains rather than an isolated phenomenon. The average age of producers and processors (26–40 years) indicates an active workforce, but limited formal education (primary or secondary level) constrains the adoption of technological innovations. This finding aligns with [ 26 ], which identified low education levels as a major barrier to agricultural residue valorization across tropical Africa. However, our study extends this observation by showing that the barrier is not only educational but also organizational: even actors with secondary education reported limited access to training and equipment. This suggests that targeted training programs rather than general education interventions may be more effective in strengthening the professionalization of the pineapple value chain in South Kivu. 4.2 Typology and volumes of pineapple waste The main waste fractions identified were peels (33%), leaves (21.5%), damaged fruits (17.3%), and stems (14.5%). These proportions are consistent with findings from other tropical producing regions: studies conducted in Ghana [ 27 ], Nigeria [ 28 ], and South Africa [ 29 ] all report that peels and leaves represent between 30 and 40% of the total weight of handled fruits. This convergence across geographically distant contexts suggests that waste composition is primarily determined by the morphology of the pineapple plant rather than by local practices. However, our finding that 32.8% of total pineapple weight becomes waste (≈ 0.46 kg per fruit) exceeds the 25% average estimated by [ 30 ] for fruit and vegetable waste in general. This divergence likely reflects the specific characteristics of the pineapple value chain in South Kivu, where losses are concentrated at processing (37.7%) and retail (36.2%) stages. Unlike [ 30 ], which focused primarily on post-harvest losses at the production level, our actor-level approach reveals that the most significant losses occur downstream, in processing and marketing. This finding has important implications: interventions targeting only producers would miss the largest sources of waste. 4.3 Current management practices and their environmental impacts The dominant waste management practices identified were informal sale (27%), open dumping (19%), burning (8.5%), and composting (8.1%), indicating that most waste is not sustainably valorized. These proportions align with observations from other sub-Saharan African contexts. A study conducted in Uganda [ 31 ] similarly found that lack of infrastructure, technical training, and economic incentives explains the persistence of informal practices in fruit waste management. Our study extends this finding by quantifying the environmental impacts perceived by actors: water contamination, air pollution, and insect proliferation were the most frequently cited. These observations align with [ 32 ], which highlights that untreated organic waste from tropical fruits can cause eutrophication of aquatic environments and greenhouse gas emissions during anaerobic fermentation. The seasonal increase in waste during the rainy season (reported by over 60% of respondents) confirms the findings of [ 33 ], which showed that post-harvest management in humid tropical climates requires appropriate storage infrastructure to reduce losses and associated pollution. However, our study goes further by showing that this seasonal dynamic is not merely a technical issue but also an economic one: the absence of storage facilities forces actors to sell rapidly at lower prices or to discard unsold fruits, amplifying both losses and environmental impacts. 4.4 Constraints and perceptions regarding valorization The results reveal that 70.8% of stakeholders are unaware of waste valorization technologies, and fewer than 10% apply them in practice. Existing knowledge mainly concerns animal feed (49%) and flour production (25%), but applications remain sporadic. The main barriers identified lack of training (26%), lack of equipment (24%) and absence of public subsidies (28%) align with studies indicating that valorization of pineapple residues into bromelain, biogas, or biofertilizers requires high initial investment and specific technical skills [ 34 , 35 ]. This convergence confirms that the barriers to valorization in South Kivu are not primarily technical but economic and institutional. However, our findings also reveal a notable divergence from the literature: while several studies emphasize the technical potential of high-value products such as bromelain [ 35 ], our respondents showed limited awareness of these options and expressed stronger interest in low-tech solutions such as animal feed and composting. This suggests that the valorization pathways promoted in the scientific literature may not align with the priorities and capacities of local actors. This misalignment underscores the need for participatory approaches that start from actors’ expressed needs rather than imposing externally defined technological solutions. Regarding economic potential, most stakeholders (29%) recognized that valorization can reduce environmental pollution and improve soil fertility (26%), while 22% perceived income generation potential. These perceptions align with [ 36 ], which demonstrated that pineapple by-products can be used to produce enzymes, animal feed and high value-added bioplastics. However, our study shows that these perceptions remain abstract: actors recognize the potential but lack the means to realize it. This finding supports the argument developed in [ 37 ] that adopting circular value chains in Africa requires not only technical solutions but also institutional and financial support. 4.5 Feasibility of valorization options in the South Kivu context Building on the constraints identified in Sect. 4.4, this section examines the feasibility of the four main valorization options cited by stakeholders animal feed, composting, biogas, and flour production in terms of local resource availability, investment costs, and market access. Animal feed emerged as the most frequently cited option (49.5% of respondents) and is the most immediately feasible. Pineapple peels and residual pulp are already used informally as feed for pigs and poultry in Idjwi and Bukavu, requiring no specialized equipment. The main limitation is organizational: without drying or storage facilities, fresh residues spoil rapidly and cannot be marketed beyond the immediate vicinity. Low-cost solar drying could extend shelf life, but requires initial investment that most smallholders cannot afford without microcredit or cooperative support. Composting is technically accessible, relying on locally available labor and simple equipment. However, it is constrained by land and time requirements (6–8 weeks per cycle) and by an undeveloped market: most farmers produce their own amendments or rely on chemical fertilizers, and there is no structured demand for commercial compost. Its viability therefore depends on emerging local demand, potentially driven by urban horticulture or institutional buyers. Biogas production remains marginal in South Kivu despite promotion elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa. The main barriers are high initial investment, continuous water supply requirements, and the technical skills needed for maintenance. Given the low levels of technology awareness and adoption reported in Sect. 4.4, biogas appears unlikely to be adopted at scale without substantial external support. Flour production from pineapple peels, cited by 25.5% of respondents, is technically feasible but requires milling equipment, drying facilities, and food-safety certification for formal markets. Its viability depends on a niche market for gluten-free or fiber-rich flours, currently absent in South Kivu. Small-scale production for local bakeries or institutional buyers could be a starting point but would require market development and quality standardization. In summary, feasibility follows a gradient: animal feed is immediately feasible with minimal investment; composting is accessible but market-constrained; biogas and flour production require substantial investment, skills, and market development. A phased approach starting with low-cost, low-barrier options while progressively building technical and market infrastructure is most appropriate for the South Kivu context. 4.6 Locally appropriate avenues for valorization The positive correlation between fruit weight and waste quantity (R² < 0.4) indicates that loss reduction depends less on processed volume than on the quality of post-harvest practices and local innovations. This supports the argument developed in [ 38 ] that adopting circular value chains is essential for improving the sustainability of African fruit sectors. Interest in training among stakeholders was high (63.2%), showing openness to collective learning as a key condition for transitioning toward a rural circular economy. The suggestions made by actors reflect a shared desire to improve waste management: producers propose creating local processing units and collection cooperatives; collectors call for better logistics and training in sorting and hygiene; processors promote energy and organic valorization through biogas and industrial composting; sellers emphasize artisanal processing and consumer awareness; and consumers advocate for domestic and community reuse. Together, these proposals reinforce the transition toward a sustainable circular economy in South Kivu, consistent with the models promoted in [ 39 ] and [ 40 ]. However, our analysis also reveals a critical gap between aspirations and feasibility. Although promising technological avenues for valorizing pineapple residues (biogas, biofertilizers, bioactive compounds such as bromelain) have been identified, their implementation in South Kivu remains hypothetical. They are based on external or pilot studies and require investment, specialized skills, and market structures. As shown in Sect. 4.5, the feasibility gradient suggests that a phased approach starting with low-cost, low-barrier options while progressively building technical and market infrastructure is most appropriate for the South Kivu context. 4.7 Limitations of the study This study has several limitations. First, the non-probabilistic sampling design combining purposive selection and snowball sampling introduces potential selection bias, as respondents recruited through referral chains tend to share similar socio-economic characteristics and market networks, possibly under-representing marginal actors. The geographic concentration of producers on Idjwi Island reflects actual production distribution but limits generalization to other territories. Second, data collection was conducted during a single agricultural season, which may not capture seasonal variations in waste generation and management practices. Although over 60% of respondents reported increased waste during the rainy season, we could not quantify this variation directly. Future studies should adopt a longitudinal design. Third, data on waste management practices and perceptions were largely self-reported through semi-structured interviews, making them subject to social desirability bias, particularly regarding open dumping or burning. Direct field observations partially mitigated this bias, but some practices may have been under-reported. Fourth, waste quantification relied on weighing a limited number of fruit batches (five fruits per site for producers; 20–50 fruits per seller for collectors and vendors). While providing robust average estimates, this approach may not capture full variability across fruit sizes, varieties, or handling conditions. The waste-to-production ratio of 2.2-3.0 t per ton refers to total vegetative biomass and does not distinguish fractions with different valorization potentials. Finally, the study focused on two geographic areas and five actor categories. While these represent the main nodes of the pineapple value chain, other actors (transporters, input suppliers, institutional buyers) were not included. Despite these limitations, the study provides the first actor-level quantification of pineapple waste flows in South Kivu and offers empirically grounded insights into valorization constraints and opportunities. 5 Conclusion The study demonstrates substantial pineapple waste production in South Kivu, exceeding 30% of fruit weight, largely due to inefficient post-harvest practices, limited technical training and the absence of structured value addition. Waste generation varies significantly among producer groups, with Mpene showing the largest cultivated area (1.6 ha), highest yield (47.6 t ha⁻¹) and greatest waste output (104.6 t ha⁻¹). On average, 38.9 t ha⁻¹ is harvested, and between 2.2 and 3 tons of waste are generated per ton of pineapple produced, highlighting the considerable biomass available for valorization. Key interventions to enhance sector sustainability include raising stakeholder awareness, strengthening technical skills, providing access to high performance equipment and establishing micro-processing units. Such measures would promote a local circular bioeconomy, enabling the conversion of pineapple residues into bioenergy (e.g., biogas), biomaterials (e.g., fibers or nanocellulose) and bioactive compounds (e.g., enzymes or antioxidants), thereby fostering rural employment, improving resource efficiency and reducing environmental pollution.
