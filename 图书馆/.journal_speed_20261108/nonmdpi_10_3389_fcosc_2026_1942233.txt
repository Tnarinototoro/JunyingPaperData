Panthera Wildlife Trust Ltd., London, United Kingdom See more Article metrics View details Abstract Counter-poaching patrols are a primary tool for protecting wildlife in threatened landscapes, yet detection rates of active poaching incursions are typically low and patrol planning frameworks that have been operationalised and performance-assessed remain rare. We describe a structured search framework applied over a decade of counter-poaching operations in the Kenyir Core Area, Malaysia, 1,200 km² of mountainous tropical forest critical to Malayan tiger recovery. Drawing on search theory and actor-based crime scripting, we developed a network of prioritised patrol routes based on poacher terrain use, termed Operational Patrol Space (OPS). This was combined with a vigilance metric that tracks time elapsed since each route was last patrolled. This two-stage framework guides rangers first to detect initial sign of an active incursion using OPS patrol routes, then to fix the precise location of the active poaching team using targeted search lines within a reduced search region. Following implementation, active incursion detection rates increased from 16% to 50% and success pinpointing the active camp location rose from 20% to 56%, with patrol team numbers held constant. We discuss transferability to other protected area contexts and provide sufficient methodological detail for the framework to be replicated and adapted by other teams. 1 Introduction Globally poaching continues to drive declines in wildlife populations despite substantial investment in ranger-based protection (Benítez-López et al., 2017). While rangers can be instrumental in protecting these populations (Moore et al., 2018), the evidence that counter-poaching patrols translate into wildlife population increases remains mixed (Rytwinski et al., 2026). Counter-poaching is resource intensive, and many protected areas face constraints. Given the limitations, this suggests there is scope for enhancing existing ranger performance through improved patrol planning and deployment. A ranger team successfully locating a poacher in a large forested protected area has been described as like looking for a needle in a haystack, accurate if the needle was mobile and actively trying to avoid you. Detection rates of poaching incursions can be extremely low (eg O’Kelly et al., 2018; Van Doormaal et al., 2021). Where the rangers’ objective is to intercept and apprehend poachers, few detections are converted into an apprehension, and even fewer in a successful prosecution. For each mechanism of counter-poaching to work, whether deterrence, incapacitation, disruption, or harm reduction, it first relies on detecting and locating the poaching team with high certainty (Wilson and Boratto, 2020). Consequently, much counter-poaching patrol effort focuses on detecting early signs of an active incursion, yet there are few published examples of patrol planning frameworks that have been operationalised and whose performance has been rigorously assessed. Ranger data collection systems such as SMART (www.smartconservationtools.org) and EarthRanger (Wall et al., 2024) offer clarity on where patrols went and what they found, but advancing from this to planning where patrols should go next to optimise protection has lagged. Equally, ranger experience and knowledge are integral to patrol planning, but over-dependence on it has left gaps and has not delivered intended conservation outcomes (Kuiper et al., 2023). Counter-poaching is logistically and tactically challenging, financially costly, and risky: over 150 rangers lose their lives each year (World Bank, 2020). These are strong reasons to ensure counter-poaching patrols are well planned. 1.1 Search theory and the counter-poaching challenge Search theory was first developed during the Second World War to enhance the efficiency of locating enemy vessels and missing allied ships at sea (Koopman, 1946). Advancements in the discipline have since improved search and rescue (SAR) practices for missing persons (Cooper, 2000). The parallels to counter-poaching are clear, both involve optimising limited team resources and balancing thoroughness of search with speed in locating the quarry. Throughout the term ‘quarry’ is used to refer to the object of a search operation, whether a victim being searched by SAR, or a poacher. The covert nature of poaching means information that could guide counter-poaching teams is limited, fragmented and contradictory. Protection team managers making decisions in this uncertainty often rely on routine or reconnaissance patrols as a primary source of information, augmented by covert cameras or community contacts. A key tension in patrol planning is the difference between exploration- visiting localities not recently patrolled to learn about current poacher use, and exploitation- focusing resources on hotspots showing repeated activity. Patrol targets such as total kilometres patrolled, and area coverage encourage broad exploration but spread resources thinly and risk wasting patroller time where there is an extremely low probability of a poacher using the place. Arrest or snare recovery indicators can encourage teams to double down on where they have experienced success in the past. Sustained high frequency police patrol revisitation to crime hotspots can be effective in depressing criminal activity in those localities (Gibson et al., 2017), and revisitation frequency is more important in reducing poaching than overall patrol effort in counter-poaching (Linkie et al., 2015). But where team leaders over-rely on private sources of information and their own experience, recency or awareness bias can over-prioritise effort in a small number of localities with often questionable results (Dhanjal-Adams et al., 2016; Kuiper et al., 2023). Attempts have been made to move beyond known poaching locations into predicted risk areas. Critchlow et al. (2017) used spatially explicit probability models fitted to ranger observation data to guide patrol allocation in Uganda, improving detection rates. In marine protected areas, near-repeat analysis and habitat-based risk modelling have similarly been used to predict poaching risk and improve patrol efficiency (Thiault et al., 2020; Weekers et al., 2020). These approaches connect to a broader body of spatial crime analysis developed in policing and crime prevention. Among these, risk terrain modelling (RTM) (Caplan et al., 2011) and actor-based crime scripting (Cornish, 1990) shift the analytical focus from where crimes have occurred to where and how they are enabled. RTM identifies how features of a landscape such as access routes and resource concentrations, co-locate to create settings where crime is most likely to occur, producing a predictive risk map to guide planners. Actor-based crime scripting defines the sequence of an offender’s actions across the crime event, from preparation and access through to exit, identifying the micro-locations relevant at each stage. These approaches are important in identifying overlooked places that are not where the crime itself occurs, but which are critical in enabling it. Applied to poaching, these methods allow patrol planners to anticipate the routes, access points, rest areas, and other micro-places that enable each stage of an incursion and open new options for interdiction and disruption. Both techniques have potential to strengthen patrol planning in wildlife protection, yet their application to counter-poaching remains limited. A second challenge is integrating risk maps into an operational planning framework that balances the exploration-exploitation tension. Fang et al. (2017) attempted this with PAWS, a game-theoretic patrol planning tool, though its assumption of poacher awareness of ranger movements may not hold universally. An important consideration with any such tool is connecting risk maps to rangers’ practical knowledge of the landscape to ensure operational utility. A final, underexplored challenge is the transition from finding initial sign of an active incursion to fixing the poacher’s exact position for an apprehension. Almost all published counter-poaching patrol planning frameworks end at the point of initial detection. The F3EAD cycle (Find, Fix, Finish, Exploit, Analyse, Disseminate), developed as a dynamic targeting process for military tracking (Gomez, 2011), offers a useful framework here. It structures the transition from detecting a quarry’s general presence (Find) through pinpointing their precise location (Fix) to interdiction (Finish), followed by learning from each operation (Exploit, Analyse, Disseminate). The distinction between ‘Find’ and ‘Fix’ emphasises that it is impossible to conclude an operation with only a general awareness of where the quarry may be. SAR teams address the same problem by developing search profiles, rules for distance travelled and most likely locations, built from databases of historical cases (Grampian Police, 2007). Equivalent search rules for counter-poaching remain largely undeveloped. 1.2 Evidence from Kenyir, Malaysia In Malaysia’s Kenyir Core Area (1,200 km² of mountainous tropical forest), Lam et al. (2023) described how structured improvements to counter-poaching practice caused a measurable decrease in poaching threat to the site’s tigers. Detection rates of active poaching incursions increased by a factor of three to 46%, camp fixes doubled to 67% and apprehension rates rose from 0% to 18%, with the number of patrol teams held constant. Here we formalise a transferable structured search framework for counter-poaching: Operational Patrol Space (OPS) and vigilance, adapting search theory and ranger knowledge (Box 1). We describe the decision logic, parameter choices, and implementation steps needed for replication, and illustrate the approach with evidence from long-term operations in Kenyir. The results are included to demonstrate that the framework can improve detection and follow-up under real operational constraints and we extend the dataset to 2025. Box 1 Key concepts underpinning the structured search framework. Active incursion. A poaching event inferred to be ongoing, based on sign estimated to be sufficiently recent to allow realistic intervention. Active incursions trigger escalation from routine patrol to targeted search. Effective Search Width (ESW). The maximum distance from a patrol route within which rangers can reliably detect relevant sign types, given vegetation, terrain, and sensory constraints. ESW determines the spatial footprint of OPS segments. Fixing the camp. The process of narrowing search effort from general detection of presence to precise spatial localisation of a poaching team’s camp or anchor location, sufficient to enable arrest or direct intervention. Last Known Point (LKP). The most recent precise location at which the quarry was confirmed present, from which all subsequent search planning originates. Operational Patrol Space (OPS). A spatial framework that reduces the patrol landscape to a connected network of routes and segments where detection of early poaching sign is most likely. OPS is defined by combining offender movement corridors, ranger detection capability, and logistical feasibility, rather than by uniform grids. Quarry. The object of a search operation, whether a victim being searched by SAR, or a poacher. Search Region. The defined area within which an active poaching team is believed to be operating, established once an incursion is confirmed and an LKP resolved, and within which systematic search effort is focused to fix the camp. Search Lines. The specific routes within the Search Region along which patrol teams systematically sweep, typically following ridgelines and streams, to detect poacher sign and close in on the camp location. Vigilance. A measure of current situational awareness within an OPS segment, defined as a function of time elapsed since the segment was last patrolled. Vigilance reflects how recently the area was effectively searched. Vigilance decay. The progressive loss of situational awareness over time following a patrol, representing increasing uncertainty about whether a poaching incursion could occur undetected. 2 Methods 2.1 Structured search approach The F3EAD cycle was adapted to the Kenyir context, and conceptualised counter-poaching as a structured search process with distinct objectives and performance indicators: (1) patrols search for sign indicating an active incursion to generate alerts. Their performance is measured by the vigilance level in key patrol lines, and the hit: miss ratio of active: old incursions; (2) Counter-poaching patrols aim to fix the precise location of the active poacher camp to enable an apprehension. Their performance is measured by the hit: miss ratio of active incursions detected:active camps fixed and by the ratio of active snares detected and disarmed: expired snares detected and removed. For poacher sign older than two weeks, chances of an interdiction were very low and consequently the patrol team documented the observations, and this was logged as a ‘missed incursion’. Similarly, expired snares found sprung, or showing evidence of past capture, were recorded as ‘missed snares’, providing a proxy indicator for wildlife mortality between patrol cycles. Poaching sign estimated to be under two weeks old by the rangers making the observation triggered a counter-poaching operation (Figure 1). Figure 1 Flowchart of key decision points from initial scanning patrols on OPS to fixing the poachers’ position. 2.2 Data collection, curation, and incursion analysis Three priority poaching problems shared similar characteristics- Vietnamese, Cambodian and Thai poachers all conducted multi-month poaching incursions targeting agarwood and carnivores using arrays of steel snares. Disrupting their operations to prevent mortality of tigers and prey was the focus of the patrol teams. Patrol teams collected geospatial observational data of all human sign including camps, snares, tree markings, rest points, footprints and litter which was stored in SMART along with an estimated date last used and nationality of the poaching group. Date estimation skills were refined in regular tracking refresher trainings involving ‘ageing stands’ of common types of poaching sign. SMART organizes observational data by patrol, providing a patroller’s eye view of data, therefore the text attribute ‘Incursion URN’ was added to the data model, in which the analyst set a unique code for a specific incursion, and added this to all observations of sign pertaining to that incursion. During routine patrol debriefs the analyst combined patroller insights and context with the raw SMART data to define incursions. We developed a search database for poaching incidents in which specific attributes of the incursion were recorded in a SMART Profiles database categorised by poaching type (Cambodian agarwood poacher, Vietnamese carnivore poacher, Thai mixed agarwood and carnivore poacher). Among these, attributes such as inter-camp distance, camp to snare line distance, camp-camp distance and snare line length (Figure 1) were key in developing search rules for guiding teams on the ground. Full details of the SMART database modifications required to support this framework are provided in Supplementary Material 2. Following each counter-poaching operation, the analyst conducted a detailed incursion analysis and after-action review to learn how the incursion unfolded. This mirrored the after-action review methodology developed by the US military (Morrison and Meliza, 1999) and since adopted widely in policing and emergency services. This was complemented by seven interviews conducted with offenders led by Malaysia’s Department of Wildlife and National Parks, (DWNP) as part of standard post-arrest procedure (see Lam et al. (2023) for full details) during which poachers explained their decision-making and reasons why they selected certain terrain features to develop specific actor-based crime scripts (Cornish, 1990). Each poaching incursion involved entering at an access point, travelling along movement corridors with minimal effort and risk of detection, camping near small streams close to targeting localities where prey species concentrated and success greatest, then retracing their footsteps and exiting via the same access point (Lam and Mat, 2020) (Figure 2). After-action reviews revealed for instance that the apprehension rate of the poaching team was highest when (a) the poaching team was at their camp and (b) the apprehension occurred before dawn. This determined both the search approach and the apprehension tactics moving forwards. Key incursion attributes measured (A–D) to develop search rules are indicated. 2.3 Mapping poacher space use Poaching groups embarking on a multi-month incursion were deeply constrained by terrain. Ridgelines, large riverbanks and logging trails offered the easiest travel routes. Tigers and carnivores targeted by the poachers likewise selectively used the narrow ridgelines to move across the landscape, creating ideal opportunities for snaring. Meanwhile terrain challenges also constrained patrol teams carrying rations and equipment for up to eight days. We created an initial poaching risk map using Maxent (Elith et al., 2011). Five environmental variables were applied based on expert opinion and patrol assessment to reflect the factors that influence the choice of a poacher’s path in moving in the forest and in choosing suitable locations to camp and snare: elevation, slope, distance to lake, distance to ridge, and distance to river. Three main categories of human activity observations used were patrol observations of i) camps, ii) snares, and iii) other human signs (such as litter, parang markings on trees, footprints, or trail cuts). These three datasets were run separately through the MaxEnt program with the mentioned five environmental predictor variables. Default settings were applied, with the selection to run random test sampling on 25% of the sample data for the program to run some simple statistical analysis, acknowledging this does not constitute rigorous cross-validation. The performance of each model run was measured by the Area Under the Curve (AUC). The AUC scores (SD) for the MaxEnt output model were 0.71 ± 0.05 for Camp; 0.89, ± 0.08 for Snare; and 0.77 ± 0.03 for Other Human Signs. The camp model AUC is modest, reflecting the limited number of confirmed camp locations available as presence points and overfitting risk exists given the relatively small number of presence points. Given the importance of finding camps and snares by rangers as the best chances for making arrests on poachers, the three probability distributions were combined, but halving the weight of other human signs predictions, to produce the initial poaching risk map. The combined MaxEnt output functioned as a single spatial layer informing OPS construction alongside ranger knowledge and offender interview data and should be interpreted as a structured synthesis of prior spatial knowledge rather than a validated predictive surface. For further details of methods and variables see Lam (2018). 2.4 Defining effective search width We used experience from operations to estimate detection range of different sign types in the mountainous and dense tropical rainforest conditions of the project site, acknowledging experimental distance sampling is recommended (Robe and Frost, 2002). Detection range was perpendicular to the ranger on the patrol’s axis of movement. Ground sign such as footprints and discards were only reliably detectable within 2-3m of the ranger, while top sign such as navigational markers and parang cuts on vegetation could be visible up to 20m at maximum distance. Snares were camouflaged and almost undetectable off the main trail over 2m, however snares and movement trails were almost exclusively set on the crest of ridges where the topography created natural pinch points that were often enhanced using brash to create barriers. Detection distance of all sign types was markedly reduced in denser vegetation in valleys and the flanks of hills compared to the more open ridgelines and higher elevations (Table 1). Certain sign types indicating poacher presence only occurred in specific areas, for instance cook fire smells were restricted to the immediate vicinity of the camp. During the sessions findings from poaching incursion analyses and after-action reviews were discussed, including successes and failures. The participatory sessions highlighted the challenge of a patrol team maintaining search acuity and mission focus on long multi-day patrols and maintaining motivation to diligently record sign. This was particularly acute when patrollers felt they were sent to ‘dead ground’ where both the likelihood of finding poacher sign was extremely low, and the terrain itself made movement by the patrol slow and physically difficult. The session was devised to account for the reality that counter-poaching is by definition a double act, and success reflects a balance in the operational constraints and advantages of both patroller and poacher. In the session a large topographical map of the site up was set on the wall covered with a transparent overlay. Next to this was a poacher risk map, illustrating detection points of snares, poacher camps, access points and marked routes and projected landscape features of high risk. The GPS tracks of the patrols and the locations used by rangers to camp and rewater were plotted on a third map. During the participatory session the analyst used a whiteboard marker to trace lines over key landscape features on the topographic map that corresponded with important poacher space use. As the lines were drawn, the experienced rangers shared knowledge of past patrols and important localities to patrol teams to ensure the lines were logical from the perspective of conducting 5–8 day patrols. Search lines were connected to form a ‘Search road network’ that covered the most at-risk landscape features with logical patrol routes concentrating on poacher movement corridors and target ridgelines for snaring. The network was necessarily selective, prioritising ridgelines functioning as poacher access highways into the reserve, ridgelines with documented repeat victimisation across multiple years, and ridgelines with a high tiger-value profile and strong snare potential. Attempting to cover every sub-ridge would have been operationally unfeasible given patrol team capacity. True hill flanks between ridgelines were excluded on ecological grounds, wildlife movement concentrates on ridgelines as least-cost travel paths in montane terrain, concentrating snaring risk there and leaving flank areas inherently low-risk for wildlife regardless of patrol coverage. The site was then subdivided into sectors that had geographical congruence and emphasized important areas. An image of the search network was then georeferenced into QGIS and digitized into polylines. Based on the detection range of poacher sign types, a pragmatic Effective Search Width (ESW) of 400m was set, representing a weighted composite across sign types, which was used to buffer the search polylines (Figure 3). This was then cut down into segments 2km long. The spatial framework was termed ‘Operational Patrol Space’ (OPS). This is the smallest practicable spatial unit for deploying patrols. Post-apprehension interviews and ranger observations, formalised into a crime script (Lam and Mat, 2020), revealed that although a typical four-man Vietnamese or Thai snaring and agarwood poaching team (Figure 2) spent little time traversing the main ridgelines, these routes concentrated the most detectable sign (Figure 4). Parang cuts marking navigation paths in and out, and snare lines set along ridgeline flanks where wildlife movement was greatest, were conspicuous to trained rangers making ridgelines the most reliable terrain for detecting an active incursion despite low poacher dwell time there. Figure 4 Terrain use by a typical four-man poaching team during a mixed carnivore and agarwood incursion: (A) relative time spent in key terrain types and (B) daily activity patterns across snare-setting and snare-maintenance phases. The activity pattern underlying this is straightforward (Figure 4). Poachers operated across two phases: during the snare-setting phase all four spent daylight hours establishing lines; during snare maintenance, one or two checked and reset snares each morning while the others searched hill flanks for agarwood, navigating by parang cuts. By late afternoon all returned to a basecamp beside a stream in a gulley to process wildlife products and agarwood, cook and rest. Between dusk and dawn the entire team was concentrated at camp. Over a typical two-week incursion approximately 65% of poacher time would be spent at camp, 24% on the flanks, and 10% at the snare line (Figure 4). The camp was therefore the highest-value target for apprehension, but gulley streams in dense tropical forest impose prohibitive search costs without a known start point. OPS was designed to provide that start point: a patrol network focused on ridgeline routes where movement sign and snares concentrate. A single detection such as a fresh parang cut, or snare would trigger the fix phase and direct rangers toward the active camp, while patrols on elevated ridgelines also maximised the chance of detecting sounds of agarwood harvesting on the flanks below. 2.6 Setting patrol vigilance thresholds After defining where the patrol teams should be deployed, we established the frequency of revisitation, or ‘vigilance’ needed for each search line to optimise the chance of detecting an active poaching incursion. ‘Vigilance’, meaning the state of keeping watch for danger is used as an indicator of our state of awareness within the OPS, essentially the temporal exposure window to poaching. At the point in time when a patrol team enters an area, the team has acute awareness of what is currently occurring in the immediate vicinity, vigilance is high. The moment the patrol has left an area, the clock starts ticking, and with each day that passes, the window of opportunity increases in which a poacher could make an incursion and leave without being detected. Vigilance decays linearly with each passing day until the next patrol revisits and brings vigilance back to high (Figure 5). Incorporating time since last patrolled into the OPS provides managers with a rapid visualisation that helps plan patrols. Figure 5 Vigilance change and poaching vulnerability. (A) Vigilance decays linearly from ‘high’ to ‘low’ as time passes in the operational patrol space (OPS) since last patrol visit; (B) As patrol vigilance drops to ‘Low’, the size of the window of opportunity for a poacher to enter and hunt undetected increases, before vigilance resets to ‘High’ with the next patrol visit. We set four vigilance thresholds that aligned with the multi-month poaching incursion durations: High (under 3 months since last patrolled), Medium (3–6 months), Low (6–9 months), and Very Low (over 9 months). The distinction between Low and Very Low flags segments that have gone critically unpatrolled and warrant priority attention regardless of current operational focus. The thresholds reflect a pragmatic balance between ideal revisitation frequency and available patrol capacity, trading off the large total area of site (1,200km2) with the low number of patrol teams available (four). This does not eliminate the poacher’s window of opportunity entirely, but significantly reduces it. For an OPS segment to be classed as ‘patrolled’ and reach High vigilance, a patrol needed to cover more than 500m (25%) inside the segment. Patrol sectors were classed as High, Medium, or Low-risk based on their topographical vulnerability and history of poaching, and within the sectors OPS search lines were classed as High, Medium, or Low-risk. Sector-level vigilance was calculated as the proportion of constituent segments classified as High vigilance. A sector was considered adequately patrolled when this proportion exceeded 30%, a threshold set operationally to balance coverage ambition against available patrol teams. This threshold is an adjustable parameter; sites with greater patrol capacity may raise it, while under-resourced sites may need to lower it to maintain a realistic patrol schedule. The QGIS workflow for calculating both segment and sector vigilance is provided in full in Supplementary Material 1; SMART database configuration is documented in Supplementary Material 2. We developed a routine method to calculate vigilance in QGIS (Supplementary Material 1). On a monthly basis, the analyst exported a patrol track shapefile from SMART and imported this into QGIS alongside the OPS shapefile. We used the ‘sum lines length’ tool to calculate the distance patrolled in each OPS segment that month, applying the rule to score 100 for every segment with more than 500m patrol distance and zero for all others. The field calculator tool was then used to apply a decay rate of -10 for each month not patrolled. 2.7 Planning patrols to find active poaching incursions Finally, we used the framework to plan out patrol priorities over a three-month window, applying a rule that sectors had to have a frequency of patrol that aligned with the priority. High-priority sectors required a patrol at least every month, Medium priority sectors were patrolled every 2 months and Low priority sectors were patrolled every three months (This is summarised in Figure 6). Within each sector, OPS segments were scheduled for patrol by the protection lead in consultation with the analyst following a review of current vigilance levels and other relevant information such as recent poacher tactical shifts and accessibility. Patrol route selection was varied to avoid repetitive patterns that could result in adaptation by poachers. We adapted the patrol plan monthly based on new information and priorities. New patrol tracks exported from SMART were used to update vigilance levels to provide the protection lead with up-to-date information on time since last patrolled in each sector or OPS segment to guide priorities for the next patrol period (Figure 6). Each year the OPS framework was critically examined to make sure it was still relevant, and the assumptions justified. This included adding additional OPS segments to ridgelines later identified as important movement corridors or snaring target locations. 2.8 Fixing the active poaching camp location The immediate intended effect of patrols maintaining vigilance in OPS was to increase the detection probability of an active poaching incursion and trigger an alert. In practical terms, the first evidence of presence of a poaching team would be ground sign such as footprints, discarded litter or navigational parang cuts on trees. Regular trainings with the ranger team were conducted to enhance skills in reading the sign to identify poaching group type and improve estimation of time since the sign was laid. In cases when it was estimated that the poaching team was still inside the forest with a reasonable chance of locating them (sign under 2 weeks old), a search operation was launched. Once an active incursion had been detected, and the poachers’ last known point (LKP) resolved, a pragmatic search approach was applied similar to Mansfield et al. (2020) (Figure 7). Search team decision making on the ground was backed up by a command centre with an analyst trained in search operations. First, a time-bound Search Area Boundary was established, to define the Search Region within which there was a 99% chance that the quarry would be located for the next week. The Search Region was then characterised to eliminate low probability terrain and establish where the Probability of Area (POA) was higher than 50% for locating sign of the quarry. This included establishing the axis of travel of the quarry and dispersion cone. Search lines were established along each ridgeline and stream within the Search Region. (A) A vigilance patrol locates last known point (LKP); (B) Characterisation of Search Region and setting mesh of search lines along ridges (R) and Streams (S). At this finer-grained search the technique employed was a combination of visual tracking the quarry’s most recent sign and identifying and targeting likely camp locations on the map based on rules from previous incursions. To coordinate a sustained search over multiple days, each search line was given a unique code for use during the operation. Search line codes were allocated to search teams, and teams reported progress in sweeping each of their designated search line throughout the day via satellite phone along with observations. Day to day activities in search operations were coordinated in the field by the protection lead. At base, incoming information on poaching sign was reviewed by the analyst and plotted on the map to build a live theory of the incursion including estimation of the quarry’s identity and intention, including the stage of the incursion they were at. This updated the search map, for instance exact poaching type might be unresolved in the initial discovery, but once identified as Vietnamese, this would entail different search parameters for the camp and snares compared to Cambodians for instance due to their differing modus operandi. 2.9 Assessment The counter-poaching outcomes at Kenyir were evaluated in full in Lam et al. (2023). The present manuscript replicates the pre-implementation data from that study and extends the post-implementation period to 2025. To provide statistical context for the before/after comparisons, Fisher’s exact tests were applied to detection rate (5/32 before; 16/32 after) and camp-fixing rate (1/5 before; 9/16 after). 3 Results 3.1 Improved search effectiveness for poachers Following the implementation of OPS & Vigilance (2018–2025), performance improved markedly (Figure 8). Detection rate increased from 15.6% (95% CI 6.9%–31.8%) to 50.0% (95% CI 33.6%–66.4%), a statistically significant improvement (Fisher’s exact p = 0.007). Once an incursion was detected, camp-fixing success increased from 20.0% (95% CI 3.6%–62.4%) to 56.2% (95% CI 33.2%–76.9%); this improvement did not reach statistical significance (Fisher’s exact p = 0.311), reflecting the small number of detected incursions in the pre-implementation period (N = 5) rather than an absence of effect. The improvement is not just in detection, but also in the follow-through to camp-fixing, the critical intermediate step that enabled apprehension, as described in Lam et al. (2023). Figure 8 Active incursion detection rate and rate of fixing the camp before and after OPS/vigilance implementation (N = 32 per period). 3.2 Improved search effectiveness for snares Prior to adoption of OPS and vigilance, 89% of the 148 snares detected had either already caught wildlife or had weathered to the point they were no longer capable of trapping. These ‘missed’ snares constituted protection failures. After implementation, missed snares dropped to 59% of the 714 snares detected (Figure 9). The percentage of snares disarmed or seized before being deployed rose to 41%. During implementation poaching teams shifted their modus operandi, deploying a greater number of snares in each incursion. Of all snares removed by patrol teams, 61% occurred within OPS. The remaining 39% lay on sub-ridges outside the initial OPS network and were detected by patrols tracking off-OPS as per protocol. Following strategic review, the OPS was revised twice to incorporate additional sub-ridges identified as higher-risk, reducing the off-OPS proportion to 24%. Figure 9 Snare detection outcomes before and after OPS/Vigilance implementation: active snares disarmed or prevented vs expired snares. 3.3 Improved ranger morale and mission focus Shrinking the patrollable area and demarcating the OPS had a positive effect on team morale: the task was more achievable, the team was getting results, and the simple traffic-light vigilance display helped communicate patrol priorities clearly. Prior to OPS, rangers were responsible for deciding which paths to patrol across the vast landscape and tended to default to familiar routes or return to locations where human sign had previously been found. Long-range patrols were determined largely by how many days a team was prepared to spend in the forest, with coverage depending on their own judgment rather than strategic priorities. In contrast, when the same rangers were tasked with deploying camera traps to a survey grid with defined target locations, they trekked further and reported greater motivation from reaching their targets. Rangers gave feedback that precise spatial targets gave their patrols a concrete sense of purpose and achievement that open-ended area searches did not. Using OPS to provide target segments, coupled with the vigilance display, produced the same effect in counter-poaching patrols. Revisitations to OPS routes also helped rangers build detailed mental maps of their patrol areas, improving their efficiency and sign-reading over time. 4 Discussion The Operational Patrol Space (OPS) and vigilance framework substantially strengthened counter-poaching outcomes in Kenyir’s 1,200 km² tropical-forest landscape. Active-incursion detection rose from 16% to 50%, and the proportion of detected incursions in which the poaching camp was successfully fixed increased from 20% to 56%, all without additional patrol teams. This was a significant factor in improving overall counter-poaching effectiveness in Kenyir which reduced poaching pressure on tigers and enabled the population to persist (Lam et al., 2023). These gains demonstrate that structured search planning, grounded in search theory, actor-based crime scripting, and ranger experiential knowledge, can convert limited resources into markedly higher detection and response rates—addressing a persistent weakness in ranger-based protection where most published frameworks stop at initial detection. While other factors including ranger tracking training and experience, and increased poaching alerts from other sources contributed to the performance enhancement (Lam et al., 2023), the direction of change is consistent with the framework’s intended mechanisms: concentrating search effort within OPS, maintaining revisitation through vigilance thresholds, and using structured escalation from “find” to “fix”. The observed changes can therefore be interpreted as evidence that OPS and vigilance can support improved performance under real-world constraints. A core advantage of the OPS approach is that it resolves the long-standing tension between exploration (broad coverage) and exploitation (hotspot focus) while aligning analytical risk maps with the operational constraints faced by rangers on multi-day foot patrols. By condensing the patrollable area from 1,200km2 to 334 km² of high-value ridgelines and movement corridors, the framework made the task achievable and purposeful. The ranger uptake described above reflects a broader pattern: patrol frameworks that reduce decision ambiguity, provide legible feedback on progress, and align daily deployments with strategic priorities are more likely to be adopted and sustained, a persistent implementation challenge in protected-area management (Sintov et al., 2019; Kuiper et al., 2023). The resulting coherence between strategic priorities and daily deployments also eased coordination across teams, an important consideration for parks with dispersed outposts and infrequent data centralisation. For a protection manager wishing to understand how ranger teams are impacting a key poaching threat, measuring patrol effort against that threat is important. This is often unclear from commonly used patrol effort metrics such as distance patrolled or percent of area patrolled, as different poaching threats can have very different spatial patterns (patrols in the east of a park have no impact on a poaching threat restricted to the west for instance). Defining a threat-specific spatial framework (OPS), and measuring patrol effort only within that framework, in time since last patrol (vigilance), provides clarity to managers in assessing true search investment against focal threats rather than raw input, addressing calls for more adaptive and user-friendly performance monitoring in protected-area management (Kuiper et al., 2023). The vigilance metric therefore offers a complementary measure of patrol performance alongside conventional patrol-effort indicators. No patrol strategy can cover an entire landscape continuously, and focused approaches inevitably raise concerns about patrol bias and offender displacement. Annual OPS reviews, incorporation of sign found outside the network, and post-arrest interviews helped detect and correct any systematic gaps. Although 39% of snares initially lay outside OPS (later reduced to 24% after framework refinement), all were discovered by patrols tracking off designated lines—an expected and manageable outcome given the ridgeline-focused design. Crime-displacement risks were real but constrained by terrain: poachers’ preference for efficient ridgeline travel and high-yield snaring sites imposed prohibitive energetic costs on any major shift to flanks or valleys, consistent with broader findings that displacement is neither inevitable nor complete when key opportunity structures are disrupted (Guerette and Bowers, 2009; Weekers et al., 2020; Kuiper et al., 2025). The framework was developed in a context defined by multi-month, camp-based incursions by foreign syndicates, a threat profile that shaped every parameter choice, from vigilance thresholds set in months to OPS geometry centred on ridgeline movement corridors used by teams carrying heavy loads over extended periods. The OPS and vigilance framework has now been operationally adopted at two additional Malaysian protected areas using the same parameters and focusing on the same poaching threat type. It is also under trial in an African savannah landscape with a different threat type, local bushmeat poaching. Formal comparative evaluation of outcomes across sites remains a priority for future work. The structured search framework described here is adaptable to other ecosystems and poacher types by redefining OPS geometry around the spatial constraints and detection processes of the focal threat (Box 2). For example, in an open savannah context, OPS segments could be centred on water points, game trails, or access roads, with larger segment sizes reflecting increased visibility and detection range. The same logic applies in marine protected areas, where slipways, vessel movement corridors and target fishing grounds can be structured into an equivalent operational patrol space (Thiault et al., 2020; Weekers et al., 2020). As the ESW is highly dependent on ecotype, poacher type and search mode, experimental distance sampling as described by Robe and Frost (2002) is advised to define the OPS. Where local poachers operate on shorter timescales, vigilance thresholds should be scaled to days or weeks rather than months. Temporal filters (e.g., weekend or night patrols) can be applied when poacher activity is strongly periodic. In each case the underlying structured search framework logic (map the poacher’s operational space, define a patrol network within it, and maintain revisitation frequency sufficient to intercept within the incursion window) remains applicable. What changes is the empirical basis for each parameter, which should be grounded in local operational knowledge and offender behaviour data. Box 2 Four steps to applying the OPS and vigilance framework. STEP ONE. Analyse priority problem and map poacher space use. First a specific poaching problem needs to be selected. Poaching types differ in their use of space within protected areas and it is important to understand how poachers make decisions about using terrain features to successfully conduct an incursion. Identify access points, movement corridors, target areas where key species clusters, and other enabling points that provide a critical function such as rest, water or mobile phone signal. STEP TWO. Consult rangers and develop a pragmatic patrol space map. Work with experienced rangers to draw and digitise a patrol route network over the poacher space use map. Apply logical, named routes concentrated on poacher movement corridors and target ridgelines. Buffer routes by an appropriate Effective Search Width (ESW) and divide into segments of uniform length. OPS scale should reflect line-of-sight and detection probability: in dense forest, detection probability declines sharply within metres of the trail, warranting shorter, narrower segments; in open savannah, desert, or marine environments, greater line of sight allows larger OPS segments covering more ground. The network will not overlap perfectly with all poacher space — this is expected and intentional. Prioritise the most critical access and snaring terrain first; off-OPS sign detected during operations can inform network refinement over time. STEP THREE. Set vigilance thresholds. Define High, Medium, and Low vigilance categories based on incursion frequency and duration. In Malaysia, where incursions were infrequent but long (1–5 months), thresholds were set in months. In landscapes with high-frequency, short-duration incursions, thresholds may need to be set in days or weeks. STEP FOUR. Implement and refine. Review the OPS framework annually to ensure assumptions remain valid. Patrol pressure may displace poachers over time, requiring adjustment of routes and weightings to stay ahead of evolving tactics. Full implementation of this structured search framework is contingent on certain capability at site, but we present the framework as modular, and the core elements of OPS construction and basic vigilance tracking can be implemented with standard GIS software and modest ranger consultation. During replication of the framework, initial patrol data analysis, ranger consultation and digitisation of OPS was led by an analyst with strong GIS skills. This constitutes an initial investment in time and expertise. The site analyst or data officer at the site was trained on how to conduct the regular vigilance calculations and patrol planning using simple QGIS methods, allowing them to maintain vigilance tracking. The coordination of a multi-team counter-poaching search operation does require the development of strong standard operating procedures, communication and workflows, with scenario-based training. Moving forwards, integration with existing platforms such as SMART or EarthRanger offer opportunities to further reduce the ongoing analyst burden once the initial OPS network is established. Success ultimately depends on complementary investments: rigorous after-action reviews, regular tracking refresher training, and a commitment to updating the OPS network as poacher tactics evolve. To do this well requires a full-time analyst with basic GIS skills and a grounding in crime science, a critical role that conservation is only now catching up with. These elements, while initially time-intensive, generate the search rules that accelerate the critical transition from “find” (initial sign) to “fix” (precise camp location). Protected-area managers facing similar challenges now have a structured, field-tested model that maximises the effectiveness of the rangers who risk their lives on the front line of wildlife protection. Wider adoption, evaluation, and refinement across ecosystems will strengthen the global evidence base for more efficient counter-poaching and, ultimately, more resilient wildlife populations.
