The Therapy Garden, Lafayette, LA, United States Article metrics View details Abstract Climate and ecological disruption can affect wellbeing, social relationships, and connection to place; when experienced across generations, these widespread disruptions can lead to collective trauma, becoming culturally embedded in identities shared at a community-level. This paper proposes a theoretical model demonstrating the reciprocal relationship between collective trauma and ecological dissociation, defined as diminished capacity for relational connection with the more-than-human world. We then present Acadiana Forest Bathing, a free, community-based initiative in south Louisiana, as an application of forest bathing to operationalize the theoretical underpinnings of this reciprocal cycle. Inviting self-guided and group-based sensory experiences in outdoor settings, the program aims to cultivate ecological identity through embodiment, agency, belonging, and connection at individual, community, and ecological scales. Initial lessons learned and suggestions for future programming are explored, as well as discussion of feasibility to systematically evaluate the conceptual application of the theoretical model, such that this place-based programming can integrate lived experiences with community-level interventions to address the ecological dissociation resulting from collective trauma. Introduction The climate and biodiversity crisis increasingly disrupts human wellbeing, social relationships, and connection to place (Burke et al., 2018; Eisenman et al., 2015; Fernandez et al., 2015; Vins et al., 2015). Communities may endure novel or repeat disasters, witness familiar lands gradually transformed, and grieve species and ways of life already lost (Bexell et al., 2023; Cunsolo Willox and Ellis, 2018). These crises unfold alongside the accelerating degradation of land, water, and ecosystems to support expanding technological infrastructure, mineral extraction, energy production, and industrial development (Giljum et al., 2025; Sonter et al., 2020). For communities displaced by these forces, the loss may involve a traumatic severance from places that hold ancestry, cultural memory, identity, livelihood, spiritual meaning, and relationships with the more-than-human world. Even for those not directly displaced, witnessing these transformations can evoke grief, helplessness, moral distress, and fear about what will remain for future generations (Albrecht et al., 2007; Pihkala, 2019). As relationships with the more-than-human world are disrupted, individuals express a range of emotional and psychological responses, captured with evolving concepts such as ecological grief, climate anxiety, and solastalgia (Ágoston et al., 2022; Coffey et al., 2021; Galway et al., 2019; Neckel and Hasenfratz, 2021). While these constructs appropriately center individual experience, collective trauma provides a complementary framework for understanding what happens when repeated disruption becomes shared, culturally embedded, and transmitted across generations (Brave Heart and DeBruyn, 1998; Cunsolo Willox and Ellis, 2018; Woodbury, 2019). One underexamined consequence of collective trauma is ecological dissociation: a diminished capacity to experience oneself as relationally embedded within the more-than-human world. Although dissociation is rooted in a biologically adaptive capacity for survival, it can disrupt the usual integration of consciousness, memory, emotion, bodily awareness, and behavior when it persists beyond the circumstances that initially required protection. Thus, ecological dissociation may affect not only individual well-being but also the relational systems through which people experience connection, responsibility, and reciprocity (Burback et al., 2024; Kimmerer, 2013; Macy and Brown, 2014; Shomuyiwa and Lucero-Prisno, 2025; Woodbury, 2019). Throughout this paper, we use the term “community” to describe a small, but culturally connected, group of individuals, while “collective” refers to characteristics (e.g., traumatic experiences) shared across any number of connected communities. This shared lived experience becomes essential to understand in efforts to build agency, advocacy, and action towards integrated human and environmental health. If collective trauma can shape embodied ways of relating, then interventions aimed at enhancing connectedness and stewardship must go beyond cognitive engagement to create opportunities for addressing dissociation through embodied relational experience. This shift can be found in moments as simple, for example, as slowing down to notice the intricate bark and shadowy leaves of an ancient tree and reflect on its resilient wisdom mirroring one’s own life journey (Figure 1). In other words, to begin to heal this relationship, we must spend meaningful time relearning each other. Figure 1 A 500-year-old Live oak (Quercus virginiana) at the Acadiana Nature Station, Lafayette, Louisiana. Photo by Kelly Guilbeau. One pathway toward this aim can be found in Shinrin-Yoku, or forest bathing as it translates in English. This preventative medicine practice, introduced in Japan in the 1980s and now practiced around the world, comprises “invitations” to slowly connect with the natural world, primarily through sensory experiences (Samoraj et al., 2025). Since its global spread, a host of publications describe the wide range of psychological and physiological outcomes of the practice (Farrow and Washburn, 2019; Hansen et al., 2017; Kil et al., 2021; Li, 2022; Park et al., 2009). Despite growing evidence for the individual benefits of nature exposure, less attention has been given to how forest bathing programs can serve as community-level interventions addressing the relational dimensions of collective trauma. This paper presents Acadiana Forest Bathing as one preliminary example of how intentional partnerships between the mental health and conservation science professions may help address these reciprocal cycles by cultivating embodiment, ecological belonging, community connection, and environmental stewardship. While we focus primarily on development of a theoretical model and operationalized community program, we recognize present limitations in providing direct evidence of intervention effectiveness. Thus, our objectives are to 1. Present a theoretical model of the reciprocal cycle of collective trauma and ecological dissociation, 2. Describe how we have begun to operationalize this model within our unique context, and 3. Discuss challenges and opportunities for more thoroughly evaluating evidence of cycle intervention As a practice of self-reflexivity and acknowledgement of our bias in this paper and community effort, we offer the following author positionality statement (Roberts et al., 2020). Both authors identify as white, able-bodied, cis-gendered women with multi-generational roots in Louisiana, where this case study occurs. Our positionality as a conservation social scientist and licensed mental health practitioner, respectively, informs the development of this community effort and our interpretation of participant experiences; the reflections presented here should therefore be understood as situated interpretations rather than objective representations of community experience. Theorizing a relationship between collective trauma and ecological dissociation Collective trauma is more than an aggregate of many individuals being traumatized or a static recollection of distressing historical events; rather, it is continuously reconstructed as communities seek to understand who they are, where they belong, and how they should respond to the world around them. Through this ongoing process of meaning-making, trauma influences collective memory, perceptions of safety, social relationships, and shared identities. These collective narratives, in turn, shape not only how communities remember the past but also how they relate to one another and to the environments they inhabit (Hirschberger, 2018). In addition to being a psychological phenomenon, collective trauma can also become biologically embedded (Yehuda and Lehrner, 2018). Chronic, repeated exposure to threatening experiences can progressively orient the nervous system toward survival-oriented processing (McEwen, 2017; Porges, 2004). While these adaptations are protective in the context of danger, prolonged activation of stress response systems contributes to increased allostatic load (Crews et al., 2019; Mair et al., 2011) and may alter the expression of genes involved in stress regulation through epigenetic mechanisms (Buthmann et al., 2019; Nomura et al., 2019; Roe et al., 2024). Over time, this chronic survival physiology can reduce access to autonomic states associated with curiosity, social engagement, presence, cognitive flexibility, and reciprocal connection (Siegel, 2012; van der Kolk, 2014). In this sense, collective trauma becomes more than a shared historical narrative; it is experienced as an embodied way of being in the world. We hypothesize that these embodied adaptations, particularly numbness and dissociation as means of coping with threat, may influence the capacity to explore, connect, and experience safety in ways relevant to developing reciprocal relationships with the more-than-human world. Building on emerging uses of the construct of ecological dissociation in ecopsychological and psychoanalytic literature (Brittenham et al., 2026; Luci and Guglielmucci, 2026), our use of “dissociation” extends its conventional clinical meaning to describe relational disconnection at social and ecological scales. In this way, the construct goes beyond a state of disconnection with nature to introduce the reduced capacity for cultivating such a relationship and, for some, recognizing the meaning of this relationship altogether. Practically, for example, we may see decision-makers prioritize non-ecological aspects of societal growth, moving ahead with development or technological advancements regardless of their impact to air, water, habitat, or populations of more-than-human beings. For others, daily choices about how to spend time may never involve options that involve nature; it is simply outside their current worldview. In the context of inescapable threats due to climate change, this form of dissociation may support survival by creating psychophysiological distance from fear or overwhelm that cannot be immediately escaped or resolved (Burback et al., 2024). Extrapolation of this idea can frame emotional experiences of solastalgia and ecological grief as expressions of ecological dissociation, especially when considering the traumatic severance of human-nature relationships and a loss of agency in reforming these connections. Pathways to a reciprocal cycle Collective trauma may emerge through multiple, historically distinct pathways, each of which can shape how people relate to themselves, one another, and the more-than-human world. Aligning with Dana (2018) clinical concept that “story follows state” (p. 35), communities whose histories have been shaped by chronic psychophysiological adaptations to collective traumas may develop shared narratives, identities, and patterns of relating that reflect these embodied adaptations. When collective narratives become organized around domination, compulsive productivity and extraction, or human exceptionalism, they may reinforce hierarchical identities in which humans perceive themselves as separate from and superior to, rather than reciprocal participants within ecological systems (Kimmerer, 2013; Plumwood, 2002; Quijano, 2000). As embodied awareness of our fundamental interconnectedness diminishes, dissociative patterns of relating - including moral disengagement and emotional distancing - may further reinforce compulsive cycles of domination, extraction, and harm toward those perceived as separate, inferior, or expendable, including marginalized human communities and other species (Bandura, 1999; Worthy, 2008). Scholars have argued that oppressive systems not only inflict profound harm on those who are marginalized but also distort the relational capacities and humanity of those who participate in or benefit from such systems (Freire, 1968). While the traumatic burdens experienced by oppressed communities are uniquely severe, both pathways illustrate how collective systems shape embodied ways of relating. For Indigenous peoples and other communities affected by climate change, biodiversity loss, or forced displacement, ecological disconnection emerges through a different pathway: the traumatic severance of reciprocal relationships with ancestral lands, cultural practices, and place-based ways of knowing. From within such a relational worldview, forced removal from or degradation of ancestral lands constitutes a rupture in systems of belonging and meaning in which human and ecological well-being are inseparable. These historical traumas are associated with enduring disparities in psychological and physical health, including elevated rates of depression, posttraumatic stress, substance dependence, and chronic disease, as well as biological embedding through chronic stress physiology and, potentially, epigenetic mechanisms that contribute to intergenerational systemic adaptation (Brave Heart et al., 2011; Nagata et al., 2024; Yehuda and Lehrner, 2018). Together, these related yet distinct pathways demonstrate that collective trauma may contribute to forms of ecological dissociation that undermine both human flourishing and environmental stewardship. Thus, we propose a theoretical framework illustrating the reciprocal cycle in which collective trauma and ecological dissociation can reinforce one another (Figure 2). Trauma narrows physiological and psychological capacity for curiosity, social engagement, and reciprocal connection by orienting individuals and communities toward survival (Porges, 2004; van der Kolk, 2014). At the same time, environmental degradation–including biodiversity loss, habitat destruction, and climate-driven displacement–further threatens human health, community resilience, and psychological well-being (Berry et al., 2010; Cunsolo Willox and Ellis, 2018; Whitmee et al., 2015). Left unchecked, this reciprocal process becomes a self-reinforcing cycle with few offramps. Intervening in the reciprocal cycle If collective trauma and ecological dissociation are relational processes, then healing must also include opportunities for embodied relational reconnection with the ecological self, a concept that understands humans as inseparable from the more-than-human world. This fundamental interconnectedness is represented in many distinct worldviews, including within Indigenous traditions since time immemorial and contemporary schools of thought including deep ecology, environmental psychology, and emergent studies and therapeutic practices related to the mental health impacts of climate change (Kimmerer, 2013; Macy and Brown, 2014; Næss, 1989). Both Indigenous and conventional scientific evidence suggests that immersive experiences in nature can support autonomic regulation, reduce physiological stress, foster social connectedness, and strengthen ecological identity–the sense of belonging to and identifying with the natural world (Park et al., 2009; Twohig-Bennett and Jones, 2018). The present case study focuses on the specific immersive practice of forest bathing to explore the pathway to ecological identity in our theoretical framework (Figure 2). Instead of choosing any one representation of interconnectedness, readers may consider a Two-Eyed Seeing approach in our conceptualization, where the blending of Indigenous and Western perspectives in planning, implementation, and assessment can inform a holistic and respectful application of concepts presented here (Bartlett et al., 2012; Iwama et al., 2009). Rather than serving merely as a restorative backdrop to assumed healing, the natural world may function as an active relational context for the tangible relationship-building practice of forest bathing. Macaulay et al. (2025) builds on these benefits by describing the utility of forest bathing in addressing ecological distress, allowing space to navigate difficult emotions, build self-boundaries, and experience healing moments of connection and awe. While not proposed as a substitute for clinical treatment, forest bathing and other similar contemplative nature-practices can be powerful tools in the journey of sharing suffering with nature relations, mourning more-than-human loss, and healing in place (Aroch, 2026; Vasko, 2026; Willis, 2009). Although the evidence base for forest bathing has expanded, its potential role as a community-level practice addressing the relational dimensions of collective trauma remains underdeveloped. The present case study extends this literature by examining how forest bathing can be translated into a place-based community initiative designed not only around individual restoration but also around ecological belonging and community connection. The conservation social sciences provide an important perspective by recognizing that connections with nature are not only intrinsic and instrumental but also relational, cultural, and identity-forming (Himes et al., 2023; Kleespies et al., 2025; Wiggins et al., 2012). More specifically, it is well established that environmental attitudes and action improve when people feel more connected to nature (Baird et al., 2022; DeVille et al., 2021; Geng et al., 2015; Pocock et al., 2023; Rosa and Collado, 2019; Schuttler et al., 2018). However, when individuals or, as we have argued, entire communities are caught in a reciprocal cycle of collective trauma and ecological dissociation, deep, lasting connections may be tenuous, vulnerable, or completely out of reach. Therefore, connecting with nature in novel ways such as forest bathing can bring benefits far beyond an individual’s health or attitude towards nature (Giusti et al., 2025; Lengieza et al., 2023a, 2023b); in fact, these immersive experiences can begin to offer a bridge to healing on the path to resilience. A central premise in this case study is that ecological connection is not only something people learn about; it is also something people experience. Next, we will apply the theoretical framework presented here to a community-based program as formative evidence of feasibility to spur future evaluation of effectiveness and outcomes. Operationalizing relational healing: building Acadiana Forest Bathing Program context: the Acadiana region of South Louisiana Acadiana is a landscape shaped by longstanding relationships with land and water as well as histories of colonization, displacement, migration, enslavement, agriculture, and extraction. The Atchafalaya Basin, the country’s largest river swamp, and the surrounding southernmost extent of the Tallgrass prairie landscape, have provided sustenance to Indigenous communities including the Chitimacha, Atakapas, and Opelousas since time immemorial (Figure 3). However, relative proximity to the Mississippi River delta led European colonizers to the region, resulting in near-decimation of the existing Tribal communities as well as the start of a “land-as-commodity” narrative (Upholt, 2024). Spanish, German, and Irish settlers were present when French “Acadiens, “ forcibly expelled from modern-day Nova Scotia in the mid-1700s, arrived in the region (McKnight, 2011). While economic diversity existed within these largely Catholic “Cajun” settlements, the wholesale reliance on enslaved Africans for labor played a significant role in both evolving cultural narratives and the experience of collective trauma over the next two-and-a-half centuries (Lumpkins, 2026). Figure 3 The Acadiana region of South Louisiana on the Gulf Coast of the United States. Nine parishes are typically included in the region, which sits to the west of Atchafalaya Basin and Mississippi River. These layered cultural histories intersect with a contemporary landscape experiencing substantial environmental and human health pressures. The nine-parish Acadiana region is home to 700, 000 residents and encompasses communities shaped by recurrent hurricanes, flooding, saltwater intrusion, and extreme heat (Colten, 2021; Skilton, 2023). Although the Atchafalaya Basin continues to deposit sediment to build land and capture carbon in portions of the region (Hupp et al., 2019), other ecological changes—including shifts in waterfowl populations, freshwater conditions, and floodplain function—affect livelihoods and cultural practices tied to the land and waterways (Twumasi et al., 2026). Environmental pollution from historical and ongoing energy extraction and industrial development also present concerns for physical health, while repeated disruption of place can carry psychological and social consequences (Arnold, 2020). As in other places, these impacts are disproportionately felt in the marginalized, under-resourced, and economically depressed portions of the region. Despite substantial research documenting environmental vulnerability across coastal Louisiana, comparatively little research has examined how residents of Acadiana experience the emotional and relational dimensions of ecological change. This gap is relevant to community health because environmental distress may influence not only individual wellbeing, but also place attachment, social connection, and perceptions of collective efficacy. At the same time, Acadiana is characterized by longstanding practices of adaptation, mutual aid, cultural continuity, and connection to place that shape how communities respond to disruption. Examples range from informal networks of assistance following disasters (e.g., the “Cajun Navy”) to more complex processes of relocation and managed retreat, including the movement of families “up the bayou” toward higher ground (Douglas et al., 2018; Hori et al., 2009). Such practices are often described through narratives of resilience and pride in recovery (64 Parishes, 2025; Colten et al., 2012), but framing resilience solely as an inherent characteristic of local people risks obscuring the social, cultural, institutional, and environmental relationships that make adaptation possible. A relational understanding of resilience instead recognizes that communities build capacity through connections among people, institutions, culture, and place (Kais and Islam, 2016; Kałwak and Weihgold, 2022; Summers et al., 2017). This distinction is important to the present case study: rather than positioning forest bathing as a means of making individuals more resilient to adversity, Acadiana Forest Bathing seeks to create opportunities for connection among self, community, and place within an already adaptive social and ecological landscape. Community partnerships and place-based design This program translates the theoretical emphasis on relational connection into practice through a distributed network of free, public, and place-based forest bathing experiences. To achieve a vision of “a healthy Acadiana where every person has access to the healing power of nature, “ partnership is key. Each trail is created in collaboration with a community leader who manages a trail and shares our vision of integrated health. Notably, our partnerships thus far have engaged environmental and wellness-based organizations; we recognize a current gap in engaging Indigenous leaders and divergent perspectives in our initial programming. This matters because the design and installation of a trail should be mutually beneficial; plans for a community advisory group are discussed in a future section. Ample time should be spent before invitation design to understand the goals of each partnership organization, their intended audience’s values and needs, and any location limitations or safety concerns. Participant experiences inherently vary from trail to trail, inviting curiosity as they explore new areas at various times of the year (Figure 4). This partnership model treats the landscape and community context as part of the intervention rather than a neutral setting in which an intervention occurs. Each sign is intentionally designed with partners to reflect unique characteristics of the place as well as goals of the intended audience. Sensory invitations as a mechanism for embodiment and ecological belonging Each self-guided forest bathing trail includes a series of written or audio-based invitations guiding participants to focus their awareness on a sensory experience within their body and in connection with the space around them. Sensory invitations operationalize the theoretical emphasis on embodiment by shifting attention from abstract knowledge about nature toward direct experience of self-in-place (Table 1). The sequencing of invitations is intentional: participants move from awareness of the body to awareness of the surrounding environment, and ultimately toward reflection on the relationship between self, community, and more-than-human life. For example, a trail typically starts with an invitation to ground oneself, perhaps to bring awareness to the bottom of one’s feet touching the Earth. A participant might then be invited to breathe deeply, noticing any familiar or novel smells in the air or sensations (e.g., temperature, humidity, movement) on their skin. These initial practices are critical in moving from whatever mindset was present before the trail to arriving “here, now” on the trail. Invitations then progress through sensory presencing (i.e., sight, smell, sound, touch) to connection with specific entities (e.g., a fallen tree, a blooming flower, a perched bird), a practice to foster ecological belonging. Finally, relational meaning emerges by inviting curious reflection about how humans and nature can mirror each other, how we can share wisdom across beings, or how nature can be an ally in difficult times. These moments on a forest bathing trail do not erase individual or collective trauma; rather, the expanse of nature can allow space for reflection, restoration, and hope. Guided experiences as a mechanism for social connection Guided walks extend the individual sensory experience into a social setting, creating opportunities for shared attention, normalization, and community connection. These guided walks are free, open to anyone, and can last from 30 minutes to two hours. During the walks, a trained guide welcomes the group and sets the tone for the walk, emphasizing participant agency and avoiding prescribing any particular outcome. The group then slowly progresses through a series of suggested solo, paired, and group activities to evoke connection with self, each other, and nature. Opportunities to share reflections aloud are built into the group experience, inviting storytelling, observation, and validation across individuals. This facilitation approach intentionally emphasizes participant agency and reduces performance expectations, allowing participants to determine their own level of engagement. Future programmatic applications Integration of community advising An important next step in the development of this program will be the formation of a community advisory group. Local representatives will help to ensure our approach is equitable, accessible, and representative of community needs. To ensure broad and divergent input, multiple intersectional identities with varying relationships to place should be represented in this group. Specifically, we will aim to engage members of the Chitimacha Nation as well as Black and Hispanic communities, waterfowl hunters and recreational anglers (i.e., traditionally older white men with strong knowledge of place), and medical practitioners interested in advancing public health outcomes. These are not mutually exclusive perspectives and should span generations (e.g., Gen Z, Elders). Place-based challenges and opportunities Conducting forest bathing in south Louisiana highlights the importance of adapting the practice to the ecological and cultural characteristics of the place. In a landscape defined by swamps, bayous, heat, mosquitoes, and frequent summer thunderstorms, participants might approach the practice with humor, skepticism, or assumptions about discomfort. Yet these same characteristics offer opportunities to cultivate curiosity about environments that may be familiar geographically but unfamiliar as sensory landscapes. Rather than asking participants to overcome every perceived discomfort, facilitators encourage them to notice responses with curiosity, moving toward a more intentional relationship with the land and waterscapes around them. When approached safely and flexibly, changing weather and other characteristics of a place can become part of the sensory experience itself. A place-based approach also expands the practice beyond forests to ecological landscapes with their own cultural meanings and histories of large-scale loss and resilience, including the region’s increasingly rare prairie habitats (Vidrine et al., 2024), historically logged cypress-tupelo swamps (Shaffer et al., 2009), and waterways such as Bayous Teche and Vermilion. Forest bathing in these landscapes, including through emerging opportunities for water-based practices such as kayak trails, may offer additional pathways for reconnecting communities with the ecological memory of native habitats, places that have themselves been shaped by environmental change and collective trauma. Participant inclusion and sensory expansion A key lesson from this community case study is that forest bathing should be understood as an adaptable practice rather than a standardized experience. Its accessibility across generations, abilities, and sensory preferences creates opportunities to engage populations who may experience nature, stress, and community differently. This is particularly relevant for younger participants, as growing concerns about climate anxiety and ecological uncertainty underscore the need for practices that support emotional regulation and a sense of connection with place (Canbulat and Dursun, 2026; Keller et al., 2024; L’Engle et al., 2026). However, the invitation to slow down, remain quiet, and sustain attention could be challenging for younger participants; programming could incorporate play, creativity, imagination, movement, and age-appropriate sensory exploration while retaining the core principles of intentional connection with self, others, and place. These adaptations may help children develop skills for self-regulation and relationship with the natural world that can be carried throughout the life course. Forest bathing may similarly offer opportunities to address dimensions of aging, including social isolation, reduced opportunities for meaningful engagement with nature, and the physical and emotional consequences of sedentary lifestyles (Cornwell and Waite, 2009; Watson et al., 2025). Adaptations that accommodate mobility, pace, and sensory needs may allow older adults to participate without requiring conventional forms of hiking or physical exertion. More broadly, attention to inclusion of those with differing abilities is essential to ensuring that nature-based practices do not reproduce assumptions about who is able to access or experience nature (Balciunaite, 2024). Expanding the practice also invites greater attention to sensory experience as an interdisciplinary area of inquiry. Sound, for example, may provide a particularly rich avenue for community engagement through projects such as Sounds of Acadiana, which could explore how residents perceive, remember, and relate to the acoustic experience of the region (e.g., summer rains, evening cicadas), especially as we gain understanding of the impact of anthropogenic sounds on the health benefits of hearing biodiverse nature (Uebel et al., 2026). Discussion of theoretical assessment and constraints We do not position forest bathing as a new discovery of relational connection to place; rather, we understand the practice as one contemporary approach that exists alongside longstanding Indigenous and other place-based traditions of relationality. Additionally, the constructs explored in this paper (e.g., collective trauma, ecological dissociation) are complex and still being developed conceptually. Forest bathing is not a substitute for addressing the structural and systemic causes of trauma and may not translate directly to other community-scales without adaptation to local ecological, cultural, historical, or accessibility contexts. With these constraints in mind, we consider how future research might effectively assess the role of forest bathing in the reciprocal cycle of collective trauma and ecological dissociation. Intended as a preliminary proof of concept, we have not yet applied formal data collection or analysis to evaluate our programs; however, we have considered how our approach to systematic evaluation could assess our theoretical model in addition to programmatic elements such as participant satisfaction and individual health outcomes. Specifically, assessing impact for individuals could expand what is established in the literature (e.g., reduced stress) to focus on exploring change in state of ecological identity and dissociation. While validated scales exist to assess relevant constructs, including Ecological Identity (Walton and Jones, 2017); Sense of Place (Erfani, 2022), and Ecological Grief (Ágoston et al., 2022), we suggest a multi-method approach, utilizing for example, brief pre- and post-walk surveys assessing perceived physical and emotional health as well as nature-connectedness, focus groups for in-depth dialogue about meaning-making, and memorializing events to understand and shift cultural norms related to naming and holding space for collective trauma and healing. Methods such as oral history and photovoice offer reflective creative expression and position participants as co-authors in this community intervention. For forest bathing to inform public health policy, future evaluation must move beyond asking whether individual participants “feel better” and examine at a population level precisely how, for whom, under what conditions, and through which pathways change in our theoretical model occurs. For example, partnerships with healthcare settings could examine whether forest bathing functions as a complementary health-promotion or preventive intervention, improving social determinants of health and reducing trauma-relevant indicators at community-scale. Similarly, assessing community-wide trends in ecological stewardship with local environmental partners could indicate positive movement within our theoretical model. A holistic approach to program evaluation and theoretical assessment could, ultimately, work toward contextually demonstrating constructs such as collective effervescence, a shared feeling of energy and harmony across people (Liebst, 2019), or collective flourishing, introduced by Indigenous scholar Yuria Celidwen (2024) as the fundamental relationality that results in responsibility for ecological stewardship. Incorporating these types of “collective” constructs into our theoretical model could help to advance our understanding of what appears when collective trauma is effectively addressed. With these evaluative suggestions in mind, we believe it is feasible to demonstrate whether and how a community-wide forest bathing program helps to re-establish relationality between communities and the more-than-human world, fostering a renewed sense of ecological identity in the face of collective trauma. This case study, therefore, demonstrates the possibility of tailoring a theoretical framework with complex constructs and interventions to a specific place where historical, present, and anticipatory traumas collide. Conclusion Collective trauma can be expressed through disrupted relationships among people, communities, and place. This paper proposes a theoretical framework to demonstrate that collective trauma and ecological dissociation can form a reciprocal cycle and that intentionally reinvigorating ecological identity through embodied, social, and ecological connection may play an important role in addressing the cycle. We operationalize this framework through a community case study, wherein Acadiana Forest Bathing illustrates one practical approach to creating conditions for reconnection through sensory awareness, ecological belonging, agency, and shared experience. Future systematic evaluation of this work is needed and may become increasingly relevant as Acadiana faces continued environmental change while also becoming a receiving community for people displaced from elsewhere in coastal Louisiana (64 Parishes, 2025). Within this changing context, accessible and place-based nature practices could become one component of a broader community health and climate-resilience infrastructure. No single intervention can resolve the structural conditions that generate collective trauma or ecological degradation. Responding to these interconnected challenges will require communities to cultivate connection, meaning-making, and mutual care across generations while also addressing the systems that continue to produce harm. Forest bathing offers one practice within that larger effort: an invitation to slow down, notice, and renew our relationship with the more-than-human world of which we are already a part.
