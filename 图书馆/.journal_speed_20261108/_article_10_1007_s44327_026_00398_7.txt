While many pilot projects have been tested across European cities, evidence on their full-scale potential remains limited. This study evaluates 78 mobility measures across ten cities through expert-based assessments on five criteria: congestion, road safety, modal share, accessibility, and user satisfaction. Using cluster analysis, supported by Elbow and Silhouette diagnostics, four distinct groups of measures were identified. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was subsequently used to provide a two-dimensional visualization of the resulting cluster structure. The results reveal: (i) digital measures with high anticipated user-related ratings but lower anticipated system-impact ratings, (ii) transformative public transport and infrastructure measures with consistently high performance, (iii) administrative actions with comparatively lower anticipated direct-impact ratings, and (iv) “push” policies characterized by moderate-to-high anticipated system-impact ratings but comparatively lower anticipated user satisfaction. Overall, the findings highlight that large-scale interventions received the highest anticipated impact ratings across multiple dimensions, while the results also suggest differentiated trade-offs between system-oriented and user-oriented outcomes across intervention types. Transport Research Urban Policy Multivariate Analysis Social Indicators Urban Sociology Transport Geography Sustainable Urban Mobility Strategies 1 Introduction Urban transport policy has increasingly shifted towards promoting sustainable mobility in response to pressing environmental, social, and economic challenges. The transport sector remains a major source of greenhouse-gas emissions and urban air pollution, making the decarbonisation of mobility a central component of European climate policy ​[ 10 , 14 , 38 ]. In this context, cities are at the forefront of implementing strategies aligned with broader sustainability goals, including climate neutrality, improved air quality, and enhanced quality of life. Sustainable Urban Mobility Plans (SUMPs) and related policy frameworks emphasize the need to reduce private car dependency while promoting more efficient, inclusive, and environmentally friendly modes of transport, such as public transport, walking, and cycling [ 28 ]. Public transport systems, in particular, are considered a cornerstone of sustainable mobility transitions due to their potential to move large numbers of people efficiently while reducing congestion and emissions [ 28 ]; [ 38 ]. Recent European urban mobility policy further emphasizes safe, accessible, inclusive, smart, resilient, and emission-free urban transport systems, with particular attention to public transport and multimodal integration [ 38 ]. To achieve these objectives, cities have implemented a wide range of measures aimed at strengthening public transport and reshaping urban mobility systems. These measures include infrastructural interventions (e.g., dedicated bus lanes, priority signaling), service improvements (e.g., increased frequency, integrated ticketing), digital innovations (e.g., real-time information systems, mobility-as-a-service platforms), and policy instruments (e.g., pricing strategies, regulatory frameworks). The diversity of such interventions reflects the multidimensional nature of sustainable urban mobility and the need to combine infrastructure, service, behavioural, and regulatory measures rather than relying on a single type of intervention [ 2 ]. Importantly, the effectiveness of these measures is often context-dependent, influenced by local urban form, governance structures, socio-economic conditions, and existing transport networks. Recent comparative research also highlights substantial variation in the mobility initiatives, objectives, and implementation approaches adopted across cities, reinforcing the importance of considering local context when evaluating sustainable mobility strategies [ 23 ]. As a result, there is growing recognition of the need for cross-city analyses to better understand which measures work, under what conditions, and to what extent. In recent years, pilot implementations and living labs have emerged as key tools for testing innovative mobility solutions in real-world environments. Urban Living Labs and related experimental settings provide environments in which new solutions can be designed, tested, and evaluated under real-world conditions, supporting iterative learning during innovation processes [ 5 ]. More recent evidence confirms that urban public transport experimentation plays an important role in introducing new mobility measures and supporting longer-term low-carbon mobility transitions [ 39 ]. European research and innovation programs, in particular, have supported numerous pilot actions across multiple cities, generating valuable insights into the operational feasibility and short-term impacts of various measures. However, despite their potential, pilot implementations often face significant challenges when it comes to scaling up. Institutional inertia, political acceptability, and context-specific barriers can limit the transferability and expansion of successful pilots [ 3 , 33 ]. More recent research on the transition from pilot projects to wider implementation similarly shows that municipalities often face substantial organizational and institutional challenges when attempting to scale tested urban solutions within or across cities [ 35 ]. Moreover, evaluations of pilot projects tend to focus on localized and short-term outcomes, leaving a critical gap in understanding their potential performance at full scale. Addressing this gap requires moving beyond ex-post assessments of pilot outcomes towards ex-ante or forward-looking evaluations that consider how measures might perform when implemented system-wide. Such assessments are inherently complex, as they must account for interactions within the broader transport system and capture multiple performance dimensions simultaneously. Furthermore, there is a need for approaches that can synthesize insights across different cities and measures, providing structured and comparable evidence to support strategic decision-making. The current study aims to systematically assess the anticipated full-scale potential of a diverse set of urban mobility measures across multiple European cities. Specifically, the study pursues three objectives: (i) to evaluate 78 mobility interventions across ten European cities using five common impact dimensions (congestion, road safety, modal share, accessibility, and user satisfaction); (ii) to identify recurring multidimensional impact profiles through cluster analysis and develop a structured typology of mobility interventions; and (iii) to examine how these profiles are distributed across the participating urban contexts. By combining expert-based assessments with cross-city multivariate analysis, the study seeks to provide structured and comparable evidence on the anticipated performance of heterogeneous mobility measures when moving from pilot implementation towards wider deployment. 2 Literature review The evolution of urban public transport is a multi-dimensional process shaped by a complex interplay of social, economic, legal, infrastructural, and environmental factors (Wolny et al., 2024). In this context, while many innovations are initially tested through small-scale pilots, their transition to full-scale implementation in diverse urban contexts requires the systematic evaluation of multidimensional performance [ 4 , 24 ]. Recent research further emphasizes that sustainable mobility measures vary considerably across cities in terms of their objectives, implementation approaches, and measurable outcomes, reinforcing the need to consider both intervention characteristics and local context when evaluating their broader potential [ 23 ]. 2.1 Demonstration of mobility and transport measures Specific applications include real-time passenger information, smart ticketing, and advanced traffic management, which can contribute to addressing urban mobility challenges related to efficiency, safety, emissions, and traffic congestion [ 19 , 36 ]. Beyond core digital technologies, physical infrastructure and operational design play a vital role in transit performance. The implementation of segregated lanes for Light Rail Transit and Bus Rapid Transit, combined with advanced public transport systems such as intelligent signal timing and holding strategies, can reduce travel times and improve service reliability and overall service attractiveness [ 15 ]. Operational efficiency can be further improved through the optimisation of route networks and vehicle fleet structures to better align public transport provision with mobility demand [ 31 ]. Environmentally, the transition to zero-emission fleets, particularly through bus electrification, represents an important strategy for reducing transport-related emissions and improving local air quality ([ 16 ]; Mangiaracina et al., 2016). Taken together, these examples illustrate the breadth of contemporary urban mobility interventions, ranging from digital and operational solutions to infrastructural and fleet-related measures. Because many of these innovations involve technological, institutional, and behavioural uncertainty, they are frequently introduced through pilot projects or urban experiments before wider implementation. Complementing these environmental imperatives with practical demonstrations of viability, many successful urban mobility solutions started as small-scale feasibility studies or pilot programs before being integrated into city policy. For instance, the Vélib’ bike-sharing system in Paris became a prominent example of large-scale shared urban mobility [ 11 ]. In Gothenburg, Sweden, the UbiGo pilot tested tailor-made mobility packages combining public transport, car-pooling, and bike-pooling [ 9 ]. The pilot provided an early real-world demonstration of integrated MaaS services and multimodal mobility packages. A pilot implementation of the SURTRAC adaptive traffic signal control system in Pittsburgh was deployed across a nine-intersection network. The pilot reported an overall reduction of approximately 26% in travel time and 41% in waiting time, together with an estimated 21% reduction in emissions [ 34 ]. More recent systematic evidence supports the broader role of experimentation in sustainable public transport development. Wimbadi et al., [ 39 ] show that urban public transport experiments can provide platforms for introducing novel mobility measures, exploring alternative implementation pathways, and supporting longer-term low-carbon mobility transitions. Some pilots serve primarily as learning laboratories, where the technology continues but the specific service remains limited scope. The rural DRT in Finland, a Demand-Responsive Transport pilot in Porvoo, targeted rural youth travel [ 8 ]. While the specific pilot was short-term, it was documented as a critical “enabler” for future MaaS integration by testing mobile-app-based dispatching in low-density areas [ 8 ]. Recent empirical evidence from European pilot implementations further highlights the complexity of transitioning from experimental mobility solutions to operational systems. MaaS demonstrations have revealed technological, legal, organizational, and interoperability challenges that can complicate wider implementation [ 21 ]. Similarly, pilot studies combining rail and ride-sharing services show that users’ willingness to try innovative mobility solutions does not necessarily ensure continued use when service readiness and reliability are insufficient [ 22 ]. The adoption of transport innovations is influenced by socio-demographic, economic, technological, and contextual conditions [ 12 , 37 ]. Pilot evidence also indicates that willingness to try innovative mobility solutions does not necessarily translate into continued use when service readiness and reliability are insufficient [ 22 ]. However, not all transitions from planning to implementation achieve their goals, particularly when large-scale infrastructure does not align with user demand. Tram systems in Spain, including those in Malaga, Sevilla, Parla, and Granada, have been cited as examples where the achieved impacts did not fully meet initial expectations, highlighting the importance of careful assessment before large-scale infrastructure implementation [ 25 ]. Based on these experiences, the factors that determine if a measure continues include policy packaging, institutional capacity, and data-driven adaptation. Policy-packaging research emphasizes the value of combining complementary transport measures rather than implementing individual interventions in isolation [ 17 ]. 2.2 Transition from small to city-wide scale The transition from small-scale pilot projects to city-wide, full-scale deployment represents a critical phase in urban mobility planning, often described as moving from “niche” experimentation to “regime” integration [ 35 ]. Pilot projects typically operate in protected environments, where innovations may benefit from external funding and lower profitability requirements than would apply under wider commercial or operational deployment [ 35 ]. To achieve full-scale deployment, these innovations must move beyond protected pilot conditions and operate within existing regimes of national laws, social habits, and institutional working methods. Where wider implementation requires changes to these regimes, additional barriers may emerge. Sondal et al., [ 35 ]. The literature identifies several critical organizational and strategic prerequisites that must be satisfied to avert the stagnation of pilot projects. A lack of manpower, technical knowledge, coordination, or clear leadership during implementation can hinder the scaling-up of urban innovation projects [ 6 ]. Bundgaard, [ 6 ] further argues that autonomous administrative units may be better positioned to support scaling because they can have greater flexibility to experiment and use mechanisms such as strategic public purchasing. Explicit mandates and objectives are also essential. Upscaling should be conceptualized as an ongoing process rather than a discrete post-pilot event, for which specific individuals or agencies are required to decide on upscaling early in the planning phase [ 35 ]. Recent evidence therefore suggests that upscaling should not be treated as an automatic final stage following a successful pilot, but rather as an ongoing process that needs to be considered from the initial planning and implementation phases of an intervention [ 35 ]. Finally, a distinction exists between horizontal and vertical scaling-up. Horizontal scaling-up refers to the replication of a successful measure in different locations or its growth to cover an entire city, depending heavily on operational arrangements and contextual compatibility [ 7 ]. Vertical scaling-up involves promoting institutional change and lesson-learning within policy networks, ensuring that the lessons from a pilot are captured and spread to influence broader transport regulations and standards [ 7 ]. The transition phase often faces a “chicken-and-egg” dilemma, particularly for infrastructure-dependent innovations such as electric vehicles, where vehicle uptake depends on charging availability while infrastructure investment is itself influenced by the size of the user market [ 1 ]. For instance, users are reluctant to adopt electric vehicles without widespread charging networks, while operators are hesitant to install infrastructure without a guaranteed user base [ 1 ]. More broadly, scaling mobility innovations requires coordinated changes across technologies, infrastructure, services, user practices, and regulatory conditions [ 1 ]. Although the literature provides substantial evidence on individual mobility innovations, pilot projects, and the organizational conditions required for upscaling, important limitations remain. Recent research continues to focus primarily on specific technologies, individual interventions, particular cities, or the barriers and enabling conditions associated with scaling. Comparative research also demonstrates considerable variation in the types of mobility measures prioritized across cities and in the extent to which these measures are connected to measurable sustainability objectives [ 23 ]. Similarly, systematic evidence on urban public transport experimentation shows that much of the available knowledge is derived from individual experiments and specific intervention contexts, making broader comparison across heterogeneous measures and urban environments challenging [ 39 ]. At the same time, much of the existing research remains focused on specific mobility technologies or service categories, such as MaaS applications or autonomous collective transport, rather than comparing heterogeneous intervention types within a common multidimensional framework [ 9 , 20 ]. This study addresses a specific gap identified in the literature: the limited availability of systematic, multi-city evaluations that compare heterogeneous public transport interventions using a common framework for assessing their anticipated full-scale performance. Existing research focuses on localized, short-term outcomes of individual pilot projects, leaving planners with limited evidence on how these measures perform when transitioned from experimental trials to city-wide implementation. At the same time, the diversity of interventions adopted across cities makes direct comparison challenging, particularly when measures differ in their technological, infrastructural, operational, or regulatory characteristics. By applying a multi-city cluster-based methodology to 78 interventions across ten European cities, this research moves beyond ex-post pilot assessments toward a forward-looking evaluation of system-wide potential. This study provides a novel, data-driven typology that categorizes measures, ranging from digital enablers to transformative infrastructure, based on their multidimensional impacts on congestion, safety, modal share, accessibility, and user satisfaction. More specifically, a research gap remains regarding the systematic comparison of heterogeneous urban mobility interventions across multiple cities using a common multidimensional evaluation framework. While previous studies provide important evidence on individual pilots, specific technologies, implementation barriers, and city-level mobility strategies, fewer studies examine whether recurring impact profiles can be identified across a broad portfolio of measures and urban contexts. The present study addresses this gap by evaluating the anticipated full-scale performance of 78 interventions across ten European cities using five common impact dimensions and by applying cluster analysis to identify recurring patterns across different types of mobility measures and urban settings. 3 Data and methods To assess the full-scale potential of sustainable urban mobility interventions, this study evaluates 78 distinct measures implemented across ten European cities participating as pilot sites. The cities and (number of measures) are distributed as follows: Lisbon (10), Thessaloniki (10), Rome (9), Valencia (9), Île-de-France/Paris (8), Mannheim (8), Leuven (7), Budapest (6), Oslo (6), and Hannover (5). A detailed list of all measures is provided in Annex A. The analysis adopts a generalized perspective, examining measures’ broader characteristics and their potential to influence urban transport systems when deployed at scale, rather than focusing on their specific local implementation details. The evaluated measures span a wide spectrum of intervention types addressing different components of the transport system. These include large-scale infrastructural and operational interventions (e.g., dedicated bus lanes, public transport prioritization at traffic signals, network redesign, and multimodal hub development), which directly affect system capacity and performance. In parallel, a substantial number of measures focus on digitalization and user-oriented services, such as Mobility-as-a-Service (MaaS) platforms, integrated ticketing, real-time passenger information, and mobility dashboards, aiming to improve usability and multimodal integration. In addition, several measures involve regulatory, pricing, and spatial interventions designed to influence travel behaviour, including low-emission zones, parking management strategies, and urban space reallocation favouring public and active transport. This diversity of measures ensures that multiple dimensions of sustainable urban mobility are captured, ranging from physical infrastructure and service provision to governance, behavioural change, and digital innovation. The impact assessment was conducted using a standardized evaluation template distributed to all participating sites. The use of structured expert judgement is particularly relevant in settings where direct empirical evidence is limited or unavailable and informed assessments are required to support analysis and decision-making [ 13 ]. Each participating site was responsible for designating the local representative(s) with the most comprehensive knowledge of the measures implemented in that city. Representatives were therefore selected locally by the participating sites rather than centrally by the research team. Depending on the local organizational structure and responsibility for individual interventions, the assessment was completed either by a single site representative or by representatives of the organizations directly responsible for specific measures. The assessment comprised 12 documented site- or organization-level contributions across the ten participating cities. One designated respondent contributed in Oslo, Budapest, Hannover, Rome, Leuven, Mannheim, Lisbon, and Thessaloniki, while three partner organizations contributed to the assessment of different measures in Valencia. For Île-de-France, the available records contained one consolidated assessment of the site’s measures. The respondents represented a range of relevant professional backgrounds, including public authorities, public transport operators, transport planning and policy, research and development, shared mobility, and technology provision. For each intervention, the designated representative assessed its anticipated impact under full-scale implementation across five predefined criteria: congestion, road safety, modal share, accessibility, and user satisfaction. A standardized five-point Likert-type scale was used for all criteria, where 1 indicated very low impact , 2 low impact , 3 moderate impact , 4 high impact , and 5 very high impact. User satisfaction specifically referred to the anticipated effect of full-scale implementation of the measure on the satisfaction of its users. Accordingly, the scores represent expert-based assessments of expected full-scale impacts rather than observed ex-post effects. Each measure was assigned one final rating for each of the five criteria by the representative responsible for its evaluation. Where more than one organization contributed within a city, as in cases where responsibility for different interventions was distributed among local partners, each organization assessed the measures falling within its area of responsibility. The resulting dataset contained a complete set of five impact ratings for all 78 measures, with no missing values; consequently, no imputation or case deletion was required. To support consistency across sites, all respondents used the same evaluation template, criteria definitions, and rating scale. Because each measure received a single final set of ratings, no formal inter-rater validation could be performed; consistency was instead supported procedurally through the common evaluation template, criteria definitions, and rating scale used across all sites. Following data collection, a multivariate analytical framework was applied to identify recurring patterns among the evaluated measures. Because distance-based clustering is sensitive to the scale of the input variables, the five impact-rating variables were standardized using z-scores prior to clustering so that differences in variable scale would not disproportionately influence the distance calculations [ 29 ]. K-means clustering was then conducted in R [ 26 ] using Euclidean geometry, 25 random starts (nstart = 25), and a fixed random seed of 123. The number of clusters was assessed using the Elbow and the Silhouette methods [ 27 ]. Although the five-point Likert-type ratings are ordinal in origin, they were treated as approximately interval-scaled for this exploratory multivariate analysis, acknowledging that equal spacing between response categories is an analytical assumption rather than an inherent property of Likert-type data [ 32 ]. To assess the sensitivity of the results to the clustering specification, a four-cluster Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) analysis was also performed on the standardized data using Manhattan distance, providing a medoid-based alternative to k-means [ 30 ]. To further support the interpretation of the clustering results, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset and facilitate visualization of relationships between measures. The clustering analysis enabled the identification of distinct anticipated-impact profiles across the evaluated measures. Finally, the resulting clusters were qualitatively interpreted by linking their quantitative profiles with the common characteristics of the measures included in each group, providing insights into the anticipated impact profiles of different intervention types when scaled. It should be noted that the analysis is based on expert judgments rather than observed performance data, which may introduce subjectivity; however, this approach enables forward-looking evaluation in the absence of full-scale empirical evidence. 4 Results 4.1 Descriptive statistics Before conducting the multidimensional cluster analysis, an initial examination of the descriptive statistics provides a foundational overview of the anticipated performance of the 78 evaluated mobility measures. The data, derived from the structured expert elicitation across the ten participating European cities, capture the expected impact of each intervention when implemented at full scale across the five key criteria: congestion, road safety, modal share, accessibility, and user satisfaction. To provide an overall summary of the expert-based assessments, a final composite score was calculated for each measure by averaging its ratings across all five evaluation dimensions. Figure 1 illustrates the distribution of these final composite scores. The distribution indicates that the majority of the evaluated measures received moderate overall anticipated-impact ratings, with 36 interventions scoring in the 2–3 range and 22 in the 3–4 range. This concentration in the mid-range suggests that most interventions were assessed as having moderate anticipated full-scale impacts, while only a limited subset received consistently high ratings across all evaluation dimensions. Fig. 3 Full size image Impact criteria Scores per city 4.2 Cluster analysis To identify underlying patterns and systematically categorize the various mobility measures implemented across the pilot cities, a cluster analysis was conducted. The objective of this analysis was to partition the 78 evaluated measures into distinct, homogeneous groups based on their shared impact profiles across five core assessment criteria: congestion, road safety, modal share, accessibility, and user satisfaction. The analysis was executed using the R programming language [ 26 ], using the cluster package [ 18 ]. A fundamental requirement of the k-means algorithm is the a priori determination of the optimal number of clusters (k). To inform the selection of an appropriate value for k, two widely recognized heuristic approaches were utilized: the Elbow method and the Silhouette method. The Elbow method evaluates the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS) across a range of k values to identify the inflection point, the “elbow”, where the rate of variance reduction significantly diminishes; indicating that adding more clusters yields marginal explanatory value (Fig. 4 ). Complementing this approach, the Silhouette method evaluates the overall clustering quality by measuring how well an object is matched to its own cluster relative to neighboring clusters. To assess the sensitivity of the four-cluster solution to the choice of clustering algorithm and distance metric, an additional Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) analysis was performed using Manhattan distance. For the primary k-means analysis, the Elbow criterion indicated a clear reduction in marginal improvement around k = 4, while the Silhouette analysis showed only modest differences across the candidate solutions. The average Silhouette widths for k = 2–10 were 0.207, 0.215, 0.224, 0.225, 0.218, 0.221, 0.232, 0.233, and 0.240, respectively; the maximum occurred at k = 10. For k = 4, the average Silhouette width was 0.224, compared with a maximum value of 0.240 across the examined solutions, indicating that the Silhouette criterion did not identify a uniquely dominant solution. The alternative PAM specification produced a broadly comparable thematic structure to the k-means classification, with an Adjusted Rand Index of 0.512 indicating moderate agreement between the two partitions, although individual measure assignments were not identical. Taken together, the Elbow criterion, the limited variation in Silhouette values, the broadly comparable structure across alternative clustering specifications, and the need to retain a parsimonious and interpretable typology supported the selection of four clusters for the main analysis. Following the clustering process, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to project the five-dimensional data onto a lower-dimensional space for visualization. To determine the appropriate number of dimensions to retain, the percentage of variance explained by each principal component was evaluated. As illustrated in the scree plot (Fig. 5 ), the first two dimensions (Dim1 and Dim2) account for 38.8% and 25.0% of the total variance, respectively. Cumulatively, the first two principal components explain 63.8% of the total variance. Accordingly, the two-dimensional PCA projection provides a useful, but necessarily partial, representation of the five-dimensional rating space, as approximately 36.2% of the total variance is not captured by the first two components. The resulting average scores define the “centroid” or typical impact profile of each cluster, allowing for a comparative analysis of their expected performance. Cluster 2: Transformative public transport and infrastructure represents the cluster receiving the highest average anticipated-impact ratings across most assessment criteria: Congestion (3.42), Road Safety (3.08), Modal Share (4.25), Accessibility (3.83), and User Satisfaction (4.67). These relatively high ratings characterize interventions with broad anticipated impacts, likely large-scale infrastructural upgrades, major public transport network expansions, or comprehensive active mobility corridors. Cluster 2 represents the only group achieving consistently high performance anticipated-impact ratings across all dimensions, suggesting that local representatives expect integrated, large-scale interventions to have broad multidimensional effects when implemented at full scale. Cluster 3: Foundational & administrative actions. The profile of Cluster 3 is characterized by the lowest overall scores across nearly all criteria, particularly in Road Safety (1.18), Accessibility (1.86), and Congestion (1.86). Importantly, the lower User Satisfaction rating should not be interpreted as direct evidence of public opposition, political resistance, or social unacceptability, since these constructs were not measured in the present study. 4.4 City-level distribution of clusters To investigate whether the identified mobility profiles were distributed similarly across the participating cities, cluster membership was cross-tabulated by city. The results revealed notable differences in the composition of local measure portfolios. For example, all measures from Leuven were assigned to the Foundational and Administrative Actions profile, while measures in Rome were predominantly associated with the Traffic Demand Management and “Push” Policies profile (66.7%). Mannheim showed a strong concentration in the Digital Mobility Services and Accessibility Enhancements profile (62.5%), while Budapest also exhibited a substantial concentration in this profile (66.7%). Other cities, including Thessaloniki and Lisbon, displayed more heterogeneous distributions across the four profiles. A permutation-based chi-square test, used because of the small expected frequencies in several cells, indicated a statistically significant association between city and cluster membership (χ² = 53.883, simulated p = 0.0011, 10,000 replicates), with Cramer’s V = 0.480. These results suggest that the composition of mobility intervention profiles varies across the participating urban contexts. However, this association should not be interpreted as a causal city effect, as it may reflect differences in the types of measures implemented, local policy priorities, and the site-specific assessment context. 5 Discussion The results of this study reveal four distinct thematics of mobility interventions, reflecting different mechanisms through which urban transport systems can be influenced: user-oriented service improvements, infrastructure-led transformation, enabling governance actions, and demand-restrictive regulatory policies. The four groups also displayed differentiated anticipated impact profiles: infrastructure-oriented measures received the highest ratings across most dimensions, digital and accessibility-oriented measures were characterized by higher anticipated user-related ratings, foundational actions received comparatively lower direct-impact ratings, while demand-management measures combined relatively high anticipated system-impact ratings with lower anticipated User Satisfaction ratings. By cross-referencing the quantitative impact profiles of the four clusters with the qualitative descriptions of the implemented measures across the pilot cities, distinct thematic categories emerge. As a result, each cluster can be defined by the nature of its interventions, ranging from digital “soft” measures to heavy infrastructural “push” and “pull” policies. Below is presented a detailed characterization of each cluster, identifying their common elements and providing a transport engineering interpretation of their anticipated impact profiles. Cluster 1 - Digital Mobility Services & Accessibility Enhancements: Measures in this cluster predominantly involve the transition to Multimodal Digital Mobility Services (MDMS), Mobility-as-a-Service (MaaS) applications, Demand-Responsive Transport (DRT) systems, and targeted communication campaigns. It also captures green fleet transitions, such as the procurement of zero-emission buses, and physical comfort upgrades at public transport stops, including greening, shading, and improved shelter designs. The unifying theme of this cluster is lowering the cognitive and physical barriers to entry for public transport. Rather than altering the macro-infrastructure of the road network, these measures focus on the micro-interactions between the user and the service. They emphasize inclusivity by designing interfaces for the visually impaired, offering non-digital booking options for the elderly, and providing gamified rewards for active mobility. Because they generally do not explicitly restrict private cars or alter road capacity, these interventions received lower anticipated ratings for congestion and road safety impact. However, their focus on facilitating travel, including easier navigation, payment, physical access, and integration with active mobility, is reflected in their high anticipated User Satisfaction and Accessibility ratings. The high anticipated User Satisfaction and Accessibility ratings suggest that the local representatives expected these measures to improve the perceived usability and accessibility of public transport services. Cluster 2 - Transformative Public Transport and Infrastructure: This cluster contains the project’s heavyweight physical and operational interventions. Typical measures involve major infrastructure overhauls, such as the implementation of Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) corridors, the deployment of dedicated bus lanes with traffic light prioritization, the physical reallocation of road space to favor active mobility, and the design and rollout of new high-capacity tramway or metro lines. The measures in this group share a commitment to permanently altering the physical streetscape. They resolve the direct conflict between private motorized vehicles and public transport by physically separating them or granting absolute priority to sustainable modes. Furthermore, these measures require significant capital investment, extensive urban planning, and complex stakeholder coordination. Consequently, this cluster received the highest ratings across almost all criteria. From the perspective of the local representatives, these interventions were expected to perform strongly across both system-oriented and user-oriented dimensions. Their high ratings for congestion, road safety, modal share, accessibility, and user satisfaction are consistent with the expectation that major infrastructure and operational changes may generate broader system-level effects than more localized interventions. These interventions are characteristic ‘pull’ measures designed to increase the attractiveness and priority of sustainable transport modes. Cluster 3 - Foundational and Administrative Actions: This cluster groups preparatory actions, back-end data-collection tools, simulation models, and strategic planning phases. Key examples include the setup of dynamic digital twins to simulate future mobility scenarios, the development of unified transport models, the deployment of advanced AI-based passenger counting cameras on buses, and the creation of regional mobility observatories. The defining trait of this cluster is that its measures form the invisible intelligence layer of the transport network. They are highly analytical, diagnostic, and preparatory, enabling authorities to transition from static, schedule-based operations to dynamic, responsive transit management. As expected from their nature, these measures score the lowest across the impact categories. A back-end data dashboard, a localized spatial study, or an administrative planning framework, while absolutely crucial for the long-term success of transport authorities, is completely invisible to the everyday commuter. Consistent with their preparatory role, these measures received comparatively low anticipated ratings for direct effects on modal share, congestion, and road safety. Their moderate User Satisfaction rating should be interpreted only as the representatives’ anticipated assessment of user satisfaction and not as evidence of public approval or neutrality toward these measures. Cluster 4 - Traffic Demand Management and “Push” Policies: Measures in this group are characterized by Urban Vehicle Access Regulations (UVARs) and restrictive policies designed to disincentivize private vehicle use. Direct examples include the implementation of pollution charge schemes, LEZs, congestion-sensitive parking pricing, strict restrictions on car access around municipal schools, and the automated camera enforcement of bus lane infractions. Unlike the first cluster which relies on incentives, the measures in this fourth cluster share a reliance on deterrence, regulation, and enforcement. They deliberately introduce friction into private car usage, whether through financial costs, geographical bans, or legal penalties, to level the playing field for sustainable modes and reclaim public space for social purposes. From a transport-policy perspective, these measures are designed to discourage private vehicle use through pricing, access restrictions, or enforcement. In the present assessment, they received moderate-to-high anticipated ratings for congestion and road-safety impacts, while receiving the lowest anticipated User Satisfaction rating among the four clusters. This pattern is consistent with the possibility of a trade-off between expected system-level effects and anticipated user satisfaction. However, the present study does not provide direct evidence of public resistance, political opposition, or social acceptability. Such interpretations should therefore be treated as hypotheses that require validation through public-opinion, behavioural, or political-response data. These patterns are broadly consistent with previous studies. The user-oriented profile of digital and MaaS-related measures aligns with research emphasizing service integration, usability, and stakeholder coordination as important dimensions of MaaS implementation [ 8 , 21 , 22 ]. At the same time, the high anticipated ratings of infrastructure-oriented measures should not be interpreted as guaranteed outcomes, as previous evidence shows that large-scale transport investments do not always achieve the expected modal-shift effects [ 25 ]. Finally, the profile of demand-management measures is consistent with policy-packaging research that emphasises the value of combining complementary transport measures rather than implementing individual interventions in isolation [ 17 ]. From a practical perspective, the resulting typology can support transport authorities and planners in comparing intervention types according to their anticipated impact profiles rather than relying on a single aggregate performance measure. The findings also suggest that different measure categories may serve complementary policy functions, with infrastructure, digital and service-oriented, preparatory, and demand-management interventions addressing different dimensions of urban mobility. The typology should therefore be used as a comparative decision-support tool rather than as a substitute for context-specific appraisal, particularly given the observed variation across cities and the expert-based nature of the ratings. The analysis indicates considerable variation in the anticipated full-scale impact profiles of the evaluated interventions. The distribution of composite scores reveals that the majority of measures received moderate anticipated-impact ratings, with only a limited number receiving consistently high anticipated-impact ratings across all evaluation dimensions. This finding highlights the importance of distinguishing between interventions and avoiding generalizations about their anticipated performance. By adopting a multidimensional perspective, the study further shows that aggregate performance indicators alone are insufficient to capture the complexity of urban mobility measures. Certain measures received relatively high anticipated ratings for user satisfaction and accessibility while receiving lower ratings for system-oriented criteria, whereas others receive higher anticipated ratings for congestion and road safety but comparatively lower anticipated User Satisfaction ratings. These insights underline the inherently multi-objective nature of transport policy and the need for balanced evaluation frameworks. The cluster analysis provides a structured and data-driven typology of mobility measures, identifying four distinct groups with differentiated impact profiles. Among these, large-scale public transport and infrastructure interventions received the highest overall anticipated-impact ratings across the five assessment criteria. In contrast, digital and user-oriented measures received comparatively high anticipated ratings for accessibility and user satisfaction, but lower ratings for direct network-level impacts. Measures related to planning, data, and administrative support appear to play a foundational role, with lower anticipated direct-impact ratings but potential enabling roles for long-term system improvements. Finally, demand-management and regulatory policies received relatively strong anticipated ratings for congestion and road safety but comparatively lower User Satisfaction ratings, indicating a potential trade-off between expected system-oriented impacts and anticipated user satisfaction. Taken together, these findings emphasize that no single type of intervention is sufficient to achieve sustainable urban mobility objectives. Instead, effective strategies require a combination of complementary measures, integrating infrastructure investments, service improvements, behavioural incentives, and regulatory actions. In particular, the results suggest that “pull” measures that enhance the attractiveness of public transport may benefit from being combined with carefully designed “push” policies to achieve meaningful modal shift, while carefully considering potential implications for user satisfaction. Despite its contributions, this study has certain limitations. The analysis is based on expert judgments rather than observed performance data, which may introduce subjectivity. Each measure received one final set of ratings from the designated local representative responsible for its evaluation. Although this approach ensured that assessments were provided by representatives with direct knowledge of the local interventions and their implementation context, the same measure was not independently rated by multiple evaluators. Consequently, inter-rater agreement or inter-rater reliability could not be assessed. The resulting scores should therefore be interpreted as informed local assessments of anticipated full-scale impacts rather than independently replicated expert judgments. Future research could strengthen this approach by involving multiple independent evaluators per measure and formally assessing agreement among raters before aggregating their assessments. Additionally, the assessments reflect locally informed expert perspectives and may not fully capture local socio-political dynamics or behavioral responses. Future research could build upon this work by incorporating empirical data from large-scale implementations as they become available, as well as by exploring hybrid evaluation approaches that combine expert elicitation with ex-post impact assessments. Further investigation into public acceptability and the conditions under which restrictive measures gain support would also provide valuable insights for policy design.
