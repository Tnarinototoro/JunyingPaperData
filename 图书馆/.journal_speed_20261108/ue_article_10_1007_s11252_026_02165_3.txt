This study examines the biodiversity–carbon relationship using a twelve-year panel dataset from Haidian District, Beijing—a highly urbanized region where green space expansion has largely reached its spatial limits. Based on a systematic monitoring network of 40 permanent quadrats across 22 subdistricts, we constructed a comprehensive Biodiversity Index (BD) integrating plant, bird, amphibian, and soil fauna diversity with ecosystem quality and ecological function indicators. Using Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs) with quadrat and year as random effects, we find that the BD index is positively associated with carbon sink capacity, whereas green coverage alone does not. Plant and bird diversity emerge as positive predictors, with amphibian diversity exhibiting context-dependent effects confined to wetland habitats. The increasing marginal contribution of biodiversity to carbon sinks over time is consistent with a "quality transition" from area-based to quality-driven greening. The findings provide longitudinal empirical evidence that biodiversity restoration is associated with low-carbon development after urbanization stabilizes. Policy implications include integrating biodiversity premiums into carbon accounting, prioritizing functional complementarity in species selection, and protecting ecological corridors that sustain trophic interactions. Environmental Geography Environmental Economics Biodiversity Environmental Studies Urban Ecology Environmental Impact Urban Biodiversity and Ecosystem Management Introduction The accelerating pace of global urbanization has placed cities at the epicenter of both the climate crisis and the biodiversity extinction crisis. Urban areas currently account for approximately 70% of global CO 2 emissions while simultaneously experiencing some of the most rapid rates of habitat loss and species decline (Seto et al. 2012 ; Grimm et al. 2008 ). This dual burden has catalyzed urgent calls for urban development pathways that simultaneously mitigate carbon emissions and reverse biodiversity loss—a paradigm increasingly framed under the umbrella of nature-based solutions (Cohen-Shacham et al. 2019 ; Chausson et al. 2020 ). Yet a persistent tension haunts the relationship between urban conservation and climate action. Traditional urban planning has often treated biodiversity protection and carbon mitigation as competing priorities, with green space allocation driven primarily by area-based targets rather than ecological quality metrics (Lepczyk et al. 2017 ; Aronson et al. 2017 ). This "quantity over quality" approach has produced a troubling pattern: cities worldwide have expanded their green infrastructure coverage while simultaneously experiencing biotic homogenization—the gradual replacement of diverse native species assemblages with a handful of cosmopolitan, disturbance-tolerant species that contribute far less to ecosystem functioning (McKinney 2006 ; Clavel et al. 2011 ). The fundamental question, therefore, is not whether urban greening matters for climate mitigation—the answer is unequivocally yes—but rather: Does biodiversity itself, as a measure of ecological quality beyond simple vegetation cover, associate with enhance urban carbon sequestration capacity? And if so, through what potential mechanisms does taxonomic and functional diversity translate into measurable carbon benefits? Exploring these questions requires empirical evidence from long-term, high-resolution monitoring programs that can disentangle the effects of biodiversity from confounding factors such as total green area expansion, urbanization intensity, and climatic variability. Yet such evidence remains remarkably scarce. Most urban biodiversity–carbon relationship studies to date have relied on cross-sectional designs comparing different sites at a single time point, which cannot establish temporal precedence or account for time-lagged ecological responses (Beninde et al. 2015 ; Nielsen et al. 2014 ). Longitudinal studies with sufficient temporal resolution to capture both biodiversity recovery trajectories and the gradual accumulation of carbon stocks are exceedingly rare, particularly in densely urbanized Asian megacities (Elmqvist et al. 2019 ). Haidian District, Beijing—the intellectual and technological heart of China's capital—presents a uniquely valuable opportunity for addressing this gap. Since 2014, a systematic monitoring network of 40 permanent quadrats distributed across all 22 administrative subdistricts has tracked the recovery of plant, bird, amphibian, and soil fauna communities. This twelve-year panel dataset, combined with meteorological, land cover, and carbon flux measurements, offers an opportunity to examine the biodiversity–carbon relationship at fine spatial and temporal scales. This study pursues three interconnected objectives. First, we construct a comprehensive Biodiversity Index (BD) that integrates plant diversity (Shannon–Wiener index), bird diversity (G–F index), amphibian diversity (Shannon index), and soil fauna diversity (Shannon index) with ecosystem quality and ecological function indicators. Second, using Generalized Linear Mixed Models with quadrat and year as random effects, we test whether BD is positively associated with total urban carbon sink capacity. Third, we disaggregate the BD index to examine the relative contributions of different taxonomic groups to carbon outcomes, thereby identifying potential ecological mechanisms underlying biodiversity–carbon associations. The central hypothesis is that in a high-urbanization, high-green-coverage context where total green area has plateaued, biodiversity quality becomes a key factor associated with further carbon sink enhancement. It is further hypothesized that different taxonomic groups contribute to carbon sequestration through distinct mechanisms—plants via direct biomass accumulation and structural complexity, birds and amphibians via trophic regulation, and soil fauna via organic matter decomposition and carbon stabilization. However, we emphasize that the observational design of this study can only establish statistical associations; causal validation of these mechanisms awaits future experimental work. Literature review The biodiversity–ecosystem functioning paradigm: from natural to urban systems The relationship between biodiversity and ecosystem functioning has been a cornerstone of ecological research for nearly three decades. Early experiments, predominantly conducted in grassland ecosystems, established a robust positive relationship between plant species richness and primary productivity, nutrient retention, and resistance to invasion (Tilman et al. 2006 ; Hector & Bagchi 2007 ). Meta-analyses subsequently demonstrated that this relationship holds across multiple trophic levels and ecosystem types, with biodiversity loss ranked among the most significant drivers of ecosystem function decline globally (Cardinale et al. 2012 ). The theoretical mechanisms underpinning biodiversity–ecosystem functioning relationships fall into two complementary categories. The selection effect posits that more diverse assemblages are more likely to contain highly productive or efficient species whose traits—deep root systems, high photosynthetic rates, nitrogen-fixing capacity—drive ecosystem functioning (Loreau & Hector 2001 ). The complementarity effect emphasizes resource partitioning and facilitative interactions among species, whereby different species exploit different niches and enhance each other's performance, leading to higher total resource capture and biomass production than any monoculture (Tilman et al. 2014 ). Extending this framework to urban ecosystems introduces substantial complexity. Urban environments differ from natural systems in three fundamental respects: they are subject to intense and persistent anthropogenic disturbances; they contain novel combinations of native and exotic species with no evolutionary history of coexistence; and their abiotic conditions deviate substantially from regional baselines (Pickett et al. 2011 ; McDonnell & Hahs 2013 ). Consequently, biodiversity–ecosystem functioning relationships observed in natural systems may not simply translate to the urban context (Williams et al. 2015 ; Kowarik 2011 ). Urban biodiversity and carbon sequestration Carbon sequestration—the capture and storage of atmospheric CO 2 in biomass and soils—is among the most policy-relevant ecosystem services provided by urban green spaces. Globally, urban trees sequester approximately 0.4 Pg C per year, equivalent to roughly 4% of annual fossil fuel emissions from urban areas (Nowak et al. 2013 ). Within the Chinese context, Beijing's urban forests have shown steadily increasing sequestration capacity over the preceding decade (He et al. 2026 ; Xie et al. 2025 ). Recent research has begun to explore how biodiversity—as distinct from total vegetation cover or biomass—may modulate carbon sequestration. Ariluoma et al. ( 2024 ) synthesized evidence from urban residential yards and identified three categories of measures that enhance carbon–biodiversity co-benefits: plant diversity, provision of good growing conditions, and maintenance practices that retain organic matter on site. Importantly, their review suggests that biodiversity and carbon sequestration are not inevitably synergistic; poorly designed interventions—monoculture tree plantings, high-maintenance lawns—can simultaneously suppress biodiversity and deliver suboptimal carbon outcomes. Soil biodiversity has emerged as a particularly important but understudied dimension of urban carbon dynamics. In a global survey of 56 cities across six continents, Fan et al. ( 2023 ) demonstrated that soil biodiversity was significantly and positively correlated with multiple dimensions of ecosystem functioning, including microbially driven carbon pools, organic matter decomposition, and nutrient cycling. These relationships persisted after controlling for climate, soil properties, vegetation, and management practices, providing strong evidence that conserving soil biodiversity is essential for urban ecosystem sustainability (Wang et al. 2026 ; Delgado-Baquerizo et al. 2021 ). Research gaps Despite these advances, the literature on urban biodiversity–carbon relationships suffers from several limitations. First, the overwhelming majority of studies employ cross-sectional designs, which cannot establish temporal precedence and are vulnerable to unmeasured confounding (Swift et al. 2026 ). Longitudinal studies that track both biodiversity recovery and carbon accumulation over time are urgently needed. Second, most studies treat "biodiversity" as plant species richness only, ignoring higher trophic levels and soil biota that may mediate carbon dynamics through cascading effects on herbivory, detrital processing, and soil structure (Sekercioglu 2006 ; Catenazzi 2015 ). For example birds influence carbon cycling through insectivory and seed dispersal, yet their contribution to urban carbon budgets remains almost entirely unquantified (Lerman et al. 2021 ). Third, existing studies rarely control for confounding factors that could spuriously inflate observed biodiversity–carbon correlations. Green area, urbanization intensity, and climatic variables all correlate with both biodiversity and carbon stocks; failing to account for them risks attributing to biodiversity effects that are actually driven by green space expansion or favorable growing conditions (Wu 2014 ). Fourth, most research has been conducted in temperate cities of Europe and North America; comparable evidence from Asian megacities which differ substantially in development trajectory, planning history, and species pools is sparse (Anucharn et al. 2025 ). Haidian District's combination of high-technology development, historic gardens, and natural mountain ecosystems represents an urban type deserving dedicated empirical attention. Methodologies Study area Haidian District (39°53′–40°09′N, 116°03′–116°23′E) is located in northwestern Beijing, covering an area of 431 km 2 . As China's foremost center of knowledge economy and technological innovation—home to Peking University, Tsinghua University, and the Zhongguancun Science Park—Haidian has undergone rapid urbanization since the 1990 s while simultaneously preserving a remarkable network of historic gardens and natural ecosystems. The district's urbanization rate reached 97.5% by 2014 and has remained stable throughout the study period, indicating that the region has entered a post-urbanization phase characterized by quality improvement rather than extensive expansion (Li et al. 2023 ). The near-constancy of urbanization rate reflects not an ecological plateau but rather the exhaustion of available land resources for expansion in this highly urbanized district—a constraint that shifts the focus from spatial expansion to ecological quality enhancement. The district's ecological matrix comprises three interconnected landscape types: (1) the Xishan mountain range in the west, functioning as a regional ecological barrier and water source; (2) the "Three Hills and Five Gardens" historical park system, including the Summer Palace, the Old Summer Palace, and Fragrant Hills Park, which preserve mature forests, ancient trees, and complex wetlands of high cultural and ecological value (Liu et al. 2025 ; Barthel et al. 2013 ); and (3) the northern agricultural-ecological transition zone, featuring Cui Lake Wetland, Daoxiang Lake, and remnant rice paddies that support diverse bird and amphibian communities (Schwarz et al. 2017 ). Fig. 1 Full size image Distribution of 40 permanent monitoring quadrats and land cover types in Haidian District, Beijing, 2025 (source: drawn by the authors) Permanent quadrat monitoring network The core empirical foundation of this study is a systematic monitoring network of 40 permanent 100 m × 100 m quadrats, established in 2014 and surveyed annually through 2025. The quadrats cover four main habitats in the entire Haidian District, including forest, wetland, urban park, and residential green space, distributed in all 22 administrative regions at the township level, with at least one sample plot set up in each township level administrative region. Locations were selected using stratified random sampling based on three criteria: vegetation type (forest, shrubland, grassland, wetland, artificial green space); human disturbance gradient defined as high, medium, or low as measured by proximity to roads and population density; and land use history including historic gardens, modern parks, residential green spaces, agricultural fields, and natural woodlands. This design ensures representative coverage of the full spectrum of urban land use intensities and vegetation conditions present in the district (Yi et al. 2023 ). Each quadrat is permanently marked at its four corners with buried PVC pipes and GPS-recorded coordinates. Within each quadrat, five 10 m × 10 m vegetation subplots where one is at center and four are at corners, each containing nested 1 m × 1 m herb-layer quadrats and 2 m × 2 m shrub quadrats when applicable. The same subplot positions were resurveyed every year between May and September, coinciding with peak growing season. Biodiversity data collection and index calculation The biodiversity monitoring protocol comprised four complementary taxonomic surveys, each employing standardized methods to ensure data comparability across years. To address the methodological discontinuity introduced by eDNA and acoustic monitoring technologies after 2023, we included "Observation Method" as a dummy variable in all statistical models to statistically isolate systematic bias from equipment sensitivity upgrades. Plant diversity Plant diversity was assessed within each 10 m × 10 m vegetation subplot by identifying all vascular plant species to species level. Abundance was recorded as percent cover using Braun-Blanquet cover-abundance classes (Dengler et al. 2008 ). Tree species additionally had diameter at breast height, height, and crown dimensions recorded to enable biomass estimation. Plant diversity was quantified using the Shannon–Wiener index (Shannon & Weaver 1949 ). $${H}_{plant}{\prime}\text{=}-{\sum}_{i= \text{1} }^{S}{p}_{i}{\text{ln}}{p}_{i}$$ where S = total number of species in the quadrat and p_i = relative abundance (proportional cover) of species i. To account for historical data reconstruction prior to the formal establishment of the monitoring network, the researchers cross-validated quadrat-derived species lists against herbarium records from the Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and historical vegetation surveys conducted by the Beijing Municipal Forestry and Parks Bureau between 2014 and 2023. Discrepancies were resolved through expert consultation, and a 5% uncertainty margin was assigned to 2014 baseline estimates (Fig. 2 ). Fig. 2 Full size image Distribution of green types in Haidian District in 2025 (source: drawn by the authors; land cover classification derived from Sentinel-2 MSI imagery at 10 m resolution) Bird diversity Bird diversity was surveyed along 22 fixed 5 km transects covering all quadrats and surrounding habitats (Jones 2008 ). Transects were walked at a constant speed of 1.5–2.0 km/h during the two hours following sunrise (06:00–08:00) and the two hours preceding sunset (16:00–18:00), coinciding with peak avian activity periods (the dawn chorus and evening activity peak). Surveys were conducted twice annually: once during spring migration/breeding season (March–May) and once during autumn migration (September–November). All birds seen or heard within 100 m were recorded, along with detection distance and time of observation. To complement visual surveys, 40 autonomous acoustic recorders (AudioMoth, Open Acoustic Devices) were deployed across the quadrat network from 2021 onward. Recorders were mounted on tree trunks at 1.5 m height and programmed to record for 30 min at dawn (06:00–06:30) and dusk (18:00–18:30) daily during survey periods. Audio files were processed using BirdNET neural network classifier (version 2.4) with species identifications retained when confidence exceeded 0.7 and subsequently verified by expert ornithologists (Kahl et al. 2021 ). Acoustic monitoring proved particularly valuable for detecting secretive or nocturnal species (e.g., owls) that are consistently undercounted by visual transect methods. Bird diversity was quantified using the G–F index (Jansen 1997 ). $${D}_{GF}=1-\frac{{\sum}_{j=1}^{m}\left({D}_{{G}_{j}}-{D}_{{F}_{j}}\right)}{{\sum}_{j=1}^{m}{D}_{{G}_{j}}}$$ Amphibian diversity Amphibian diversity was surveyed during the active breeding season (April–September) through night-time visual encounter surveys (20:00–24:00) along shorelines of all water bodies within 500 m of each quadrat (Brooks et al. 2024 ). Surveys used headlamps and followed systematic transects at water's edge, with all amphibians encountered (including larvae observed in shallow water) identified to species and counted. From 2024 onward, environmental DNA metabarcoding supplemented visual surveys to improve detection of rare or secretive species (Deiner et al. 2017 ). Amphibian diversity was quantified using the Shannon–Wiener index. $${H}_{amphib}{\prime}=-{\sum}_{i=1}^{S}{p}_{i}\mathit{ln}{p}_{i}$$ Soil fauna diversity Soil fauna diversity was sampled through five soil cores per quadrat extracted biannually (May and September). Specimens were extracted using modified Tullgren funnels over 72 h at 35 °C and identified to order or family level. From 2023 onward, eDNA metabarcoding was incorporated to increase taxonomic resolution (Taberlet et al. 2018 ). Soil fauna diversity was quantified using the Shannon–Wiener index (Phillips et al. 2019 ). Comprehensive biodiversity index (BD) To integrate the four taxonomic groups, we constructed a Biodiversity Index following the ecosystem multifunctionality framework (Hector & Bagchi 2007 ; Manning et al. 2018 ). The BD index comprises three weighted components: $$BD={w}_{B}\times \left(B{D}_{plant}+B{D}_{animal}\right)+{w}_{E}\times EQ+{w}_{F}\times EF$$ The weights were determined using the entropy weight method, a fully objective statistical weighting approach based on the 12-year panel data. This yielded: w_B = 0.38 (biotic groups), w_E = 0.31 (Ecosystem Quality), and w_F = 0.31 (Ecological Function) (Lu et al. 2023 ; Mitsch et al. 2015 ). The sensitivity analysis table for weight calculations could be seen in Supplementary Materials (Table S1 ) as it does not represent the main findings of this study. The BD index was calculated annually for each quadrat, then used in the GLMMs with quadrat as a random effect. Carbon sink estimation Total urban carbon sink capacity was estimated using a multi-source integrated approach combining remote sensing, field inventory, and model-simulated data. The research team used Landsat-8 OLI (30 m resolution, 2014–2023) and Sentinel-2 MSI (10 m resolution, 2020–2025) imagery to calculate the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) for each year during peak growing season (July–August). NDVI was then converted to carbon density using the Beijing-specific NDVI–carbon density model calibrated against 118 field plots across central Beijing (Wang et al. 2026 ). $${C}_{density}=a\times NDVI+b$$ where a and b are local calibration coefficients (a = 48.3, b = − 12.7, R 2 = 0.76). Total carbon sink was calculated as: $${Y}_{RS}=\sum \left({C}_{density,i}\times Are{a}_{i}\right)$$ where i indexes land cover types. Every two years (2014, 2016, 2018, 2020, 2022, 2024), the researchers conducted destructive biomass sampling in a subset of 10 of the 40 permanent quadrats, rotating among quadrats to avoid repeated disturbance. Within each quadrat, we harvested all aboveground biomass from three 1 m × 1 m subplots, weighed fresh biomass, and calculated dry weight after drying at 70 °C for 72 h. Allometric equations for tree species were applied to estimate aboveground tree biomass from DBH and height measurements (Fang et al. 2001 ): $$AG{B}_{tree}=0.55\times DB{H}^{2.12}$$ Soil organic carbon (SOC) was measured at the same biennial interval from three soil cores (0–30 cm depth) per quadrat, with samples air-dried, sieved (2 mm), and analyzed for total carbon content using an elemental analyzer (Elementar Vario EL III). SOC stock was calculated as SOC concentration × bulk density × depth. Final annual carbon sink estimates (Y) were derived by integrating remote sensing and field data through a spatiotemporal statistical model (Jeyanny et al. 2026 ). Model validation against 25 eddy covariance flux tower sites in the Beijing region yielded an R 2 of 0.93 and RMSE of ± 1.8 tCO 2 · ha⁻ 1 · yr⁻ 1 , confirming adequate estimation accuracy (Pan et al. 2011 ). Detailed spatial distribution of calibration plots, species-specific allometric equations, and deep soil carbon sampling protocols are provided in Supplementary Materials ( S2 – S5 ). Regression models To address the spatiotemporal panel structure of our data and avoid the overfitting problems associated with aggregating 40 quadrats into 12 annual observations, we employed Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs) with quadrat and year as random effects. All models were estimated using the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015 ) in R version 4.3.1. Model 1 (Disaggregated taxonomic diversity): Total carbon sink (Y_ijt) was regressed on the four individual diversity indices plus control variables: $$\begin{array}{l}\begin{array}{c}Y_{ijt}=\beta_0+\beta_1H_{plant,ijt}'\\+\beta_2GF_{bird,ijt}+\beta_3H_{amphib,ijt}'\\+\beta_4H_{soil,ijt}'+\beta_5Urban_t\\+\beta_6Green_t+\beta_7Temp_t\\+\beta_8RH_t+\beta_9Method_t+\alpha_i+\gamma_j+\epsilon_{ijt}\end{array}\end{array}$$ Model 2 (Comprehensive Biodiversity Index): Total carbon sink was regressed on the comprehensive BD index plus control variables: $$\begin{array}{l}\begin{array}{l}Y_{ijt}=\beta_0+\beta_1BD_{ijt}+\beta_2Urban_t+\beta_3Green_t+\beta_4Temp_t\\+\beta_5RH_t+\beta_6Method_t+\alpha_i+\gamma_j+\epsilon_{ijt}\end{array}\end{array}$$ where i indexes quadrat (1–40), j indexes year (2014–2025), α_i is the quadrat random effect, γ_j is the year random effect, and Method_t is a dummy variable indicating whether the observation was made using pre − 2023 or post-2023 detection methods. Variance inflation factors remained below 3.5 for all predictors. Model comparison was conducted using AIC and BIC criteria to assess whether composite BD measures outperform single-taxon indicators. To further explore potential non-linear relationships, we also fitted Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) using the mgcv package in R as a sensitivity check. Results Biodiversity dynamics 2014–2025 Over the twelve-year study period, Haidian District's biodiversity exhibited sustained recovery across all four taxonomic groups (Fig. 3 ). Plant species richness increased from approximately 800 species in 2014 to 1,082 species in 2025 (35% gain). The proportion of native plant species rose from 58 to 69%, driven by policies promoting "site-appropriate tree species" and "near-natural reforestation" (Yi et al. 2023 ). Invasive species declined from 38 actively managed species to 30. Fig. 3 Full size image Variation trend of biological species in Haidian District in recent 12 years (source: drawn by the authors) Bird diversity showed even more dramatic recovery. Cumulative observed bird species increased from 126 to 212 (68% increase). However, the sharp increase after 2023 partly reflects improved detection efficiency from acoustic monitoring rather than purely ecological recovery—a methodological consideration addressed by the "Observation Method" dummy variable in our statistical models. Particularly noteworthy was the return of wetland-dependent species, including the relict gull (Ichthyaetus relictus), National Grade I protected species, and yellow-breasted bunting (Emberiza aureola), IUCN Critically Endangered, recorded as stopover migrants at Cui Lake Wetland (Fig. 4 ). Fig. 4 Full size image Some rare bird species captured during field investigations in 2025 (source: photographed by Yukun Zhang in Haidian, Beijing) Amphibian species richness tripled from 8 to 21 species. The recovery was punctuated by the documented return of the oriental fire-bellied toad (Bombina orientalis) to the Jingmi Diversion Canal in 2024——the first confirmed record of this highly pollution-sensitive species in the Beijing plain area in nearly three decades (Fig. 5 ). Soil fauna taxonomic richness increased from approximately 30 classes to 63 classes at family level. Acari (mites) and Collembola (springtails) initially dominated, but as soil organic matter accumulated and management practices shifted toward reduced disturbance, predatory and detritivorous groups increased substantially, (Fan et al. 2023 ). Summary of hypothesis tests Hypothesis H 1 (BD is positively associated with carbon sinks) is supported. The BD index coefficient was positive, and highly significant in the GLMM framework. Hypothesis H 2 (Consumer diversity improves model fit) is supported but nuanced. Plant and bird diversity made significant contributions. However, the BD index substantially outperformed the plant-only model based on AIC comparison, suggesting that the integrated effect of multiple functional groups exceeds the sum of individual contributions. Hypothesis H 3 (Marginal biodiversity contribution increases over time) is partially supported. The increasing marginal contribution of biodiversity to carbon sinks is consistent with the quality transition hypothesis, but this pattern should be interpreted cautiously as post-2023 increases partly reflect improved detection methods rather than purely ecological change. Discussion Associations underlying the biodiversity–carbon relationship The observed positive association between biodiversity and carbon sink is consistent with three potential mechanistic pathways. Critically, the current data support correlational inference for these mechanisms; causal validation would require experimental manipulation. We therefore frame these as hypotheses for future research rather than verified conclusions. Structural complementarity: the role of vegetation diversity The observed increase in carbon density substantially exceeded what would be expected from green area expansion alone, suggesting that vegetation compositional shifts may have contributed to carbon gains. Mixed-species stands of Pinus tabuliformis (Chinese pine), Acer truncatum (purpleblow maple), and Robinia pseudoacacia (black locust) showed superior carbon density compared to pure stands in our field observations. This pattern is consistent with the complementarity effect from biodiversity–ecosystem functioning theory (Tilman et al. 2014 ), in which different species exploit different resource niches and facilitate each other's growth (Fornara & Tilman 2008 ). However, we did not directly measure resource partitioning or facilitation mechanisms. The superior performance of mixed stands could also be explained by selection effects (i.e., the inclusion of inherently high-performing species) or by site-specific factors such as soil quality. Therefore, this interpretation remains speculative without experimental validation. Trophic regulation: a hypothesis for future research The significant positive coefficient for bird diversity is consistent with the hypothesis that higher trophic levels may influence carbon dynamics. Insectivorous birds such as the great spotted woodpecker (Dendrocopos major) and the great tit (Parus major) have been documented to reduce herbivorous insect damage in other studies (Narango et al. 2018 ; Mäntylä et al. 2011 ). However, we did not collect intermediate trophic data (i.e., insect abundance or herbivory rates) in this study. Therefore, the researcher team cannot confirm that bird diversity could influence carbon sinks through trophic cascades. The trophic regulation mechanism remains a hypothesis requiring direct testing through experimental exclusion studies or systematic entomological sampling. We acknowledge this as a significant limitation of the current study (see Sect. " Limitations ") and recommend it as a priority for future research. The non-significance of amphibian diversity in the aggregated model is explained by habitat-stratified regressions: amphibian diversity was significant only in wetland habitats (where amphibians are concentrated), not in forest or urban park habitats. This context-dependent effect suggests that amphibian contributions to carbon dynamics may be spatially constrained (Guerra et al. 2021 ). Climatic resilience: the insurance effect The differential response of high- versus low-diversity systems to the 2022 drought provides observational evidence consistent with the insurance hypothesis (Isbell et al. 2015 ). High-diversity forest stands maintained 88–92% of their pre-drought carbon density, whereas low-diversity areas suffered 25–32% losses. This pattern is consistent with the interpretation that functional diversity provides a buffer against climatic stress (Kang et al. 2025 ). The quality transition: from green area quantity to biodiversity quality The progressive strengthening of the biodiversity–carbon association as green area expansion slowed suggests a "quality transition" in mature urban regions. In the early years (2014–2017), green coverage expanded at approximately 0.5 percentage points annually, and carbon sinks increased commensurately. As Haidian approached its spatial limits for further green area expansion (growth rates declining to 0.1 percentage points annually after 2022), carbon sink growth did not stall; rather, it continued, increasingly associated with improvements in biodiversity quality. This insight parallels findings from other urban systems. Ariluoma et al. ( 2024 ) similarly concluded that systematic measures to enhance plant diversity, growing conditions, and maintenance practices could unlock substantial carbon–botanical diversity co-benefits, even in the absence of area expansion. However, while biodiversity quality is a meaningful factor associated with continued carbon enhancement after expansion plateaus, the statement that it is "the primary driver" requires moderation. A formal variance decomposition or relative weight analysis would be needed to quantify the relative importance of different factors in future studies. Implications for urban planning and policy The empirical findings support a reorientation of urban green space policy in high-urbanization contexts toward what we term the "Q 2 framework": quality (biodiversity) and quantity (area) as complementary but non‑substitutable policy levers. Biodiversity-sensitive carbon accounting Current urban carbon accounting frameworks treat all green space as functionally equivalent, assigning carbon credits based solely on area and vegetation type (Song and Gang 2026 ). Our results suggest that this approach may systematically underestimate the carbon benefits of high-quality restoration. A high-diversity, multi-strata forest may sequester substantially more carbon than a low-diversity plantation on the same area, yet receives no additional carbon credit. Designing "biodiversity premium" mechanisms for carbon markets could align economic incentives with ecological outcomes. Functional complementarity in species selection The carbon advantage of mixed-species over single-species stands implies that species selection policies should explicitly target functional complementarity. In Beijing's context, species combinations that pair deep-rooted trees with shallow-rooted species, and nitrogen-fixing with non-fixers, appear to maximize resource use complementarity.. Ecological corridors for trophic interactions The significant coefficient for bird diversity underscores the importance of maintaining habitat connectivity for mobile consumers (Haddad et al. 2015 ). The "Xishan–Wenyu River Ecological Corridor," completed in 2022, increased regional connectivity and was associated with a measurable increase in bird-mediated pest control services. Biodiversity in climate adaptation The drought-insurance effect observed for high-diversity forest stands suggests that biodiversity may serve as a climate adaptation asset. Urban climate adaptation plans that currently focus on gray infrastructure should incorporate biodiversity-based adaptation as a complementary strategy (Miao et al. 2024 ). Limitations Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is restricted to a single district of Beijing; generalizing findings to other cities will require replication across different climatic, cultural, and planning contexts. Second, the twelve-year time series, while longer than most urban ecological panel studies, may still be insufficient to capture long-term, slow-moving processes such as soil carbon accumulation and old-growth forest development. Third, the accelerated increase in biodiversity indicators observed after 2023 partly reflects methodological improvements that increased detection efficiency. Although we addressed this statistically by including "Observation Method" as a dummy variable, we cannot definitively separate true ecological recovery from improved detection. Fourth, as an observational study, this research can only establish statistical associations, not causal relationships. The mechanisms we discuss—structural complementarity, trophic regulation, and climate resilience—remain hypotheses requiring direct experimental testing. Fifth, and most critically, we lacked intermediate trophic data (insect abundance, herbivory rates) to test the trophic cascade mechanism directly. Bird exclusion experiments and systematic entomological sampling would be required to confirm causal pathways. We recommend these as priorities for future research. Sixth, the non-significance of urbanization rate as a control variable likely reflects its near-constancy over the study period rather than a genuine absence of effect. Future studies should incorporate more temporally dynamic socioeconomic confounders. Seventh, while β diversity is an important component of biodiversity, our study focused primarily on α diversity. Future research should investigate whether changes in species composition among plots (β diversity) independently contribute to carbon dynamics. Comparison with prior literature These findings extend the biodiversity–ecosystem functioning literature in three directions. First, we demonstrate that positive biodiversity–function associations can be detected in highly managed urban green spaces, consistent with Fan et al. ( 2023 ) but extending their work by examining carbon flux as an outcome variable. Second, the longitudinal design provides observational support for the insurance hypothesis in urban contexts, echoing mechanisms documented in natural systems (Isbell et al. 2015 ). Third, the disaggregated model revealing significant independent effects of bird diversity adds to the growing literature linking urban avian diversity to ecosystem services (Lerman et al. 2021 ; Stucchi et al. 2025 ). Conclusions This study investigated whether biodiversity—as a measure of ecological quality beyond simple vegetation cover—is independently associated with urban carbon sequestration capacity. Using twelve years of panel data from Haidian District, Beijing, including 40 permanent monitoring quadrats covering all 22 administrative subdistricts, we constructed a comprehensive Biodiversity Index integrating plant, bird, amphibian, and soil fauna diversity with ecosystem quality and ecological function indicators. GLMMs controlling for urbanization rate, green coverage rate, temperature, and humidity—all of which remained stable over the 2014–2025 period—yielded three principal conclusions. First, the BD index positively and significantly associated with total urban carbon sink capacity. Green coverage and other control variables did not reach statistical significance, indicating that in a mature urban context where green area expansion has slowed, biodiversity quality is an important factor associated with continued carbon enhancement. Second, plant diversity and bird diversity were significant independent predictors. The BD index substantially outperforms plant-only models, suggesting that the integrated effect of multiple functional groups exceeds the sum of individual contributions. However, the researchers emphasize that these are statistical associations, not causal relationships. Third, the marginal contribution of biodiversity to carbon sinks appears to increased over time as green area expansion slows, consistent with a "quality transition" hypothesis. This pattern should be interpreted cautiously as post-2023 increases partly reflect methodological improvements. These findings carry direct policy implications for cities in late-stage urbanization. Carbon reduction protocols that ignore biodiversity may systematically undervalue high-quality restoration relative to area expansion. Species selection for urban forestry should emphasize functional complementarity. Ecological corridors that support mobile consumers may enhance carbon outcomes. And biodiverse green space may provide resilience to extreme events. At a broader level, this study contributes to the emerging synthesis of urban biodiversity conservation (Cohen-Shacham et al. 2019 ) and climate change mitigation (Jayakrishnan et al. 2026 ) as mutually reinforcing rather than competing objectives. In the high-density, high-green-coverage context of Haidian District, biodiversity restoration is associated with urban carbon sinks and, by extension, livability—suggesting that for mature urban regions, the pathway to low-carbon development may runs through ecological quality, not merely ecological quantity.
