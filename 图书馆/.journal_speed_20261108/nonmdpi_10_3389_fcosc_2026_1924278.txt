Department of Plant Science and Crop Protection, University of Nairobi, Nairobi, Kenya See more Article metrics View details Abstract Introduction: Climate change is increasingly affecting pastoral communities in sub-Saharan Africa through more frequent and severe droughts and floods, threatening livelihoods, food security, and mental well-being. Pastoral women and children are particularly vulnerable because of their dependence on livestock for nutrition, income, and cultural identity. This study assessed the psychosocial and livelihood impacts of climate change among pastoral women in Kenya and evaluated community-based interventions to strengthen resilience and adaptive capacity. Materials and methods: A mixed-method baseline survey was conducted among 1,138 women in Kajiado, Narok, and Taita Taveta counties following severe drought in 2022 - 2024. Data were collected using structured questionnaires, focus group discussions, and Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) measurements for 941 children below 10 years. In partnership with the county government, 401 women from 18 women's groups and 342 learners from five schools were trained in climate-smart mushroom and vegetable production. The impact of the interventions was evaluated among 194 women and 204 learners. Results: Most baseline survey respondents (64%) were aged below 35 years, 74.3% depended on casual labor, and 13% had no source of income. Narok recorded the highest proportion of children (33%) with poor nutritional status. During the drought, 347 mothers reported being unable to feed their children, while 86% received no food assistance. The most reported impacts were mental health problems (58%), resource conflicts (45%), and starvation (40%). More than half (58%) expressed a need for mental health intervention and support. The introduced farming practices were new to most participants. Among the women trained, 87% rated mushrooms as highly acceptable and 45% incorporated them into household diets. Among learners, 83% participated in training and 39% practiced mushroom cultivation at school. Discussion and conclusion: The interventions improved dietary diversity, promoted alternative livelihoods, and strengthened community resilience. Participants also reported increased hope, social cohesion, and confidence. These findings demonstrate that expanding livelihood options and demonstrating alternative ways to grow food and improve nutrition can reduce the collective trauma of communities already burdened by poverty, demographic pressure, early marriage, and pregnancy, while strengthening their resilience the next time prolonged drought strikes. Introduction Climate change continues to reshape African ecosystems and human systems (Laibuni, 2020), with the greatest harm falling on countries with the fewest resources, resilience and adaptive capacity (Nyambariga et al., 2023; Zwane et al., 2024; Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), 2022). In Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), climate change now ranks among the most significant environmental, social, and economic threats (Ringler et al., 2010) depressing agricultural production and undermining food security and livelihoods. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), 2022) reports that climate change in Africa has increased drought and flood cycles across Africa. These are increasingly unpredictable, severe, and prolonged, driving acute food insecurity through crop failure and livestock loss, worsening water scarcity, displacement and malnutrition. Marginalised pastoralist households in arid and semi-arid regions, bear the brunt of these impacts. Children, older people, pregnant women, and those with other vulnerabilities are especially affected. Practitioners working in communities increasingly report mental health consequences from these cumulative shocks, although the supporting evidence base for Africa remains limited (Lewins et al., 2025). In Kenya, 36% of the population live below the poverty line (International Monetary Fund (IMF), 2020) and more than 5 million people faced chronic acute food insecurity during the peak of the 2023 drought crisis (Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), 2023). Climate change impacts on vulnerable communities affected by other stressors such as poverty and weak institutional support has devastating consequences on food security and livelihoods (Connolly-Boutin and Smit, 2016). The country’s Arid and Semi-Arid Lands (ASALs), account for roughly 80% of its land mass and are home to about 30% of its population. They are among the poorest and most climate-exposed regions (Maino and Emrullahu, 2022) with over 4 million people in ASALs facing severe food insecurity by March 2023 - the peak of the recent drought (Connolly-Boutin and Smit, 2016). Malnutrition remains entrenched, with at least one in four children chronically undernourished and rates of stunting and wasting reaching 46% in some ASAL countries (World Bank, 2018; National Drought Management Authority (NDMA), 2023; World Bank, 2021). For pastoralist households, livestock is both the primary livelihood and the source of household staple diet. Reduced numbers, unhealthy or dying animals result directly to less milk and meat, with a decline in the nutritional status and well-being of women and children (United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), 2023). Climate change strikes at the centre of this dependency: prolonged heat, consecutive failed rains, worsening water scarcity, and flooding depress crop and livestock production, bringing hunger and the threat of famine. For example, more than three million livestock died in Kenya’s 2022–2024 drought alone (Connolly-Boutin and Smit, 2016). As water and pasture disappear, men increasingly migrate with their animals in search of both leaving women to manage households alone, while competition over dwindling resources fuels conflict between communities whose livelihoods depend on rain-fed agriculture and pasture (United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), 2007; Ofori et al., 2021). Conflicts drive livestock rustling, displacement and further anxiety and trauma. This patten of vulnerability is consistent with SSA’s broader exposure to climate change (Thompson et al., 2010). Mental health remains the most neglected dimension of this crisis. Even where the need is recognized it is often left unaddressed in marginalised communities (Charlson et al., 2021; Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), 2022; World Health Organization (WHO), 2022; Yen et al., 2024). In the interdependent communities in which pastoralists live, trauma is experienced collectively as well as individually. Women and children – who are most vulnerable - are usually affected severely. We draw on the concept of solastalgia, the distress caused by negative environmental change in one’s home environment while one remains within it (Albrecht et al., 2007), as a guiding lens for interpreting the psychosocial impacts documented in this study. This term captures the grief of communities who experience the erosion of a familiar landscape and livelihood without being displaced from it. To help build the resilience and adaptive capacity of communities already under this strain, we conducted a baseline survey of 1,138 pastoral women in Kajiado, Narok and Taita Taveta counties to document collective trauma and other impacts of the cumulative, extreme drought and famine events experienced between 2022 - 2024. Over repeated visits before and during the training, the research team spent considerable time listening to individuals and groups about their experiences following the loss of their livestock. Most people were traumatized by the scale of the disaster and unsure how to proceed given their limited livelihood options, their limited experience of agriculture, and few means of obtaining alternative subsistence. This process of relationship building and of trying to understand the situation from the community’s own perspective, helped co-develop solutions, build trust and establish an effective partnership between communities, local officials and the research team, generating the evidence on climate distress and trauma reported here. Lack of food was the concern most often and most urgently raised by women. Working with them, the research team identified strategic, low-cost interventions that could be implemented quickly. Mushroom cultivation stood out as one potential way forward. Rich in nutrients, they can be grown without soil or dependence on rainfall, using recycled dry matter and agricultural residues (Akter et al., 2022). A crop cycle of about two months allows year-round production, while post-harvest waste can be composted to support vegetable gardens. Introducing mushroom cultivation therefore became more than a nutrition intervention. It provided a positive entry point for building trust with climate-affected communities, giving the associated training an immediate sense of community cohesion, hope, and a safe focus for further discussion. We trained 401 pastoral women and youth from 18 women’s farmer groups, and 342 learners from five secondary schools across the three counties, to grow and use low-cost, climate-smart methods for producing nutritious mushrooms and vegetables. We co-developed a process centred on collective action, hands on training in small and large groups, and the positive, problem-solving strengths of pastoralist culture. This approach helped participants build the confidence and courage to work collaboratively toward a clear, shared goal: cultivating climate-resilient, nutrient-dense foods as alternatives (or in addition) to the traditional, climate-sensitive staples of milk and meat, thereby strengthening household nutrition and social resilience in the face of climate stress. The specific objectives of this study were to: (i) assess the psychosocial and mental health impacts of the 2022–2024 drought on pastoral women in Kajiado, Narok and Taita Taveta counties; (ii) determine the nutritional status of children under 10 years and (iii) evaluate the relevance, coherence and early effectiveness of community-based, climate-smart mushroom and vegetable production interventions in strengthening household food and nutritional security and resilience. In so doing, it was hoped to create a positive cycle where new livelihood options not only provided food but reduced individual and collective fears for the future. This in turn would enable people to take more control of a situation that was previously out of their control, further supporting positive mental health. Conceptual framework Climate change increases the vulnerability of pastoral households through recurrent droughts, famine, crop failure, loss of livestock and water scarcity, resulting in adverse environmental, livelihood, nutritional, resource conflicts and psychosocial outcomes. Community-based adaptive interventions, including climate-smart food production, capacity building, and partnerships with local stakeholders, are hypothesized to improve food security, diversify livelihoods, enhance mental well-being, and strengthen adaptive capacity, ultimately contributing to resilient pastoral communities. Materials and methods Description of the study sites Baseline surveys were conducted in three counties: Kajiado, Narok and Taita Taveta in Kenya (Figure 2). These semi-arid counties face increasingly frequent and prolonged droughts (Laibuni, 2020; Nyambariga et al., 2023; Zwane et al., 2024). During drought periods, the communities in these counties face several challenges that threaten their livelihoods and cultural identity. In 2022 and 2023 these counties were classified as an ‘alert drought phase’ occasioned by late onset and poor rainfall performance following consecutive failed seasons. Figure 2 Map of Kenya showing the three semi-arid counties where baseline survey was conducted and the number of respondents in each county (Map credit: ChatGPt). A key factor worsening the drought conditions is the shift from community land management. The dissolution of community group ranches into private land has led to fragmentation of rangelands and increased crop planting, homogenizing the landscape thereby reducing ecosystem adaptability to climate stress and increased natural resources degradation. The key impacts of drought are: (i) water scarcity following the drying up of water sources such as rivers and water pans; (ii) large scale livestock death with those that survive being emaciated and producing little or no milk. This removes people’s staple source of nutrition; (iii) food insecurity with high levels of malnutrition among children under five years and nursing mothers; (iv) rise in disease incidences due to lack of water and poor sanitation; (v) migration and resource conflict (human and wildlife) where herders move to seek pasture. In the process, intercommunity fights arise because of competition for declining resources, while wildlife conflicts intensify where animals invade farms and destroy crops; (vi) coping mechanisms such as charcoal burning, selling of land, livestock and household properties further exacerbate the community’s poverty levels. These impacts, together with the land use changes and limited alternatives noted above, mean that collective anxiety in these communities is often high. Worries for the future are daily topics of conversation. A single destructive event can tip already stressed communities into distress. Whereas all three counties experienced a food and nutrition crisis during the 2022–2024 drought (Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), 2022), the pathways were different. In Kajiado, for example, communities suffered a loss of livestock and milk resulting in the collapse of their pastoral life. In Narok, the community lost livestock, milk and wildlife; a vital resource of income, while in Taita Taveta, the community faced crop failure, livestock deaths and human-wildlife conflicts as wildlife encroached farms and destroyed crops. Sample size calculation and selection of study participants Sample was calculated based on an average of 30% incidence of malnutrition in arid and semi-arid counties in Kenya (Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), IPC, 2023) as determined using a formula by Daniel (1999). Setting the power at 80% and a two-sided test with α = 0.05, a sample size of 322 in each county giving a minimum sample size (n) of 966 in the 3 study counties was reached by using a modified Fisher’s population proportion - sample size calculator for the sample size (n) where Zα/2 was the critical value of normal distribution at α/2 (confidence level of 95%; α was 0.05 and the critical value was 1.96); Margin of Error (MOE) was = 5%; p was sample proportion = 30%, N was an infinite population size. Consenting households led or co-led by adult women were recruited to assess impacts of climate change. Children below 10 years in each consenting household were assessed for nutritional status. For adaptive capacity training, women groups were purposively identified through the County agricultural department. Participating schools were recommended by the county education office. Quantitative and qualitative baseline survey data collection A quantitative and qualitative baseline survey was conducted between May 2024 and January 2025 to understand and document the traumatic and cumulative impacts of climate change on pastoral communities in the three counties. Using a descriptive questionnaire (Supplementary File 1), data were collected from 1,138 women respondents - 200 from Kajiado, 455 from Narok and 483 from Taita Taveta. The socioeconomic and demographic status of the participants was recorded. Determination of the nutritional status of children Malnutrition in children <10 years was assessed by measuring the mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) (World Health Organization (WHO) and United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), 2009); Mehta et al., 2013). A colour-coded MUAC band was used to measure arm circumference midway between shoulder and elbow. If the girth of a participant’s arm was within the green part of the band, nutritional status was recorded as ‘good’. Measurements within an orange or yellow part were categorised as ‘moderate’ or ‘bad’ health respectively. A measurement within the yellow part indicated malnutrition. The red portion of the band indicated severe malnourished status and risk of death. Implementation of adaptive capacity building training activities Baseline survey data indicated that lack of food and malnutrition was a recurring trauma for many households. As an intervention to improve household food and nutritional security, 401 women farmers from 18 women’s farmer groups, and 342 school learners from five schools (two from Kajiado, three from Taita Taveta) were trained on growing and using low-cost, climate-smart technologies and materials to produce mushrooms and/or vegetables between February 2024 and August 2025. Participants attended weekly, participatory training classes using the Field Farmer School approach (FFS - (https://www.fao.org/agriculture/ippm/programme/ffs-approach/en/). Lead women were selected and trained as facilitators who would train and guide other group members through a series of hands-on training activities. Key training activities included mushroom cultivation, substrate sterilization, seeding, growth monitoring, preparation, cooking and drying. The women and school learners were also trained on climate smart vertical garden installation and maintenance. Each activity was co-developed and well structured, with agreed procedures for action, observation, analysis, and decision-making so that the farmers could learn the ‘how’ and the ‘why’. Three women groups and two schools in Kajiado County were also trained on tree nursery seeding, tree seedling planting and reafforestation of bee forages. Project impact evaluation An evaluation was undertaken to assess the impact of interventions across the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development-Development Assistance Committee (OECD-DAC) criteria of relevance, coherence, effectiveness, efficiency, impact, and sustainability (OECD-DAC, 2002). The evaluation carried out between August and September 2025 employed a mixed-methods approach, including structured household interviews for farmer groups, school interviews, Focus Group Discussions (FGDs), one household survey questionnaire administered to the farmers and a different one for the learners. Survey data were collected using the Kobo Collect platform on smart phones and administered by trained enumerators. Level of awareness, knowledge and skills acquisition and utilisation of alternative diet of cultivated mushroom and vegetables as food supplement was assessed in 194 women households and 204 learners. The FGDs were conducted with the farmer participants using guides with key questions. Each FGD session had 8–12 participants selected at household level, while ensuring diversity regarding age, location, and experiences of the project. Discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and reviewed for accuracy prior to analysis. The transcripts were analyzed using the OECD-DAC thematic criteria of relevance, coherence, effectiveness, efficiency, impact, and sustainability. Two researchers independently coded the transcripts, with discrepancies resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Codes with similar meanings were grouped into broader categories, from which overarching themes and subthemes were iteratively developed and refined. Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently, and thematic saturation was considered achieved when no new codes or themes emerged from successive discussions. Data analysis Data cleaning and entry used SPSS version 21.0. Descriptive statistics including socioeconomic demographic characteristics of baseline survey respondents were obtained. A cross-tabulation analysis was used to determine the frequencies of traumatic experiences and negative impacts of climate change on the women. Across all three counties, nutritional status of children was qualitatively assessed as ‘good’, ‘moderate’ or ‘bad’. These were accorded as ‘healthy’ for ‘good’ responses and ‘unhealthy’ for ‘moderate’ or ‘bad’ responses. A similar analysis between the binary variable on previously known numbers of household malnutritional cases as reported by the mother was also determined. An asymptotic significance test (p < 0.05) was used to determine result significance. Taita Taveta and Narok Counties had the highest number of mothers with young <5 years old at 428 (45%) and 392 (41%) respectively. The main source of income for household food acquisition was casual labor (74% of respondents) while 13% of respondents did not engage in any income generating activities. Reported traumatic impacts of climate change Different types of trauma and adverse impacts attributed to climate change were recorded (Table 3). Interventions to build adaptive capacity and resilience among the pastoral women and youth To strengthen household food and nutritional security and build the community’s resilience and adaptation to climate change, a group of 401 farmers and 342 school learners from the three counties were trained in producing mushrooms and vegetables using climate-smart technologies. Of these, 136 women were from Taita Taveta county, with 125 from Kajiado and 140 from Narok. The women belonged to 18 women’s farmer groups spread across the three counties. However, only 11 groups were actively involved in mushroom and vegetable cultivation. The women groups were trained between May 2024 to July 2025. A total of 18 operational active mushroom cultivation units and 92 vegetable production units were established across farmer sites and in schools. The units produced an average of 825 kg of fresh mushrooms per quarter and an average of 1,650 kg of vegetables for all the 11 active groups. About 342 learners in schools were trained. From these, 152 learners in two schools were trained in both mushroom and vegetables while 190 learners in three schools were trained in vegetable cultivation only due to logistical constraints. The schools produced an average of 86 kg of fresh vegetables per month during a period of 12 months. Mushrooms were only actively produced in one school at an average of 10 kg of mushrooms per month. The five schools produced an estimated total of 377 kg of vegetables per month. In addition, three women groups in Kajiado were trained in bee keeping best practices including types of hives, establishment of bee forages, colony maintenance and apiary management. The groups have set up 25 Langstroth beehives and planted over 2,000 bee forage trees. Summative impact evaluation Impact evaluation of the implemented interventions was conducted on 194 trained women group members to assess the adoption of mushroom production practices, consumption patterns, nutrition knowledge, and perceived benefits. Most women respondents (47%; 91/194) were youthful between 18–37 years of age. Notably, there were 28 elderly participants aged between 58–97 years. Surveys for 204 trained school-going children were undertaken to assess knowledge gained, dietary habits, vegetable consumption, and nutrition knowledge. Among the 204 learners surveyed, 69 were from Kajiado and 135 from Taita Taveta - 62% female and 38% male. The learners ranged from primary school students in Grade 4–7 and high school students in Form 2-3. Six FGDs with women in two farmer groups in each of the three counties engaged in mushroom and vegetable production were conducted to explore experiences, benefits, challenges, and sustainability. Of the 194 farmers evaluated, 101 (52%) were from Kajiado, 46 (24%) from Narok and 47 (24%) from Taita Taveta. Among these, 174 belonged to a group that had been trained in mushroom and vegetable farming. They reported producing mushrooms and vegetable products in the group. The farmer groups produced collectively an average of 75 kg of mushrooms and 200 kg of vegetables per quarter which was shared for household consumption and the excess sold earning an average income of KES 2,710 ($20.93 USD) shillings per month. Among them, 89 respondents (46%) produced vegetables at household level and consumed vegetables at least three times a week and mushrooms collectively once a month. During FGDs, the groups reported that in some instances, they divided the produce amongst themselves for household consumption. The household mushroom consumption pattern showed that 174 (89%) of farmer respondents produced mushroom and vegetable products at group level and consumed mushrooms on average once per month at home. The mushrooms were consumed in several forms (fresh, dried and processed as porridge flour) with a preference for freshly cooked mushrooms. Findings from 122 respondents indicate that mushrooms were accepted by all family members. Forty-one respondents reported noticeable changes in family health and nutrition, including improved dietary diversity. Nine participants reported producing mushrooms at household level. The most common type of value addition undertaken by the groups was drying mushrooms using solar driers (carried out by 16 respondents). Twenty-three respondents reported selling surplus mushrooms to friends and relatives. Impact data revealed household dietary scores of the survey respondents. A total of 146 (76%) households used more than seven food groups the previous day indicating high dietary diversity and access to a balanced diet with cereals, vegetables, fruits and protein sources indicating food security. Twenty-three percent (45) of households scored a medium dietary diversity score of 4–6 with some inclusion of vegetables, pulses, or dairy; but still limited in animal-source foods. Only 1% (2 households) had a very low dietary score of between 0 and 3, implying their diet mainly consisted of cereals/staples; a poor nutrient diversity subjecting them to risk of malnutrition. The analysis of household Food Consumption Score (FCS) revealed that out of the 194 households surveyed, 27 households (about 14%) were placed within the “Poor Food Consumption” category, scoring between 0 and 28 while a smaller proportion, 14 households (about 7%), were within the ‘Borderline Food Consumption’ category, with FCS scores ranging from 28.5 to 42. Most households, 153 (78%), were within the “Acceptable Food Consumption” range, scoring above 42 (Figure 4). Mushroom and vegetable farming was widely regarded as more efficient, less risky, advantageous and less labor-intensive than livestock rearing, particularly during drought. The shared view among the farmer groups was that mushrooms and vegetables provided a sustainable, climate-resilient alternative to livestock. Divergence from this view emerged mainly in Taita Taveta, where some groups still viewed livestock as a critical fallback despite higher risks due to climate-related shocks. Discussion Compounded vulnerabilities: the climate change nexus The women in our sample were overwhelmingly young: most mothers were below 35 years of age with Taita Taveta and Narok recording the highest proportion of young mothers and children under five. Casual labor was the main source of income for 74% of respondents while 13% had no income-generating activity at all, and only 8% relied on livestock production, the community’s traditional livelihood. Many of these mothers had left school early, with low levels of formal education linked to unintended pregnancy or early marriage. Similar patterns have been reported elsewhere in Kenya’s pastoral counties. Adongo et al. (2024) found a mean family size of 5.2 in Samburu County, with married women accounting for 87% of caregivers at a mean age of 28.9 years, two-thirds of whom could not read or write. The same authors reported that vulnerability to climate change among pastoralists in Turkana was shaped by age, gender, education and disability, with fewer than 30% of women and men able to read and write, and women-headed and married households more vulnerable than those of men or youth. Taken together, these overlapping disadvantages; youth, interrupted schooling, early childbearing, and dependence on casual labor or a single climate-sensitive livelihood left women in Kajiado, Narok and Taita Taveta already under strain well before drought and livestock losses compounded it further. Nutritional crisis as an indicator of systemic institutional failure In this study, 55% of children were classified with ‘moderate’ or ‘bad’ nutritional status. Narok, recorded the highest proportion of children in the ‘bad’ category, and 24% of women overall reported a previous case of malnutrition in their household, rising to 44% in Kajiado. The clearest predisposing factors were going without food for one to two days a week, lack of lack of clean drinking water, poor dietary diversity, and the near-total absence of food aid during drought – all pointing to the inadequacy of emergency response structures. Similar factors influencing stunting, wasting and undernutrition among children aged 6–59 months have been reported in Marsabit, an arid County in Kenya, included child age and weight, source of drinking water, waste disposal and caregivers’ age, income, education, and hygiene practices (Lokuruka, 2020). Given the severe water scarcity in ASALs, water availability, quality, sanitation, and hygiene practices probably contribute to the malnutrition observed here as well. There were important county-level distinctions in children’s nutritional status. Narok had the highest proportion of children in ‘bad’ nutritional status (33%), compared with Kajiado (17%) and Taita Taveta (15%) even though Taita Taveta had the highest share of mothers reporting inability to feed their children. In 2022, malnutrition levels in parts of Narok were 17%, 22% and 22% for wasting, stunting and underweight respectively (Kotut, 2022). The malnutrition levels observed in our study may reflect different drought pathways in each county. In Kajiado, the pastoral system collapsed outright; in Narok, the loss of wildlife-based income compounded livestock losses; and in Taita Taveta, food insecurity stemmed mainly from crop failure, livestock deaths and human-wildlife conflict. These county-specific pathways, rather than a single uniform shock, produced the distinct patterns of household food insecurity observed. The Maasai community, who dominate Kajiado and Narok, have previously been reported to have poor nutritional status, regardless of socioecological changes such as sedentarization and livelihood diversification (Omolo and Mafongoya, 2019). Against this backdrop, it is notable that the empowered pastoral women and youth in our study readily took up the cultivation and consumption of mushroom and vegetables (Figure 4), despite the community’s traditional mycophobia, a cultural aversion to eating mushrooms. Cumulative climate impacts on the lives of pastoral women Famine, livestock loss and livestock diseases have previously been reported as the major climate change impacts on pastoralist communities in Narok (Korir, 2019) and Kajiado (Abdimajid et al., 2020) where loss of livestock to drought was rated the most significant vulnerability. Kenya’s ASALs, which hold roughly 70% of Kenya’s livestock and 30% of its population, mostly smallholder pastoralists (United Nations Development Organization (UNDO), 2013) are the poorest counties. These lands continue to face increasingly frequent drought, famine, flooding and, in places, rising risks of desertification (Abdimajid et al., 2020). Teenage and early pregnancy sharpen this picture further. Rates in Kenya’s ASALs are disproportionately high. The Kenya Demographic and Health Survey recorded teenage pregnancy at 28% in Narok, 22% in Kajiado and 18% in Taita Taveta, all above the national average of 15% (Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS), 2023). Poverty follows the same geography. County poverty indices in 2022 stood at 37.3 in Kajiado, 39.3 in Taita Taveta and 26.2 in Narok (Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS), 2023), meaning a substantial share of each county’s population could not meet basic needs even before drought losses were counted. Poverty, early pregnancy, interrupted education, unemployment and dependence on a single climate-sensitive livelihood therefore did not act as separate hardships. They compounded one another, so that when drought and livestock losses arrived, very young women were often left to manage family survival alone, navigating overlapping socioeconomic and environmental shocks with few resources and even fewer alternatives. Consistent with findings elsewhere (Drysdale et al., 2020), drought exposure among the women in this study was associated with childhood malnutrition. The absence of food aid during drought was associated with an approximately fourfold increase in the odds of child malnutrition (Table 4). This underscores the need to plan for, and provide, food relief to pastoralist households during drought to supplement their diminished staple diets. Building women’s capacity to produce alternative nutritious foods, in turn, was found to supplement these diminished diets and strengthen household food and nutritional sources (Figures 3 & 4). Prolonged drought has similarly been linked to long-term food insecurity among a pastoral community in Namibia, with an accompanying shift away from traditional foods (Prall and Scelza, 2023). Despite coming from pastoralist communities, many women in our study did not themselves keep livestock. In many pastoral communities, livestock are owned and managed by men (Gitungwa et al., 2021; Lutta et al., 2021). Only 78 of 1,036 women (7.5%) in our sample were livestock keepers. This cultural norm leaves most women without an independent income, depending on their husbands for food and other basic needs. When men migrate with animals during drought in search of pasture and water, women lack even the staple diet of milk and meat. Left with limited or no food and no resources of their own, women and girls turn to casual labor simply to raise the money to buy food. Most were also carers: 84% of households had one to five children, and 16% had between six and ten. Providing for a family with erratic, meagre, casual labor earnings, essentially alone, is stressful in itself, affecting both the health of children and the mental health of the young mothers who carry this burden. The IPCC has identified Africa as one of the region’s most vulnerable to climate change (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC, 2022). The destabilization of human systems and the natural ecosystems they depend on has driven poor quality of life and heightened vulnerability to socioeconomic shocks in communities like these (Wahid et al., 2025). East Africa’s 2020–2023 drought was among the most severe in over four decades (World Meteorological Organization (WMO), 2022), producing famine, acute malnutrition, large-scale crop failure, livestock death and displacement. More than 20 million people faced acute food and nutritional insecurity, and over 13 million animals were lost (Funk et al., 2023; United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR), 2024). Case studies from pastoral communities in Ethiopia and Kenya similarly describe ecological grief from climate change as reaching into social, emotional, spiritual and cultural life contributing to a loss of identity and traditions (Wahid et al., 2025). For pastoral women in Kajiado, Narok and Taita Taveta, solastalgia captures this experience precisely (Albrecht et al., 2007). The progressive loss of rangeland, water and livestock is not an abstract climatic trend but the erosion of the ecological space in which their identity, and that of their community, is rooted. Similar patterns are documented well beyond this setting. In Australia, adolescents living with prolonged drought reported significantly higher emotional distress than those without such exposure (Dean and Stain, 2007; 2010). A systematic review of Pacific Island nations found consistent associations between climate-related hazards such as sea-level rise and extreme weather, and psychological distress, particularly among women (Mengesha and Sarnyai, 2025). Drought-affected farmers in Tamil Nadu, India showed a high prevalence of depression alongside low resilience scores (Viswanathan et al., 2019). Our baseline data reveal comparable and previously undocumented traumatic experiences among pastoral women and their children. Famine and starvation were among the most frequently reported direct impacts of the drought (Tables 2 and 3). The close tracking of food scarcity with child malnutrition, described above, reinforces that food deprivation was the central pathway linking drought exposure to poor child health outcomes in this study. Conflicts and mental stress were the most reported indirect impacts attributed variously to lack of food, loss of animals and family separation. That the great majority of affected women received no food aid or other assistance compounds this picture. These figures, easily overlooked, reveal the extent of climate distress borne by vulnerable women and girls with no social support system to help them cope with its cumulative effects. A subset of 50 women from two Kajiado women’s groups, surveyed at the peak of the 2023 drought, illustrates this in finer detail. Eighteen reported travelling up to 5 kms for household water, and 32 (66%) reported trekking further than 6 km. Fifteen described severe backache and leg pain from carrying water over these distances and five reported waterborne illness from unsafe drinking water. All 50 reported family separation, with spouses or elder sons relocated in search of pastures and water leaving them as sole providers; 35 knew of family members involved in conflict or violence over water and pasture, some of whom lost animals as a result. Twenty reported feeling depressed and anxious because of food shortages, family separation, and livestock loss, yet only four had received support from well-wishers or NGOs. Three were undergoing stress-related treatment, and twenty said they would welcome professional counselling that simply was not available to them. Adaptive capacity and uptake of implemented interventions The FGDs generated insights regarding the relevance and coherence of the interventions implemented to build resilience and reduce collective traumatic experience. The relevance of interventions in meeting household food and nutrition needs was clear. Across all six women’s groups interviewed, mushroom and vegetable farming were consistently cited as a major improvement to household diets and nutrition, in place of diets that were previously limited and often monotonous. In pastoral areas such as Narok and Kajiado, milk had been the primary staple; In Taita Taveta, diets centered on traditional vegetables like amaranth, cowpea leaves and beans, which, while valuable, lacked diversity. The introduction of mushrooms (high in protein, calcium, and immunity-boosting properties) diversified diets, particularly for vulnerable groups such as children and the elderly. Initial skepticism about mushrooms was a common theme across all groups: Maasai communities are traditionally mycophobic and avoid eating mushrooms (Tibuhwa, 2012). By contrast, groups in Taita Taveta and one in Narok – mostly non Maasai - reported faster adoption. Cultural perceptions diverged: some groups associated wild mushrooms with danger, delaying acceptance, while others saw them as a better substitute for meat. Vegetables were already part of most diets, but home production through climate-smart vertical kitchen gardens made them more accessible and affordable. In helping the community cope with drought, livestock loss, and food shortages, mushrooms and vegetables were described as critical buffers. Their short growing cycles, storage potential, and independence from rainfall were particularly valued. A shared theme across groups was that mushrooms acted as a ‘safety net food’ during drought, even as their specific use diverged; a meat substitute in some groups and dried for long-term storage in others. The interventions were also relevant to women’s roles in the household and community. Across all groups, women reported that mushroom and vegetable production enhanced their influence in household decision-making, largely through financial contributions and improved family nutrition. It also strengthened their confidence, savings, and participation in community roles, underscoring the interventions’ relevance to women’s empowerment. Alongside these gains, recurring gaps emerged across all groups. Water scarcity was the most critical unmet need. High costs, limited storage and preservation, and inconsistent water supply were cited as ongoing challenges for both mushroom and vegetable production. Lack of market access was another shared gap, driven by a lack of reliable buyers. Infrastructure constraints limited their groups’ ability to reach more diverse, formal markets such as supermarkets. Examples include having no dryers, poor production facilities, and the absence of product certification. On coherence, the FDGs revealed how well mushroom and vegetable farming connected with other food or income initiatives led by NGOs and county governments. All six women groups described meaningful convergence with broader food and income initiatives within their counties, demonstrating coherence with local institutional actors, where NGO support complemented project efforts to address systemic barriers such as water access. In Taita Taveta and Narok, however, some groups described limited or non-existent linkages with external initiatives, relying instead mainly on family and neighbors rather than formal institutions. These observations reflect uneven institutional coherence, with some groups benefitting from strong local alignment and others operating in relative isolation. Community support was a strong theme across all six groups, though its form varied. Some drew mainly on immediate social networks, others on local authorities and community leaders who actively endorsed the interventions. One group of physically challenged persons noted minimal leadership involvement, while another reported that acceptance came not from government or community leaders but from community members impressed by the nutritional benefits. Community initiatives that create opportunities for people to engage and support one another can foster strong social ties and a sense of belonging, helping to mitigate the isolation often associated with climate trauma (Shomuyiwa and Lucero-Prisno, 2025). Farmer-school-style skills training strengthened self-confidence and knowledge-sharing capacity across groups, though two groups needed further reinforcement of technical skills. Groups consistently emphasized the need for practical, inclusive and accessible training that would work even for members with limited literacy, alongside a longer training period to address gaps identified through continuous practice and learning. Project activities aligned well with women’s daily responsibilities overall. Many noted that mushroom cultivation required minimal time and effort, letting them balance childcare, livestock care and household duties. Localized tensions did emerge, however. One Taita Taveta group described long travel distances to group activities as a strain, while poultry encroachment on kitchen gardens created minor disruptions. Bringing this training closer to women’s homes may further improve adoption and uptake. Nutrition messaging was consistently aligned with what women heard from clinics and schools, reinforcing trust and coherence between project interventions and existing health systems; a consistency that was crucial to building credibility and strength observed across all six groups. Limitations This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting findings. First, the cross-sectional, observational design does not permit causal inference. The associations reported here, including those from regression analysis, should be read as statistical associations rather than causal effects. Second, mental health and trauma were assessed through descriptive survey items and qualitative accounts rather than validated psychometric instruments (for example the PHQ-9 or the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale), so the reported prevalence of psychosocial distress should be treated as indicative rather than diagnostic. Third, all outcome data were self-reported by mothers and caregivers. This introduces the possibility of recall and social-desirability bias, particularly for retrospective reports of malnutrition and food insecurity during the 2022–2024 drought. Fourth, the evaluation of the mushroom production interventions was not based on a control group but on pre-intervention baseline status where no mushroom consumption occurred prior to the training. Consequently, the reported improvements in dietary diversity incorporating mushrooms and food consumption scores may partly be attributed to the interventions and other concurrent changes, such as the easing of drought conditions. Despite the limitations, the large sample size and the combination of quantitative and qualitative methods provide a valuable descriptive account of climate-related psychosocial and nutritional impacts among pastoral women that can inform future research. Conclusion These findings show how compounded socio-economic vulnerability and climate change produced deep traumatic experience among marginalized pastoral women and their households in Kajiado, Narok and Taita Taveta. The adaptive capacity and resilience interventions we implemented in response were transformative, contextually relevant, and gender-responsive, offering a practical model for addressing food and nutritional insecurity in arid and semi-arid regions. Anchored in alternative food production, dietary diversification and nutrition education, they shifted household consumption from predominantly livestock-based foods toward more nutrient-dense, plant-based options contributing meaningfully to national goals for climate adaptation and food security. Mushrooms, once culturally avoided, were widely accepted and valued by women for their nutritional and economic benefits. School-based farming extended this reach further, equipping learners with practical skills in sustainable food production. Women’s empowerment was a clear and significant impact, as participants gained financial independence from surplus sales, stronger decision-making power, and greater visibility in community leadership. Bringing people together to name their trauma collectively, and to explore it as a shared experience, was the essential starting point for building the trust and confidence needed to begin finding solutions. This is unsurprising. In pastoralist communities, mental health is intimately bound up with animal health and food security. When rains fail, are too strong, too intense, or too unpredictable, people become food-insecure very quickly, unable to feed their children, unsure how to find alternatives. This study shows how tightly these issues are interwoven with education, demography, early pregnancies, pre-existing malnutrition, health and land-use change. Precisely because these issues are so entangled, finding alternative means of subsistence became the point around which participants could gather and focus their collective solution finding, even where social and cultural factors made this process difficult at the outset. Sustainability prospects were promising but required targeted reinforcement. Experienced groups showed strong continuity potential, while newer groups needed longer engagement to stabilize adoption. Institutional sustainability depends on integrating this model into county government agriculture, education, and nutrition systems. There is a further opportunity to build microenterprise pathways through certification, branding and sales in partnership with local buyers. Overall, the interventions implemented in this study exemplify a scalable, climate-smart, and inclusive approach to addressing nutrition challenges of pastoral communities. Taken together, these findings indicate the need for reframing what climate adaptation could mean for pastoral women. The collective trauma they experience is not simply the product of drought, but of drought-stricken communities that are already stretched thin by poverty, demographic pressure, early marriage and pregnancy, and near-total dependence on a single, climate-sensitive livelihood. This study reveals an alternative to aid. Expanding livelihood options, and ensuring practical, low-cost ways to grow food and improve nutrition, measurably reduced trauma and strengthened resilience in the present. It also left communities materially better equipped for the next drought with food that does not depend on rainfall, income that does not depend on livestock survival, and a confidence and social cohesion forged by solving a shared crisis together. The evidence for this study is that climate-smart livelihood diversification reduced trauma and built resilience. What is now needed is holistic, joined up thinking and action for future programs. This means sustained investment in water infrastructure, healthcare, mental health support, social support and education, alongside ongoing technical mentorship and market development. Paired with longer training timelines and clear exit strategies these approaches will help to ensure and scale the nutritional, economic, and social gains achieved.
