Institute of Law, Nirma University, Ahmedabad, India Article metrics View details Abstract Human-Elephant Conflict (HEC) has emerged as a critical conservation, governance and socio-environmental challenge in India; particularly in Odisha, where high elephant populations coexist with expanding human activities. Increasing human-elephant interactions have resulted in rising human fatalities, elephant deaths, crop loss, habitat fragmentation, and livelihood insecurity. This study examines the ecological, socio-economic, legal, and governance dimensions of HEC in Odisha with particular emphasis on compensation mechanisms, coexistence strategies, and conservation governance. The policy and practice review research paper adopts a qualitative and doctrinal research methodology based primarily on secondary data, including government reports, policy documents, wildlife literature, census records, and conservation studies. Through interdisciplinary analysis, the paper identifies habitat fragmentation, land-use change, mining activities, infrastructure expansion, climate variability, and socio-economic vulnerabilities as major drivers intensifying HEC in the state. The study further evaluates the effectiveness and implementation challenges associated with compensation mechanisms, and existing mitigation frameworks. The findings indicate that although Odisha possesses a comparatively structured compensation framework and several conservation initiatives, persistent governance challenges such as delayed compensation disbursement, fragmented institutional coordination, weak habitat governance, and inadequate community participation continue to undermine long-term conflict mitigation efforts. Comparative analysis of selected elephant-range regions and countries highlights the growing importance of coexistence-oriented governance approaches integrating habitat connectivity, scientific monitoring, community participation, land-use planning, and localized mitigation strategies. The paper argues that sustainable mitigation of HEC in Odisha requires a shift from reactive compensation-based responses toward integrated coexistence-oriented conservation governance. Strengthening habitat protection, improving compensation administration, enhancing institutional coordination, incorporating ecological considerations within developmental planning, and promoting community-based conservation strategies remain essential for reducing conflict intensity and fostering long-term human-elephant coexistence in Odisha. 1 Introduction Human-wildlife interactions (HWI) are increasingly occurring in shared habitats, and encompass a vast array of effects on both humans and wildlife, including positive, neutral, and negative interactions. The contemporary conservation literature increasingly recognises that not all interactions involving wildlife constitute “conflict”; rather, human-wildlife conflict emerges in specific contexts where the presence or behaviour of wildlife poses actual or perceived, direct and recurring threats to human interests or needs, generating disagreement among people and negative impacts on people and/or wildlife (IUCN, 2023). This distinction is particularly important for understanding Human-Elephant Conflict (HEC), where recurring interactions may develop into conflict through livelihood losses, threats to human safety, elephant mortality, and competing conservation and developmental interests (IUCN, 2023). The extent to which this problem exists is further exacerbated by projections that over half the surface of the planet will experience increased human-wildlife overlap by 2070, with agricultural and forest systems being most affected (Sherburne, 2024; Ma et al., 2024). Human-Elephant Conflict is defined as “any interaction between humans and elephants that has negative effects on human social, economic, or cultural life and elephant conservation and the environment” (Sumatran Elephant Project, 2020). The Elephant Task Force (ETF), constituted by the Government of India, similarly characterised human-elephant conflict as the adverse impact people and elephants have on each other (MoEFCC, 2019). Human-Elephant Conflict in India has deep historical roots and reflects the long-standing relationship between humans and elephants across the Indian subcontinent (Sukumar, 2003; Lahiri-Choudhury, 1995). India has always regarded the elephant as its cultural symbol. Because of their importance in Hindu and Buddhist mythology and culture, elephants are considered sacred symbols of serenity, inner strength, and power (Blonder, 2017; Lahiri-Choudhury, 1995). Elephants play a vital role in the conservation of forest and savannah ecosystems, thereby contributing to the overall biodiversity. They establish pathways through densely wooded areas, thereby promoting the mobility of other fauna (Sukumar, 2003). When filled with water, elephant footprints can generate micro-ecosystems or provide temporary habitat for a variety of organisms. They are essential to the preservation of biodiversity in the ecosystems in which they reside as keystone species (World Wildlife Fund, 2024; Sukumar, 2003). They perform critical ecological functions through seed dispersal, vegetation modification, nutrient cycling, and the maintenance of ecosystem heterogeneity, thereby contributing significantly to ecosystem resilience and biodiversity conservation (Campos-Arceiz and Blake, 2011). Elephants therefore called as the Forest Gardeners (Campos-Arceiz and Blake, 2011) or the Ecosystem Engineers. However, increasing anthropogenic pressure on natural habitats has disrupted traditional coexistence patterns between humans and elephants (Dickman, 2010; Sukumar, 2003; Redpath et al., 2013). The expansion of urbanization, agriculture, deforestation, infrastructure development, and habitat fragmentation have further increased the contact between humans and elephants, and leading to crop depredation, destruction of homes, loss of life, and retaliatory killings (Goswami et al., 2014; Leimgruber et al., 2006). Beyond its ecological dimensions, HEC represents a complex socio-economic and governance issue affecting biodiversity conservation, rural livelihoods, and sustainable development (Dickman, 2010; Woodroffe et al., 2005). These pressures can also manifest as profound social tensions and disagreements between various human communities (Njomi et al., 2026; Sukumar, 2003). India hosts nearly 60% of the Asian elephant population which are already classified as endangered under Appendix I of the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Flora and Fauna (CITES) and Schedule I of the Indian Wildlife Protection Act,1972 (Ministry of Environment Forest and Climate Change- Government of India, 2022); and experiences some of the highest levels of HEC globally (MoEFCC, 2024; Sukumar, 2003). Recognising the need for a stronger institutional and conservation framework for elephant protection, the Government of India launched Project Elephant in 1992. It is a centrally funded and technically supported conservation scheme with 3 main objectives: a) To protect elephants, their habitat & corridors; b) To address the issue of man-animal conflict; and c) Welfare of captive elephants. In recent decades, increasing pressure on forest ecosystems, linear infrastructure expansion, mining activities, and changing land-use patterns have significantly reduced elephant habitats and migratory corridors (MoEFCC, 2019). As a result, several elephant-range states have witnessed escalating human-elephant interactions. These interactions cost a substantial number of human and elephant lives annually. A total of 400 humans and over 100 elephants are lost annually in conflict-related deaths in India. In addition to direct human death, HEC imposes massive economic loss, with the loss of crops alone affecting an estimated 500,000 families annually (MoEFCC, 2019). The challenge is particularly severe in Odisha, where forest fragmentation, dependence on forest-based livelihoods, and expanding human settlements have intensified conflict incidents across multiple districts. And therefore, Project Elephant has been partially successful in terms of conserving species. There have not been enough efforts directed towards the involvement of communities, security of livelihoods, mitigations plans at the local level, and conflict prevention. The State of Odisha presents an important case study as it is ecologically important, has a high elephant population and there are ongoing conflictual dynamics. It has experienced several instances of elephants and human deaths and crop loss, which questions about the success of elephant conservation policies, compensation schemes, and coexistence programs. The Government of Odisha and the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change have taken steps in the form of Project Elephant, ex gratia compensation schemes, habitat restoration plans, and the anti-depredation mechanism in an attempt to address the issue of conflict mitigation in the long term, but implementation issues remain. Existing research and literature on HEC have largely been focused on elephant behaviour, fragmentation, migration routes and wildlife conservation approaches. However, fewer studies critically examine whether existing legal, administrative, and policy mechanisms are adequately designed to address the multi-dimensional nature of HEC, particularly in Odisha. Compensation mechanisms, conservation programmes, community participation, and governance coordination remain crucial but relatively underexplored dimensions within HEC research. Addressing this gap, the present study adopts a structured policy and governance analysis approach to examine Human-Elephant Conflict in Odisha. 2 Methodology This paper adopts a qualitative policy-gap analysis framework supported by doctrinal legal analysis, comparative policy evaluation, and secondary empirical evidence to examine the ecological, socio-economic, legal, and governance dimensions of Human-Elephant Conflict (HEC) in Odisha, India. The research integrates conservation science, environmental governance, and legal-policy analysis to evaluate existing conflict mitigation measures and identify pathways toward sustainable coexistence. The study primarily relies on secondary data sources including government and forest department reports, census data, policy documents, academic literature, judicial pronouncements, international agency reports, and relevant HEC statistics to examine patterns, drivers, impacts, and governance responses associated with HEC. Literature was selected based on relevance to ecological drivers, socio-economic impacts, legal frameworks, governance mechanisms, and coexistence-based mitigation strategies. The doctrinal legal analysis examines statutory and policy instruments governing elephant conservation and conflict mitigation in India, Project Elephant guidelines, constitutional environmental obligations, and state-level compensation frameworks. These instruments were analysed to identify the extent to which existing frameworks address habitat protection, institutional coordination, compensation administration, community participation, and long-term coexistence strategies. The comparative policy evaluation component examines selected elephant-range regions and conservation approaches to identify governance practices relevant to Odisha. The comparison was structured around key parameters including conflict mitigation strategies, compensation mechanisms, habitat connectivity approaches, community participation, institutional coordination, and technology-based monitoring systems. The study does not attempt to establish statistical causation or conduct quantitative modelling of conflict drivers. Instead, it synthesizes existing ecological, legal, and policy evidence to critically evaluate current governance responses and develop recommendations for strengthening coexistence-oriented Human-Elephant Conflict management. The analysis is organised around four interrelated policy dimensions: (i) the ecological and socio-economic contenxt of Human-Elephant Conflict, (ii) existing legal and institutional frameworks, (iii) compensation and coexistence mechanisms, and (iv) comparative policy practices and governance responses. This structure treats the ecological and socio-economic dimensions primarily as context for assessing the adequacy of existing legal, administrative, and policy mechanisms in Odisha. Certain limitations remain within the scope of the research. The study depends upon the availability, consistency, and accuracy of secondary data, which may vary across institutions and reporting periods. Further field-based empirical studies involving affected communities, forest officials, and other stakeholders may provide additional insights into local perceptions and implementation challenges. Nevertheless, this interdisciplinary policy and governance approach provides a basis for evaluating existing HEC mitigation frameworks and identifying future policy priorities for Odisha. 3 Study area 3.1 Forest and administrative landscape of Odisha Odisha, the 8th largest state of India, located in the eastern-coastal region of the country, has a total Recorded Forest Area (RFA) of 61,204 sq. km, covering around 39.31% of the state’s total area. Its Forest Cover Area (FCA) is around 52,156 sq. km that is about 33.50% of the state’s geographical area. The state’s forest cover is classified as very dense (7213 sq. km), moderately dense (20,995 sq. km) and open forest (23,948 sq. km), representing 13.83%, 40.25%, and 45.92% of the Total Forest Area (TFA) respectively (Principal Chief Conservator of Forests & Head of Forest Force Odisha, 2023). The state is divided into 51 Divisions (37 Forest Divisions and 14 Wildlife Divisions) under 9 Forest Circles and 30 Administrative Districts. There are 283 Forest Ranges which include 60 Wildlife Ranges and 223 Territorial Ranges (PCCF & HoFF Odisha, 2024). These forest landscape and administrative structure provides the institutional context for forest management, wildlife conservation, HEC mitigation, habitat management, and compensation administration in Odisha. 3.2 Elephant distribution, corridors, and conflict vulnerability Odisha has 14 identified elephant corridors and 3 Elephant Reserves (ERs) - Mayurbhanj ER, Mahanadi ER, and Sambalpur ER, which were established in September 2001, June 2002, and March 2002, respectively, and notified by the State Government of Odisha to preserve important elephant habitats. The combined area of officially declared and planned forest areas within these three reserves is 4129 sq. km, whereas the total coverage of these reserves is 8509 sq. km (PCCF (Wildlife) & Chief Wildlife Warden-Odisha). Odisha is an important state within India’s HEC landscape. The state is home to approximately 1976 Asiatic elephants as per the 2017 elephant census, representing about 5% of the global population of this species (Tata Power, 2024). It increases to 2098 elephants in 2024 (Mishra et al., 2024). Elephants occur in 28 of the state’s 30 districts, indicating their wide distribution across administrative landscapes (Barik, 2022; Mishra et al., 2024). 4 Human-elephant conflict in India HEC represents a significant conservation and governance challenge in India that requiring the protection of both human livelihoods and elephant populations (Das et al., 2025; Dickman, 2010). The government must manage two different responsibilities at once. The public’s safety and way of life must be managed on the one hand, and the protection and survival of the ecologically prominent flagship species in their natural environment must be ensured on the other. This indicates that effective HEC mitigation requires coordinated governance extending beyond forest departments to include transport, electricity, land-use planning, rural development, and local administrative institutions. 5 Human-elephant conflict in Odisha The data in Tables 1–5 demonstrate that Odisha consistently records the highest levels of conflict-related elephant deaths and human casualties, underscoring the need for strengthened coexistence-oriented governance, and the need for integrated policy interventions beyond conventional wildlife conservation measures. Although it has just around 6.6% of India’s elephant population, still it accounts for one-fifth of total elephant deaths annually in India. The number of people died due to the HEC is around one-fifth of all HEC related human deaths in India. On the other hand, 191 elephants died in the state between 2014 and January 2022 from unnatural causes (train and road accidents-24, poisoning and poaching-77, electrocution-90), and 143 elephants died from uncertain causes (Mohanty, 2024). An average of one human-elephant conflict occurs in Odisha every two days, and one person dies as a result of this encounter every three days. The year 2022–23 has become even worse for Odisha as the human casualties due to elephant attacks have increased to 148 which is around one-fourth of India (refer Table 5) (Mohanty, 2024); whereas from the year 2019-2023, Odisha accounted for around one-fifth of country’s tally of human casualties due to elephant attack (expect in the year 2018–19 where it was 15%). Surprisingly, Odisha is neither the top human-populated state nor the most elephant-populated state; still, the number of deaths of both parties is the highest. These statistics indicate that Odisha represents not merely a high-conflict landscape but also a governance-intensive conservation setting where ecological protection, rural livelihoods, infrastructure management, and administrative coordination intersect. The scale and persistence of conflict suggest that conventional wildlife conservation measures alone are insufficient, necessitating integrated policy responses that simultaneously address ecological and socio-economic dimensions of HEC. Crop damage is one of the most frequent manifestation of HEC, accounting for around 75% of all reported incidents, with paddy constituting approximately around 70% of all crop damage cases (Mohanty, 2024). Damage incidences usually peak during harvesting time, especially in November and December. For instance, from 2001 to 2018, huge stretches of agricultural land, totaling about 5145 hectares, have been damaged in Keonjhar district alone (Mishra et al., 2024). Such losses directly pose a threat to rural food supplies, food security and the economic stability of farmers. The predominance of crop depredation further demonstrates that conflict mitigation policies must extend beyond wildlife protection to include agricultural planning, livelihood security, and rural development interventions. The impacts of HEC extend beyond measurable economic losses. Repeated encounters make the farmers take precautionary measures to prevent elephants from interfering with their farming (Ravenelle and Nyhus, 2017). These include extremely heightened vigilance and a capability to do damage to anything and everything that comes in their way, thereby disrupting normal activities on a massive scale and causing psychological distress among the affected people. The dual effects of economic damage and psychological stress have the potential to create an anti-elephant attitude in the communities nearby, thereby making the task of conservation more difficult than ever. HEC, therefore creates a governance challenge in which compensation and mitigation mechanisms must address both material losses and the broader conditions affecting human-elephant coexistence. 6 Causes of human-elephant conflict in Odisha Human-elephant conflict in Odisha is a complex phenomenon driven by a confluence of ecological, anthropogenic, and socio-economic factors. The increasing frequency and intensity of these conflicts are directly linked to the pressures exerted by human activities on elephant habitats and traditional movement patterns (Woodroffe et al., 2005; Dickman, 2010). HECs are frequently due to the alienation of important elephant habitats (as occurred in the Sambalpur Elephant Reserve), especially their historic seasonal migration routes. The most severe alienations appear to have been caused by mining, the expansion of development projects like the canal network, electric power, road and railway network, an increase in the number of industries, anthropogenic expansion and conversion of their land to human habitations threat their survival at their habitats itself. As shown in Figure 1, there are five major causes of HEC in Odisha. These interconnected drivers collectively demonstrate that HEC in Odisha is fundamentally a governance challenge requiring integrated ecological, developmental, and institutional responses rather than isolated wildlife conservation measures. Figure 1 Causes of HEC in Odisha. 6.1 Habitat fragmentation and habitat loss The shrinking and degradation of natural elephant habitats constitute a primary driver of HEC in Odisha. This process is largely a consequence of various human developmental activities (Woodroffe et al., 2005). Deforestation, rapid urbanization, the growth of human settlements, and the extension of the road, railway, and canal network have further fragmented the forest and pushed humans closer to elephant habitats, increasing contacts and conflicts (Panda, 2014; Goswami et al., 2014). Odisha possesses approximately 52,156 sq. km of forest cover, of which nearly 46% consists of open forests, indicating that a substantial proportion of the state’s forests are relatively less dense and therefore more vulnerable to anthropogenic disturbances, encroachment, infrastructure expansion, and increased human-elephant interactions. The consequences of habitat fragmentation are reflected in the mortality trends presented earlier (Tables 1, 2), where Odisha recorded 113 electrocution deaths and 21 train-related elephant deaths between 2014-2023. And due to the unavailability of sufficient food in their natural habitat, the elephants are entering human settlements in quest of food and water, causing fatalities, injuries, and property damage in Odisha. These trends reinforce the need for habitat restoration and ecological connectivity to be integrated within infrastructure and land-use planning policies. 6.1.1 Deforestation Odisha has experienced significant forest cover loss, with 147 thousand hectares of tree cover lost between 2001 and 2024, representing a 5.5% decrease since 2000. From 2002 to 2024, Odisha lost 22.3 kha of humid primary forest, making up 15% of its total tree cover loss in the same time period. Humid primary forests, crucial for elephants, decreased by 5.9% from 2002 to 2024. Districts such as Kandhamal, Rayagada, and Kalahandi have recorded the most substantial tree cover loss (Global Forest Watch, 2025). Despite Odisha retaining substantial forest cover, increasing fragmentation and conversion of forest landscapes have reduced habitat connectivity. This highlights the importance of prioritising forest restoration alongside forest protection within long-term HEC mitigation strategies. 6.1.2 Mining and industrialisation Rampant mining, horizontal growth of the dugout areas, and weak regulation of industrial expansion have disrupted elephant corridors, contaminated the water and air, and intensified human-elephant interactions across several mineral-rich districts like Keonjhar, Sundargarh, Jharsuguda, and Angul (Senapati, 2021). Mining operations are contributing to the drying up and siltation of natural streams and rivulets, compelling elephants to utilize highways in search of water and food, thereby increasing their vulnerability to accidents (Senapati, 2019). Due to this anxious and enraged elephants are breaking into villages, and communities in quest of food, destroying paddy fields, and properties, and even killing villagers. In some instances, elephants infiltrate urban areas and towns and madly destroy whatever comes in the way. According to Odisha forest officials, the industrialization of the Angul, Sundargarh, and Jharsuguda districts has caused elephants to leave their native habitats and move to neighbouring regions, which has increased HEC. The highest numbers of human deaths attributed to HEC in 2021–2022 were registered in Sundargarh (21 of 97 total deaths); and a large part of it is because of industrial activity. It was followed by Keonjhar (12 human deaths), where mining operations are conducted within close proximity to elephant habitats. There have been 11 reported fatalities in Dhenkanal, a region with several laterite and black stone quarries. Angul follows with 10 human deaths, where the establishment of sizable steel companies and increased coal mining near elephant habitats have multiplied their attacks (Senapati, 2022). The relationship between extractive development and HEC therefore highlights the need for stronger integration of wildlife-corridor protection and ecological safeguards into mining and industrial planning. 6.1.3 Urbanization Rapid urban expansion has led to the encroachment upon natural habitats, exemplified by the Chandaka-Dampara Wildlife Sanctuary, which has become a small piece of island surrounded by highly populated urban areas & development (Nanda and Sahu, 2024). This phenomenon directly contributes to the shrinking of habitats and the severance of ancient migratory routes, forcing elephants entering into human-dominated areas. This example demonstrates how urban planning and habitat connectivity are directly relevant to HEC governance. 6.1.4 Infrastructure development The rapid proliferation of railways, roadways, electrification projects, and irrigation canal networks, such as the Rengali irrigation canals, has had a devastating impact on the traditional migratory paths of elephants. And due to the canal system of the rivers Brahmani and Mahanadi, the elephants appeared to have been disoriented and ended up in small patches of fragmented forest. The ongoing doubling of railway lines further exacerbates the obstruction of elephant movement and thus increasing mortality risks associated with train collisions. Odisha’s disproportionately high electrocution mortality suggests that power infrastructure, rather than just natural ecological processes, has become a major governance failure in elephant conservation. This shifts the discussion from wildlife management alone to regulatory oversight of electricity distribution networks. Accordingly, infrastructure planning should incorporate ecological safeguards, corridor-sensitive design, and preventive measures to reduce risks before projects are implemented. 6.2 Land use changes Changes in land-use and forest-management practices can further alter food availability and increase elephant dependence on agricultural landscapes (Goswami et al., 2014). 6.2.1 Forest practices Modifications in local cropping patterns and changes in forest practices have led to a shift in the preferred food crops consumed by elephants. The cultivation of fruiting trees and sprouting bamboo in agricultural areas entices elephants, increasing the likelihood of crop-raiding incidents (Gupta, 2023). Large-scale bamboo and cane extraction, clear-cutting of large tracts of forest, illegal logging, conversion of natural forests into monocultures with plantations of teak, eucalyptus, and other non-fodder species, and the growing development of forest villages are some of the forestry practices that have contributed to the degradation of many forest areas in Odisha leading to unavailability of sufficient food to these giants. And due to this elephants are spending less time in the forest and invading agricultural areas. 6.2.2 Agricultural expansion Agricultural expansion and conversion of forest areas into cultivated land have concentrated HEC incidents around farming landscapes (Tripathy et al., 2021). On agricultural land, it is easily obtain a large amount of nutritious food across a smaller area with little work. An elephant prefers to raid agricultural areas, especially if there is a lack of forage in the jungles. Elephants are naturally attracted to nutritious paddy fields i.e. the primary grain grown in Odisha. This creates substantial livelihood losses for farming communities, particularly where multiple cropping increases the duration of agricultural exposure to elephants. HEC mitigation therefore requires land-use planning and agricultural strategies that account for elephant movement and crop vulnerability. 6.3 Climate change Climate variability further intensifies ecological stress within forest ecosystems, affecting the availability of water, fodder, and suitable habitat for elephants. Odisha has experienced a significant increase in temperature and changes in rainfall patterns, including a decline in the number of rainy days. The data from the meteorological department reveals that although the average worldwide temperature has increased by 0.5°C over the past 50 years, Odisha has seen a rise of 1°C, with the maximum reported temperature increase being 6-8°C during the past 10 years (India Meteorological Department, 2022). The state has also noticed a one-day decline in the amount of rainy days every five years. This unsettling tendency has an impact on not just the natural cycles but also human livelihoods and the survival of elephants (Palei, 2008). These changes may compound existing pressures from habitat fragmentation by reducing the availability of water and forage in forest areas and increasing elephant movement into human-dominated landscapes. Climate change should therefore be incorporated into long-term HEC planning through adaptive habitat and water-management strategies. 6.4 Socio-economic vulnerabilities The socio-economic conditions of communities living near elephant habitats influence both exposure to HEC and their capacity to absorb its consequences. 6.4.1 Poverty and forest dependency Approximately 85% of Odisha’s population resides in rural areas and relies substantially on agriculture, while around 40% of the state’s population depends on forests for livelihood and subsistence, with forest resources contributing approximately 25-52% of household income in some communities (Mohapatra, 2020). Such dependence increases exposure to HEC because households often rely directly on agricultural and forest resources located within or near elephant habitats. 6.4.2 Proximity of habitations Human settlements and agricultural land located close to elephant habitats and established movement routes increase exposure to HEC. The poor economic conditions of farmers and inadequate infrastructure, such as lack of irrigation facilities, serve as major barriers to their ability to adapt to environmental changes (Mohapatra, 2020), rendering them more susceptible to the adverse impacts of HEC. Also, due to socio-economic vulnerabilities, locals have started moving more often through the forests without appropriate records for livestock grazing, fodder, fuelwood, and non-timber forest products. These results in over-exposure which leads to increasing encounters with elephants than in the past. These vulnerabilities demonstrate that effective HEC policy must address community exposure and livelihood resilience alongside ecological conservation. 6.5 Inadequate mitigation measures and management challenges Deficiencies in existing mitigation strategies and broader management challenges further exacerbate HEC. 6.5.1 Elephant population relative to habitat Official reports indicate that Odisha’s elephant population of approximately 2098 is about 400 elephants more than the sustainable carrying capacity of 1700 elephants for the available habitat in Odisha. This perceived “overpopulation” is a key factor driving elephants to venture out of their traditional habitats (Nanda, 2024). If the available, viable habitat has drastically shrunk due to human pressures, then even a stable or slightly increasing elephant population would exceed the reduced carrying capacity. The issue is not necessarily an absolute “overpopulation” of elephants, but rather a severe under-provision of suitable habitats for the existing population due to human pressures. Framing HEC primarily as an elephant-population problem risks diverting attention from habitat protection, restoration, connectivity, and sustainable land-use planning. The issue therefore requires assessment within the broader governance of habitat availability rather than population numbers alone and need to be addressed the fundamental issues of habitat protection, restoration, and sustainable land-use planning, which are critical for long-term solutions. 6.5.2 Failure to implement safeguards Despite recommendations from a 2010 inquiry by the MoEFCC for preventive measures such as raising sagging power lines and installing safety fuses, the state has largely failed to implement these safeguards effectively despite of spending almost Rs.700 Crore (Mohanty, 2025). The reported expenditure on mitigation measures, alongside continuing electrocution mortality, raises questions regarding the effectiveness of implementation and regulatory oversight. 6.5.3 Lack of coordinated action The central government issued an advisory in February 2021 emphasizing the need for coordinated inter-departmental action and the establishment of rapid response teams to manage Human-Wildlife Conflict (MoEFCC, 2024). Continued HEC incidents indicate the importance of assessing how effectively such coordination mechanisms operate in practice, particularly across forest, electricity, railways, roads, district administration, and local communities. Strengthening inter-departmental coordination is therefore central to effective HEC governance. The interconnectedness of habitat degradation and socio-economic vulnerabilities creates a challenging cycle. Human development pressures reduce available elephant habitat, and elephant incursions into human areas generate livelihood and human-safety losses. These impacts increase community vulnerability and retaliatory responses (Dickman, 2010). The “overpopulation” narrative, when viewed through this lens, highlights the reduced carrying capacity of degraded environments rather than an inherent excess of elephants. Breaking this cycle therefore requires integrated governance that simultaneously restores ecological connectivity, strengthens institutional coordination, improves infrastructure planning, effective compensation and enhances community participation within long-term coexistence strategies. 7 Compensation mechanism in India The Government of India uses ex-gratia compensation as a policy instrument to provide financial assistance to individuals affected by human-wildlife conflict, with state governments responsible for determining and administering compensation within applicable frameworks. Compensation may cover human fatalities and injuries, crop and plantation losses, livestock deaths, and damage to property, with assessment generally requiring verification by relevant forest, revenue, agricultural, and veterinary authorities. Under the current system, a compensation of Rs.5 lakh is provided for death or permanent incapacitation resulting from the conflict. For cases of grievous injury, a compensation of Rs.2 lakh is granted, while treatment of minor injuries is eligible for reimbursement of upto Rs.25000 (Aggarwal, 2018). The compensation system in India is not just a form of monetary assistance or financial relief, but is also a key instrument of policy governance that seeks to curb any retaliatory tendencies against wildlife and help foster peaceful existence for both local people and conservation. 7.1 Compensation mechanism in Odisha Odisha has established a comparatively detailed compensation framework. Under Rules 45-AA, 45-BB, 45-CC and 45-FF of the Wildlife (Protection) (Odisha) Rules, 1974, Odisha provides compensation across multiple categories of HEC-related losses such as human fatalities and injuries, crop and livestock losses, and damage to residential property, with corresponding verification and disbursement procedures (Barik, 2023). For human fatalities, the compensation is ₹6 lakh, of which 10% is paid initially, with the balance subject to verification by the Forest Department, including evidence of elephant involvement. Permanent disability attracts ₹1.5 lakh for disability below 60% and ₹2.5 lakh for disability above 60%, while temporary injuries are compensated at ₹10,000 or ₹5,000 depending on the duration of hospitalisation, along with free treatment in specified cases. The framework also provides compensation for cattle loss, crop damage i.e. ₹20,000 per acre for paddy and cereals, ₹25,000 per acre for cash crops, and house damage, ranging from ₹10,000 for partial damage to ₹20,000 for complete damage, together with provision for a Biju Pucca Ghar or its monetary equivalent. These provisions demonstrate the relatively broad coverage of Odisha’s compensation framework, extending beyond human casualties to livelihood and property losses. Although Odisha’s compensation framework appears comparatively detailed and structured, procedural requirements relating to verification, evidence collection, and departmental approval often delay disbursement processes. In remote rural and forest-adjacent regions, affected communities may face significant administrative and logistical barriers in accessing timely compensation. Such delays may weaken public trust in conservation governance and contribute to negative perceptions toward elephants and wildlife protection measures. So, improving administrative efficiency may contribute as significantly to conflict mitigation as increasing compensation amounts themselves. When the compensation policies of both Odisha and Karnataka were reviewed, it is clearly evident that Odisha enjoys a relatively thorough and detailed compensation policy, while there are certain shortcomings with respect to compensation policies in Karnataka. However, HEC is relatively lower in Karnataka than in Odisha. This comparison makes it quite clear that while compensation is one important means of mitigating HEC, it cannot achieve this objective in its absence unless it is coupled with other equally effective strategies (Ravenelle and Nyhus, 2017). Compensation should therefore be understood as part of a broader coexistence strategy rather than as a standalone solution. 7.2 Governance challenges in compensation administration Despite the existence of compensation systems at both national and state levels, several governance and administrative barriers limit their effectiveness in addressing HEC in Odisha. Compensation is intended to provide timely financial assistance and reduce retaliatory responses towards wildlife, but delays, procedural complexity, and institutional fragmentation can undermine these objectives (Ravenelle and Nyhus, 2017). One of the major problems with compensation schemes is the delay in verification and payment of the same. Compensation schemes usually involve thorough field verification of claims, involving proof of the existence of elephant tracks, proof of the extent of damage, post mortem of humans who were killed by elephants, and even approval from various departments before payment of the compensation amount. While it makes sense that such procedures help prevent fraudulent claims, they can delay disbursement, particularly in remote forested areas where administrative access is limited. Another major challenge is institutional fragmentation. Forest, revenue, agriculture, electricity, and local administrative authorities may be involved in different aspects of HEC mitigation and compensation, yet coordination among these agencies remain limited. Inadequate institutional coherence and inadequate inter-departmental coordination are barriers to the timely preparation of plans for prevention, monitoring and implementing of such plans in conflict-prone areas. Another issue is compensation adequacy. While the compensation structure of Odisha is relatively detailed compared to other Indian states, the assistance provided to the villagers for crop damage, destruction of property, and livelihood disturbance falls short of providing for all the consequences of these losses. These losses include food insecurity, loss of livelihood, fear of further attacks, disruption of the educational and livelihood infrastructure, and the psychological trauma suffered by these people (Ravenelle and Nyhus, 2017; Ogra and Badola, 2008; Mishra et al., 2024). Moreover, due to ignorance about the process of compensation, requirement of documentation, and reporting, many of the affected individuals will refrain from following up with their cases of compensation. Sometimes even damages may go underreported. Additionally, informal local settlement procedures may also compromise the effectiveness of the compensation system. Eventually, all these governance issues may affect the trust of people in conservation bodies and create negative attitudes towards elephants and conservation initiatives (Woodroffe et al., 2005). Compensation should therefore be treated not merely as monetary relief but as an integral component of conservation governance. An effective system requires timely and accessible procedures, transparent verification, clear accountability, community participation, and coordination among relevant agencies. Improving these administrative dimensions may strengthen both the effectiveness of compensation and broader efforts towards human-elephant coexistence. 8 Comparison with other elephant-rich nations Legal and constitutional provisions in India are also instrumental in providing necessary support for the protection of wildlife and co-existence governance. Upon conducting a comparative analysis of India’s policy framework for the conservation of elephants with those of Botswana and Sri Lanka, it becomes apparent that India has implemented a comprehensive legal and regulatory system for the protection of elephants. The Indian elephants are afforded the greatest protection through Schedule I of the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972, whereas Article 48A and Article 51A(g) of the Constitution of India make it mandatory for both the State and its people to protect wildlife and ensure environmental preservation. Odisha too has taken steps in this direction by adopting state-based laws like Wildlife (Protection) (Odisha) Rules, 1974, and has also undertaken several conservation measures under Project Elephant. Nonetheless, the existence of Human-Elephant Conflicts in Odisha clearly shows that legal protection alone is not enough to ensure effective conservation outcomes. Rather, successful coexistence depends upon effective policy implementation, institutional coordination, habitat governance, community participation, and continuous monitoring. Botswana’s strict elephant hunting laws and concentration on animal parts show its commitment to elephant conservation (Hoare, 2000). Botswana has additionally implemented elephant-monitoring and anti-poaching initiatives under international conservation frameworks such as the Monitoring Illegal Killing of Elephants (MIKE) programme coordinated through CITES (Hoare, 2000). These initiatives strengthen wildlife monitoring, ivory-trade surveillance, and conservation enforcement mechanisms. Strong enforcement, continuous wildlife monitoring, and coordinated anti-poaching governance remain indispensable components of long-term elephant conservation in Botswana. Sri Lanka provides a more directly relevant comparison for HEC governance. Its approach has increasingly shifted away from attempts to confine elephants within protected areas towards strategies that address human habitation, agricultural areas, and wider stakeholder interests (The Presidential Committee SriLanka, 2020; Centre for Environmental Justice, 2024). Community-based electric fencing, land-use planning, compensation, scientific monitoring, and stakeholder participation form components of this broader approach (Fernando et al., 2005; Centre for Environmental Justice, 2024). Monitoring measures such as elephant-distribution surveys, GPS-based tracking, and assessment of conflict-triggering activities further support adaptive management (Fernando et al., 2005; Redpath et al., 2013). The Sri Lankan experience therefore demonstrates the value of integrating local participation, preventive mitigation, habitat management, and monitoring within a broader coexistence framework. Certain countries have also adopted broader rights-based approaches toward nature, wildlife and environmental protection (Kauffman and Martin, 2017). Ecuador, for instance, in 2022 became the first country in the world to legally recognise wild animals as subjects of rights rather than property, reflecting an evolving global discourse on ecological protection and the intrinsic value of non-human life. Their highest court ruled that animals have the right to exist, evolve, and be protected from cruelty. Although India follows a different constitutional and legal framework, such developments highlight considering wildlife protection alongside animal welfare, human livelihoods, and ecological sustainability (Redpath et al., 2013). Comparative experiences from elephant-rich nations therefore demonstrate that effective HEC governance cannot depend on legal protection or compensation alone. For Odisha, the relevant policy lesson is the need to integrate habitat connectivity, land-use planning, community participation, scientific monitoring, preventive mitigation, and timely compensation within a coordinated institutional framework. These approaches provide a basis for assessing the strengths and implementation gaps of India’s existing HEC governance mechanisms. 9 Recommendations The analysis indicates that HEC mitigation in Odisha requires a shift from elephant confinement and predominantly reactive responses of towards a coordinated coexistence-oriented framework (Njomi et al., 2026; Redpath et al., 2013). The recommendations below focus on four complementary policy priorities: land-use and habitat governance, community-based mitigation, technology and early-warning systems, and institutional and compensation governance. Together, these measures seek to reduce conflict risk while strengthening human security, elephant conservation, and administrative responsiveness. 9.1 Coexistence-oriented governance and land-use planning Integrated land-use planning and habitat governance must form a central component of long-term HEC mitigation strategies in Odisha. Rapid infrastructure expansion, mining activities, deforestation, railways, roads, canal and other land-use changes, and unregulated developmental interventions should incorporate elephant movement patterns and ecological sensitivity at the planning and approval stages. Elephant corridors and movement routes should be protected and, where degraded, restored through landscape-level planning rather than isolated protected-area management. District-level development plans and infrastructure approval processes should therefore include corridor protection, ecological connectivity, and wildlife-sensitive safeguards. Priority should be given to protecting and reconnecting Odisha’s open and moderately dense forests, which together constitute the majority of the state’s forest cover, in order to strengthen habitat connectivity and reduce HEC (Fernando et al., 2005;Dickman, 2010;Goswami et al., 2014;Balasubramanian, 2023). Restoration and protection of elephant corridors can play an important role in preventing forced movements of elephants into agriculture and human settlements. Effective implementation will require coordination among forest authorities, local administration, infrastructure agencies, and affected communities (Goswami et al., 2014). 9.2 Community-based conflict mitigation strategies Community participation should form an integral part of HEC mitigation strategies in Odisha, particularly because rural and forest-dependent communities bear much of the economical, social and psychological burden of conflict. Communities should be treated as active partners in conflict prevention and conservation planning rather than solely as beneficiaries of compensation. In highly vulnerable regions, government should support agricultural diversification and encourage the local communities to cultivate Non-Preferred Crops (crops less preferred by elephants) (Wahed et al., 2016), promoting natural deterrent barriers, supporting alternative livelihood opportunities, reducing recurring crop-raiding incidents. Crop success in great measure is a function of land, soil and weather. And in this regard, government-supported agricultural diversification programmes, soil-suitability assessments, technical guidance, and financial incentives for cultivating elephant-resistant or less-preferred crops may help improve the long-term viability of coexistence-oriented farming practices in conflict-prone regions. Financial and technical support could include seed assistance, agricultural guidance, protective fencing, and alternative livelihood opportunities. Localised, non-lethal measures such as solar or natural fencing, bee-based deterrents, watch towers, and community-based early-warning mechanisms may also be deployed according to local conditions. Effective HEC governance ultimately depends upon local community participation. Panchayats, forest-dependent communities, civil-society organisations, and eco-development committees should be incorporated into local conflict-management planning and monitoring. Laws and policies should therefore empower local communities not merely as beneficiaries of compensation schemes but as active partners in conservation planning, conflict prevention, habitat protection, and long-term coexistence initiatives. 9.3 Technology, monitoring, and early warning systems Technology-assisted monitoring should be used to improve early detection of elephant movement and reduce preventable human and elephant casualties. Given that electrocution constitutes the leading cause of unnatural elephant mortality in Odisha according to the MoEFCC data (see Table 2), priority should be accorded to insulating low-hanging transmission lines, regulating illegal electric fencing, integrating wildlife-sensitive standards into rural electrification projects, and deploying real-time monitoring systems in identified elephant corridors (Narayanan, 2015). Environmental Impact Assessments (EIAs) for infrastructure projects should explicitly incorporate elephant movement patterns to minimise future conflict. Where locally appropriate, GPS-based monitoring, camera or sensor systems, mobile alerts, and community-based early-warning mechanisms can support timely responses to elephant movement, thereby minimizing adverse human-elephant interactions (Wall et al., 2014). Such technologies should complement, rather than replace, habitat protection, community participation, and institutional coordination. Scientific monitoring of elephant behaviour, migratory routes, habitat-use patterns, and stress indicators may further support evidence-based conservation planning and localized conflict-management strategies (Palei, 2008; Narayanan, 2015). Research relating to elephant stress, behavioural adaptation, and ecological pressures can help policymakers and conservation authorities better understand the long-term impact of habitat fragmentation, developmental pressures, and repeated human interaction upon elephant populations. Effective long-term conflict mitigation ultimately depends upon integrating scientific monitoring, ecological planning, institutional coordination, and local community involvement within a broader coexistence framework. 9.4 Institutional strengthening and conservation measures Strengthening institutional coordination and improving compensation governance alongside ecological interventions remain essential for long-term HEC mitigation in Odisha. Compensation procedures should provide timely disbursement, transparent verification, accessible grievance mechanisms, and clear administrative responsibilities. Coordination among forest, revenue, agriculture, electricity, disaster-response, local-government, and other relevant authorities should be institutionalised in conflict-prone areas to improve prevention, rapid response, and compensation administration. Local institutions should also be supported in implementing habitat-restoration and corridor-management measures. Restoration should prioritise ecologically appropriate vegetation and habitat conditions that improve forage and water availability while maintaining connectivity between elephant habitats. Such measures should be integrated with district-level conservation and development planning rather than treated as isolated projects. Elephant translocation should be considered only as an exceptional, site-specific intervention where recurring conflict cannot reasonably be addressed through habitat management and preventive measures. Any such intervention should be based on ecological and behavioural assessment, animal-welfare considerations, post-translocation monitoring, and institutional coordination. Scientific evaluation, ecological feasibility, post-translocation evaluation, and organizational collaboration become very important before doing anything of that sort. Translocation should therefore complement, rather than substitute for, habitat protection, corridor restoration, and coexistence-oriented conflict prevention (WWF-India). 10 Discussion The analysis indicates that Human-Elephant Conflict (HEC) in Odisha is not attributable to a single ecological or socio-economic factor. Rather, conflict persists through the interaction of habitat fragmentation, human dependence on forest landscapes, land-use pressures, inadequate institutional coordination, and limitations in the implementation of existing mitigation and compensation mechanisms. The central policy challenge is therefore not the absence of legal protection for elephants, but the limited integration of conservation, compensation, land-use governance, and local-level conflict management. The review of India’s and Odisha’s legal and institutional frameworks demonstrates that substantial mechanisms already exist for elephant protection and compensation. However, the presence of formal provisions does not necessarily translate into effective outcomes. Delays in compensation, complex verification procedures, fragmented administrative responsibilities, and uneven implementation can reduce the practical effectiveness of otherwise comprehensive schemes. Compensation can provide important immediate relief and may reduce incentives for retaliation, but it cannot by itself address recurring crop losses, habitat fragmentation, unsafe human-elephant encounters, or the underlying pressures that produce conflict. The comparative analysis further indicates that effective HEC governance is generally associated with integration rather than reliance on a single intervention. Experiences from Karnataka, Sri Lanka, and Botswana illustrate the relevance of combining compensation and enforcement with community participation, scientific monitoring, habitat and land-use management, and locally appropriate preventive measures. These approaches are particularly relevant to Odisha because elephant populations and conflict patterns vary across administrative and ecological landscapes. A uniform state-wide response may therefore be less effective than landscape-specific strategies supported by coordinated institutions and reliable monitoring. The findings also suggest that the effectiveness of HEC policy should be assessed not only by the amount of compensation provided or the number of mitigation measures introduced, but by their timeliness, accessibility, coordination, and preventive value. A compensation system that reaches affected households only after prolonged verification may provide financial relief while failing to maintain public confidence in conservation governance. Similarly, habitat protection measures may have limited impact where corridors and movement routes are not adequately incorporated into wider land-use and infrastructure planning. Community participation is consequently not merely a supplementary component of HEC management. Communities living alongside elephant habitats are directly exposed to the costs of conservation and are also important actors in early reporting, local prevention, monitoring, and coexistence initiatives. Strengthening their participation, while maintaining scientific and institutional oversight, can help shift HEC management from a predominantly administrative response towards a more locally responsive governance model. Taken together, the findings support a shift from reactive conflict compensation towards integrated coexistence governance. This does not imply that existing legal and compensation mechanisms should be replaced. Rather, they should be better connected with habitat connectivity, preventive mitigation, community participation, inter-departmental coordination, and evidence-based monitoring. Such an approach would allow Odisha to retain the strengths of its existing framework while addressing the implementation gaps that continue to limit its effectiveness. 11 Conclusion Human-Elephant Conflict in Odisha reflects a continuing governance challenge arising from the interaction between elephant conservation and human use of shared landscapes. This review finds that India and Odisha already possess substantial legal, institutional, and compensation mechanisms, but their effectiveness is constrained by implementation and coordination gaps. The analysis therefore points towards better integration rather than simply more regulation or higher compensation. Timely and accessible compensation, protection of habitat and corridors, coordinated institutional action, community participation, scientific monitoring, and preventive local measures need to operate as interconnected components of HEC governance. HEC cannot be eliminated entirely from shared landscapes. However, its frequency and severity can be reduced through a preventive and coexistence-oriented approach that protects both elephant populations and the livelihoods and security of affected communities. For Odisha, the priority should consequently be to strengthen implementation of existing mechanisms while improving their coordination across ecological, administrative, and community levels.
