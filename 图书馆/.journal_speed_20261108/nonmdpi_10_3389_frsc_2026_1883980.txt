Sustainable Society Design Center, Graduate School of Frontier Sciences, The University of Tokyo, Kashiwa, Japan See more Article metrics View details Abstract Introduction: Urban green spaces are widely recognized for enhancing human well-being and delivering co-benefits for both communities and the environment. In Chinese cities, gated communities have become the dominant form of residential development and often include collectively owned private green space. However, these semi-private green spaces (accessible only to residents and their guests) raise important justice questions. Therefore, we examine whether access to private community green spaces substitutes or complements public park engagement and construct an environmental justice typology based on private green advantage and perceived public park accessibility. Methods: A cross-sectional survey of 1668 residents was conducted in Fuzhou and Guiyang, two second-tier provincial capital cities selected for their contrasting public park accessibility. We developed three composite indices to capture residents’ objective and perceived green space use: Private Green Advantage, Public Park Engagement, and Perceived Public Accessibility. An environmental justice typology was constructed by cross-classifying respondents on Private Green Advantage and Perceived Public Accessibility. Complementarity was tested through correlation analysis, and spatial variation was examined at city and district levels. Results: Findings reveal a consistent complementarity pattern. Residents with higher Private Green Advantage show greater public park engagement (r = 0.420, p < 0.001). The typology identified four population groups: Advantaged (29.5%), Private-Reliant (17.1%), Public-Reliant (18.3%), and Disadvantaged (28.2%). City-level patterns were similar despite stark differences in objective spatial accessibility between the two cities, while substantial district-level variation highlights the importance of local-scale analysis. Discussion: These results indicate a cumulative pattern of green space inequality, consistent with cumulative advantage rather than compensatory distribution. The findings extend environmental justice scholarship on urban green space beyond distributive access, and imply that city planning should direct park investments towards residents experiencing dual disadvantages, as private and public green access are associated with complementarity rather than compensation across the population. 1 Introduction Urban green spaces provide critical benefits for city residents, including physical and mental wellbeing, social interaction, and environmental quality (Aronson et al., 2017; Hillsdon et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2017; Yao et al., 2022). Target 11.7 of the Sustainable Development Goals (United Nations, 2015) focuses on providing access to inclusive and public green spaces; however, despite the 2030 target approaching, access to these benefits is still not equally distributed. Environmental justice scholars have increasingly examined urban green spaces as critical environmental goods, noting that their unequal distribution can aggravate social inequalities (Rigolon, 2016; Wolch et al., 2014). Distributive justice scholarship frames these inequalities as failures in the allocation of environmental goods across populations (Schlosberg, 2007). Previous research shows that park availability, quality, and access tend to favor socially advantaged communities, raising concerns about distributive justice in urban environments (Byrne and Wolch, 2009; Wolch et al., 2014). While this extends beyond SDG Target 11.7 and Paragraph 37 of the New Urban Agenda (commitment to promoting safe, inclusive, accessible, green and quality public spaces) (UN-Habitat, 2017), environmental justice has extensively documented inequalities in public park access. More importantly, the focus on public parks means it has largely overlooked private green spaces within residential communities. Considering that urban residents experience both public and private green spaces, neglecting private green spaces only provides part of the picture. Recent research suggests that private gardens can partly compensate for limited access to public parks, particularly when public parks are beyond a convenient walking distance (Poortinga et al., 2021). However, whether this compensatory concept could be applied to the community scale remains underexamined. If residents with better private community green spaces also have better access to public parks, then green space benefits accumulate rather than offset each other, and may be associated with advantages for some and disadvantages for others. The question of cumulative advantage versus compensation is particularly unexamined in cities dominated by gated communities. Gated communities serve as spatially exclusive enclaves that restrict access to privately managed internal green spaces and recreational areas, which are accessible only to residents (Atkinson and Blandy, 2005; Hamama and Liu, 2020; Webster, 2001). The internal greens function as club goods (Webster, 2001; Xiao et al., 2016), improving residents’ quality of life while potentially aggravating social-spatial fragmentation. Wealthier households tend to enjoy better quality private greens and better access to amenities, while lower-income or migrant-worker communities rely primarily on public parks, often with poorer accessibility (Rao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2020). If private green spaces substitute for public ones, access to green space risks becoming a “luxury good” concentrated among high-income residents (Zhang et al., 2025). Previous research has also shown that some private green spaces within residential communities may substitute for nearby public parks, decreasing reliance on smaller or lower-quality public spaces, or complement large parks that serve different functions (Shan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). However, empirical studies testing this relationship in the context of gated communities are lacking, especially when examining the implications for environmental justice. Critically, prior research has not classified residents by their combined access to private and public green space, leaving groups experiencing dual advantage or dual disadvantage largely unidentified. Chinese cities provide a critical context for examining these dynamics. First, 66% of the population in China already lives in cities, which is close to the expected 68% globally by 2050 (UN-Habitat, 2022). Secondly, gated communities are the dominant residential form in urban China (Tomba, 2010), making this an opportune case to better understand and contribute knowledge to effectively plan just cities (Bai et al., 2025). Gated communities are linked to housing market dynamics, planning practices, and governance procedures (Hamama and Liu, 2020). China’s national regulations mandate a green space ratio for residential developments, providing near-universal private green provision since the 1978 economic reforms (Fan et al., 2017). Urban green space planning standards specify that residential parks are intended to serve populations within a 300 to 1,000 m radius (Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development, 2019). Nonetheless, whether this provision translates into green space experiences across residents in different housing market positions remains untested. This context enables examination of how private green and public park provision shapes environmental justice outcomes. The present manuscript examines the relationship between collectively owned private green spaces in gated communities and public parks in two provincial capital cities in China. The cases: (1) Fuzhou and (2) Guiyang are often described as second-tier cities and were selected because, according to a previous study by Liu and Terada (2025), these cities exhibit contrasting spatial justice profiles in urban park accessibility among ten second-tier provincial capitals. The study also showed that Fuzhou demonstrates higher overall accessibility but exhibits inequalities across housing price categories, whereas Guiyang shows relatively lower overall accessibility but a more equitable distribution across housing price categories. This contrast provides a comparative case design for examining where different accessibility contexts produce different patterns of green space experiences. Specifically, this study aims to tackle three research questions: Do private green spaces substitute for or complement public park engagement, and does this relationship indicate cumulative advantage or compensation? What environmental justice patterns emerge when analyzing private green advantage and perceived public accessibility across cities and districts? How do residents perceive green space equity in relation to environmental justice positions? By integrating perceived accessibility, usage patterns, and environmental justice typologies, this research provides a deeper understanding of how gated urbanism is related to green space justice and urban wellbeing in contemporary Chinese cities. 2 Methods 2.1 Study area and overall workflow Case study analysis and comparison were conducted in Fuzhou and Guiyang (Figure 1), two provincial capital cities in China that provide analytically comparable cases for examining environmental justice in large non-first-tier urban contexts. Both cities are shaped by gated-community residential development and a broader planning framework that make the comparison meaningful, while differences in residential market dynamics and urban development trajectories offer an opportunity to compare justice outcomes unfolding under contrasting conditions. Consequentially, they offer insights that can serve further development of cities with similar conditions. Previous research revealed that Fuzhou demonstrated higher overall park accessibility (mean = 0.46) but showed inequalities across housing price categories, whereas Guiyang displayed lower overall accessibility (mean = 0.38) but a more equitable distribution among different housing price categories (Liu and Terada, 2025). This contrast makes the two cities suitable for examining whether different park accessibility profiles are associated with different patterns of green space experiences. In the referenced study, objective accessibility was measured using a composite index that incorporated three dimensions of proximity (network-based walking distance to the nearest park), availability (number of parks within a 15-minute walking isochrone), and attractiveness (park size as a proxy for recreational capacity), following Biernacka and Kronenberg’s (2018) work. The index was calculated for residential communities in both cities and normalized to a 0–1 scale. For district-level analyses, only districts with more than 100 respondents (9 of 12 districts) were retained to ensure statistical reliability. Public park locations are shown for contextual reference only and represent a subset identified via the GAODE API (Gaode Open Platform API, 2023). 2.2 Questionnaire design and data collection A questionnaire was designed according to following four thematic sections: (1) Gated Green Space Assessment, which measured residents’ access to community green spaces within their residential compounds, frequency of use, perceived quality, and perceived importance of these spaces; (2) Public Park Access Patterns and Perceptions, captured the perceived accessibility, functional services, and people’s satisfaction with the parks in terms of activity provision; (3) Environmental Justice Perception, asked people’s degree of agreement using Likert scale for justice related statements; and (4) Demographic Information, collected respondents’ basic information including age, gender, income, and family composition. Combined across the four sections, the questionnaire totaled 24 questions. Questionnaire data were collected via an online survey. The target population consisted of adults living in Fuzhou and Guiyang at the time of the survey. The survey was conducted using a widely used survey platform for research called Wenjuanxing and recruited respondents from its user pool and distribution channels. The survey was open to all residents, whether homeowners or renters, if they resided in the target city. Since recruitment was done through the platform’s proprietary sampling methods rather than a controlled, closed sampling frame, a traditional response rate could not be determined. Therefore, the sample should be considered an online non-probability sample rather than a statistically representative sample of the entire population. Results should be interpreted as indicative of patterns and associations rather than population-level estimates and may be subject to selection bias (digitally active population and population reachable through the platform’s distribution channels). The survey platform applied its standard quality-control procedures, including duplicate-submission checks, bot detection, and respondent eligibility verification. Additional screening was conducted by the authors as follows: responses were excluded if incomplete, straight-lined, from IP addresses outside the target city, or indicated non-residence. Of 866 responses in Fuzhou, 36 were excluded (n = 830). Of 843 responses in Guiyang, 5 were excluded (n = 838). The final sample comprised 1,668 responses. The sample comprised 58% female and 42% male respondents (excluding 52 who preferred not to disclose gender). The age distribution was concentrated in the 35–54 age group (65.3%), with 28.7% aged 18–34 and 5.7% aged 55 and above. This study involved an anonymous online survey with no sensitive personal data collection and posed minimal risk to participants. Formal ethics review was not required under the applicable institutional guidelines. Study information was displayed to all participants through the survey platform prior to participation, and completion of the survey indicated voluntary informed consent. 2.3 Measuring green space accessibility To capture the multidimensional nature of green spaces’ access and experience, we constructed several composite indices by combining multiple survey items into standardized scores for each dimension. The procedure followed established methodological guidelines (Nardo et al., 2008), which cover theoretical framework specification, indicator selection, data normalization, multivariate analysis, weighting, aggregation, and sensitivity testing. Similar composite approaches have been applied in urban green space research (Biernacka and Kronenberg, 2018; Zhang et al., 2020). Green space accessibility operates through two dimensions, introduced as follows: (1) objective accessibility reflects physical distance, travel time, and service areas, and (2) perceived accessibility captures individuals’ self-assessed ability to engage in preferred green space activities, shaped by safety perceptions, quality assessments, and personal familiarity including behavioral engagement and satisfaction with provision (Lättman et al., 2016b; Lättman et al., 2018). Previous studies indicated that both objective and perceived access to public and private green space were associated with better health and wellbeing (Poortinga et al., 2021). In this study, objective accessibility is represented at the city scale through the contrasting spatial profiles of Fuzhou and Guiyang established by prior GIS-based research (Section 2.1). Perceived accessibility is measured by the individual scale through three composite indices: private green advantage, public park engagement, and perceived public accessibility. The three indices were developed because the analysis requires comparing private and public green dimensions across residents. The underlying survey items are measured on different scales and cannot be compared in raw form. Each index was developed through a systematic process considering: (a) theoretical conceptualization of component dimensions; (b) normalization of components to a standard 0–1 scale; (c) assessment of internal consistency using Cronbach’s α; verification of one-dimensionality through principal components analysis (PCA); (d) determination of the weighting scheme based on empirical loadings; and (e) sensitivity analysis to confirm robustness. The developed indices to conduct the green space accessibility analysis are elaborated on in the following sections: 2.3.1 Private green advantage index The private green advantage index was developed to evaluate the overall benefit of community green space within residents’ living communities, which is based on four dimensions: usage frequency, reported size, perceived maintenance quality, and perceived importance of gated community green space. These components reflect behavioral engagement, environmental assessment, and subjective value, hence represent the relative advantage residents gain from community green spaces. Index weights were derived from normalized PCA loadings rather than assuming equal contributions: quality (0.296), size (0.278), usage (0.238), and importance (0.189). 2.3.2 Public park engagement index The public park engagement index was created based on the usage frequency and perceived importance ratings of public parks. This measure captures both behavioral and motivational dimensions of public park use. Both dimensions were normalized to the 0–1 scale, with higher values indicating greater engagement. Equal weighting was applied based on equal empirical loadings. 2.3.3 Perceived park accessibility index The perceived park accessibility index measures residents’ perceived accessibility to public parks, following the conceptualization of perceived accessibility as a subjective assessment of an individual’s ability to access desired activities (Lättman et al., 2018; Lättman et al., 2016a). It combines perceived ease of reaching and perceived walking time to both mini and comprehensive parks, reflecting residents’ self-assessed ability to access valued green amenities beyond a simple measure of distance. Equal weighting was applied across the four components (0.25 each) based on balanced empirical loadings. Each index was validated by assessing internal consistency (Cronbach’s α), verifying unidimensionality (PCA), and conducting sensitivity analyses comparing weighted and equal-weighted versions. In addition to the composite indices, an unmet needs measure was derived from single survey items asking how well available green spaces meet respondents’ activity needs, for private community green spaces and public parks separately. Respondents selecting “rarely meet needs” or “don't meet needs” were classified as having unmet needs. A positive association was interpreted as evidence consistent with complementarity, where individuals who visit more and benefit more from private green spaces also tend to use public parks more, whereas a negative correlation would be more consistent with substitution. To assess whether this relationship differs by park type, we further disaggregate this analysis into mini parks and comprehensive parks. Following China’s Urban Green Space Categorization Standards, mini parks are small-scale public green spaces, typically under 1 ha, with high vegetation coverage and basic neighborhood-level facilities such as benches, simple pathways, and small exercise equipment. By contrast, comprehensive parks are larger parks of 1 ha or more that serve multiple communities and typically provide a wider range of recreational functions, such as playgrounds, sports facilities, lakes, and extensive walking paths. This distinction is analytically important as mini parks are more similar to community green spaces in scale and neighborhood orientation, whereas comprehensive parks serve broader and more multifunctional recreational roles. To identify populations experiencing different configurations of green space advantages, we developed a two-dimensional typology. This typology classifies residents based on their combined private and public green space access, enabling identification of groups invisible to analysis focusing on public parks alone. The typology was constructed by cross-classifying residents on two dimensions: private green advantage and perceived public accessibility. Perceived public accessibility was used as the second typology dimension as it reflects the structural conditions of green space access that residents face, independent of individual usage choices. For private green advantage, the sample median (50th percentile) was used as the threshold, with values above the median coded as “good” and those below as “poor”. For perceived public accessibility, residents were classified as having poor or good access if their composite score fell below the sample median or if they rated the accessibility of either mini parks or comprehensive parks as difficult or very difficult (Table 2). This compound classification captures residents who report specific access barriers even when their composite score is above the median. The median split was applied because each dimension has a meaningful interpretation of good versus poor access, and the median provides a transparent and replicable threshold. Theoretically, this typology is expected to produce roughly balanced proportions across four categories. Deviations from balanced distribution indicate skewed access configurations across the population, providing a framework for assessing environmental justice outcomes under different configurations of public and private green space. Sensitivity analysis using alternative thresholds, tertile classification, and k-means clustering (k = 4) confirmed the robustness of the four-group structure (Appendix Table A1). First, to examine whether private green spaces substitute for or complement public park engagement, we calculated Pearson correlations between the private green advantage index and the public park engagement index (RQ1). A positive correlation was interpreted as evidence consistent with complementarity (cumulative advantage), while a negative correlation would suggest substitution (compensation). To address the possibility that this association reflects shared sociodemographic determinants rather than an independent relationship between private green advantage and public park engagement, we further estimated a multiple regression model with public park engagement as the dependent variable and private green advantage, income, age, gender, household structure, and residence duration as covariates. Stratified correlations by income group and age group were also calculated as robustness checks. As a further sensitivity analysis, the private green advantage index was recalculated without its usage component, and a provision-only measure based on reported private green size and quality was examined in relation to public park usage while accounting for private green usage and importance. Second, to identify environmental justice groups and examine spatial variation (RQ2), we applied the environmental justice typologies described above and then compared across cities and districts. Third, to examine perceptions of green space equity (RQ3), we analyzed survey responses on substitution beliefs, equity endorsement, and fairness perceptions, comparing the results across environmental justice groups. All statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.11.13. 3 Results 3.1 Descriptive patterns of green space access and use A total of 1,553 respondents (93.11%) reported access to community green spaces within their gated residential complexes, consistent with the prevalence of gated community development in contemporary Chinese cities. Across the combined sample from Fuzhou and Guiyang, access to and use of both private community green spaces and public parks were widespread (Table 3). A large majority of respondents (93.1%) reported having community green spaces within their residential compounds, and 91.0% indicated that they actively used these spaces. Public parks were used even more frequently: 96.6% of the respondents visited mini parks and 97.4% visited comprehensive parks at least occasionally. These descriptive patterns confirm that both private and public green spaces are integral to urban inhabitants’ everyday environments in both cities. The near-universal usage of community green spaces confirms the assumption of the importance of parks in contemporary second-tier cities and highlights the importance of understanding environmental justice dynamics. A series of correlation tests reveals a strong, consistent pattern of complementarity. A positive and statistically significant association was found between the private green advantage index and the public park engagement index (r = 0.420, p < 0.001) (Figure 2). This indicates that residents who use and value their community green spaces more also tend to visit public parks more often and consider them more important. Instead of compensating for a lack of public park access, active engagement with one type of green environment appears to co-occur with engagement with the other. To assess whether this association was attributable to shared demographic factors, a multiple regression model was estimated with public park engagement; income, age, gender, presence of children, residence duration and city as covariates. The private green advantage remained positively and significantly associated with public park engagement after adjustment (B = 0.339, standardized β = 0.351, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.292,0.387]). Income was not a significant predictor after adjustment. The full regression results are reported in Appendix Table A2. Stratified correlations further revealed positive and significant correlations across all income groups (r = 0.387–0.447) and all age groups (r = 0.319–0.432). These results indicate that the observed association is not driven solely by available demographic factors. In the sensitivity analysis, excluding private green usage reduced the correlation with public park engagement from r = 0.420 to r = 0.321, while the provision-only measure remained positively associated with public park usage after accounting for private green usage and importance (standardized β = 0.107, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.057,0.157]). The complementarity correlations were similar. Private green advantage was positively correlated with public park engagement at r = 0.398 (p < 0.001) in Fuzhou and at r = 0.440 (p < 0.001) in Guiyang. The park-type differentiation also replicated in both cities: stronger correlations with mini park usage (Fuzhou: r = 0.368; Guiyang: r = 0.432) than with comprehensive park usage (Fuzhou: r = 0.287; Guiyang: r = 0.314). The environmental justice typology distributions were also similar between the two cities. The Advantaged proportion was 29.6% in Fuzhou and 29.4% in Guiyang, while the proportion of Disadvantaged residents was 28.2% in both cities. A chi-square test confirmed no statistically significant difference in typology distribution between cities (χ2 = 1.560, p = 0.669). For the district-level analysis, nine districts with more than 100 respondents were retained: four in Fuzhou (Cangshan, Gulou, Jinan, and Taijiang) and five in Guiyang (Baiyun, Guanshanhu, Huaxi, Nanming, and Yunyan). District-level typology distributions varied across these districts (Appendix Table A3). The Disadvantaged proportion ranged from 19.4% (Guanshanhu) to 35.2% (Huaxi). Districts within Fuzhou showed statistically similar typology distributions (χ2 = 10.64, p = 0.3012), while Guiyang districts showed significant variation (χ2 = 31.289, p = 0.0018). Across all nine districts, the correlation between private green advantage and public park engagement remained positive and significant, with coefficients ranging from 0.294 to 0.445. In all districts, correlations were stronger for mini parks than for comprehensive parks. 3.5 Residents without community green spaces Of the 1,668 respondents, 6.9% (n = 115) reported no private green in their residential community. Compared with respondents who had private green space, this group was younger, had shorter residence duration in the neighborhood, reported lower income, and was less likely to have children. Among respondents without community green space, 62.6% were aged 18–34, compared with 26.1% among those with private green spaces. Similarly, 59.2% had lived in the neighborhood for 2 years or less, compared with 31.7% among those with private green space, and 61.7% had no children, compared with 35.8% in the group with community green. Respondents without private green space also reported lower perceived accessibility (mean = 0.399 vs. 0.547; t = −7.364, p < 0.001), lower public park engagement (0.44 vs. 0.59; t = 10.03, p < 0.001), and higher unmet needs (27.0% vs. 19.1%). Detailed distributions are reported in Appendix Table A4. 3.6 Residents’ perception of green space equity Regarding self-assessed relative accessibility, most respondents reported it positively. 42.4% rated access as similar to average, 35% as somewhat better, and 12.6% as much better. Only 7.4% rated access as worse than average. For the statement “To what extent do you agree: ‘Having good private green spaces in my community reduces my need to visit public parks’”, 46.0% of the respondents answered Strongly agree/Agree. Importantly, substitution belief varied systematically with actual green space advantage: the Advantaged group showed the highest agreement (62.8%), while the Disadvantaged group showed the lowest (30.0%) (Table 6). Those with good private green spaces are more likely to believe it reduces their need for public parks, even though this group shows the highest public park engagement, contradicting their stated belief. The correlation between substitution belief and public park engagement was weakly negative (r = −0.180, p < 0.001). The complementarity correlation (r = 0.420) far exceeds this effect, indicating that behavioral complementarity dominates substitution beliefs. Residents with better private community green space visit public parks more often and value them more. This relationship is found across both cities, all districts, and both park types. This behavioral complementarity sits in productive tension with the dominant finding in the Chinese economic literature on private and public green amenities, which has relied on hedonic pricing methods. Zhang et al. (2025) show that in neighborhoods in Beijing with higher community greening rates, the capitalization effect of nearby public parks on housing prices weakens, suggesting market-level substitution between private and public green provision. This market-level substitution and the behavioral complementarity documented here are not contradictory. They describe different facets of the same pattern, in which private and public green amenities co-locate in higher-value neighborhoods and co-occur at the level of resident experience. One interpretation of this clustering of greater private green advantage and higher public park engagement is provided by the cumulative advantage framework developed by Merton (1968) and systematized by DiPrete and Eirich (2006). Cumulative advantage describes any process in which an initial favorable position becomes a resource that generates further gains, producing widening gaps over time. In the present case, the pattern may partly reflect differences in housing market position. Households able to afford higher-priced gated communities gain access to better private community green space. The same position also locates them in districts with stronger public park provision. Hence, it is associated with greater engagement with public parks. The two reported advantages co-occur rather than offset each other. This is a positive outcome, as more residents access more green space across both private and public provision. The planning concern is that combined access is unevenly distributed across housing market positions, and public provision has not kept pace with that variation. The pattern can be understood through the club goods structure of Chinese gated communities. Webster (2001) describes private green space within gated developments as a club realm, accessible only to residents who pay the entry cost through housing purchase. Atkinson and Blandy (2005) argue that this enclosure undermines the spatial mixing on which urban justice depends. The Chinese case extends the argument. As gated communities are near-universal in contemporary Chinese cities, almost all urban residents have some form of community green access. The relevant inequality is not between those with and without club membership, but between those whose membership delivers high-quality green amenities and those whose membership delivers little. The Disadvantage group in the present study is those living in lower-quality gated communities, whose private green provision is minimal and whose public park access is also constrained. The stronger complementarity with mini parks (r = 0.400) than with comprehensive parks (r = 0.300) reinforces the cumulative advantage interpretation. Mini parks as neighborhood-scale facilities share functional similarities with community green spaces while offering additional amenities and social opportunities. Residents who use their community green space frequently also incorporate nearby mini parks into their daily routines. Comprehensive parks serve more distinct recreational functions, which creates weaker coupling with private community green engagement. The cumulative advantage association is strongest at the neighborhood scale, where private and public green spaces function as parts of a single daily green environment. From a planning and institutional perspective, the near-universal access to community green space (93.1% of respondents) reflects China’s regulatory framework of residential development. Under the Code for Urban Residential Areas Planning and Design (Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development, 2018), residential projects are subject to mandatory green space ratio requirements, typically ranging from 25% to 35%, with higher density and high-rise developments often required to meet higher minimum standards. These regulations ensure that private green spaces are structurally embedded in the new residential developments, producing widespread green provision across cities. However, regulatory compliance does not guarantee green quality. Community green spaces differ substantially in usability, maintenance, and functional value. High-end residential developments tend to deliver more extensive and better-maintained green space within the compound, and are typically located in areas with stronger surrounding environmental amenities. More constrained developments meet the minimum ratio through more fragmented or poorly accessible green spaces. Compliance with a quantitative standard therefore coexists with sharp inequality in quality, consistent with the cumulative advantage observed in the complementarity patterns. A methodological consideration is that both private green advantage and public park engagement indices incorporate usage frequency as a component. The observed association may therefore partly reflect a broader individual propensity to engage with green space. The sensitivity analysis showed that removing private green usage reduced, but did not eliminate the association. While the reported private green size and quality retained a smaller positive association with public park usage after accounting for private green usage and importance. General green space engagement therefore explains part of the relationship, but does not fully account for the observed pattern. The findings remain consistent with cumulative advantage, although the magnitude of the association between private and public green engagement should be interpreted cautiously. 4.2 Environmental justice typology: the hidden inequalities The environmental justice typology reveals how green space resources are distributed across urban populations when both community and public access are considered. Four groups emerged from the intersection of private green advantage and perceived public accessibility. The Advantaged group (29.5%) and the Disadvantaged group (28.5%) account for over half of all respondents. Roughly one in three residents is experiencing a dual advantage or disadvantage. This distribution represents distributive injustice in Schlosberg's (2007) sense that environmental goods are not allocated equitably across the population. The pattern extends prior work on park accessibility inequality (Rigolon, 2016; Wolch et al., 2014) by adding the private dimension. Previous literature has largely framed park access as a public provision problem, asking who lives near public green space. While in line with SDG Target 11.7 and Paragraph 37 of the New Urban Agenda, the Chinese case reshapes the question as the near-universal gated communities provide often substantial private community green spaces for most urban residents. The inequality operates inside the gated landscape rather than outside it. The Private-Reliant group (17.4%) benefits from a higher private green advantage despite poor public access. Private provision is associated with lower perceived impact of public access deficits, resulting in relatively low unmet needs (0.069). However, compensation exists but remains a minority pattern, indicating that relying on private amenities to offset public deficits produces a small, Private-Reliant group alongside a much larger group that lacks both. The Disadvantaged and Public-Reliant groups represent more vulnerable populations that require considerate planning attention. The Disadvantaged group (28.2%) has inadequate access to both private and public green spaces, and shows higher unmet needs (0.133), indicating welfare deficits. Quality of life consequences are well documented in the health and environmental literatures (Stigsdotter et al., 2010; Zhang and Qian, 2024), including elevated heat exposure and fewer opportunities for physical activities. When these conditions co-occur with housing-related material constraints, the cumulative burden is compounded. The Public-Reliant group (18.3%) presents a different vulnerability profile. These residents currently maintain adequate green space access through public parks, compensating for limited private green provision. However, their situation is precarious. Unlike the Aadvantaged and Private-Reliant group, who have better private community green space as a buffer, the Public-Reliant group has no fallback if public park access is reduced. Their welfare depends on the public park investments; any reduction in the public park provision would harm this group. Standard planning evaluations focused on current access levels would not flag this population as at risk; their vulnerability is latent. These combined findings set preconditions for green gentrification (Wolch et al., 2014). Public investment that raises park accessibility in disadvantaged districts risks triggering displacement. Planning strategies should target disadvantaged areas directly, combine park investment with affordable housing, protect Public-Reliant populations from service downgrade, and move beyond per capita green space standards that treat private and public provision interchangeably. Beyond the four typologies, 6.9% of respondents (n = 115) lack any private green space, representing the most vulnerable population in the sample. Their demographic profile reveals environmental injustice rooted in housing market stratification. They are significantly younger, relatively newcomers, and do not have children. This pattern reflects how China’s urban housing system is structured by income, hukou (household registration) status, and housing market position. Recent migrants and lower-income workers may occupy those old settlements, or cheaper rental accommodations where green provision is limited or absent. These residents may be in transitional life stages, and they choose housing that meets immediate affordability but lacks environmental amenities. Critically, this group showed lower engagement with public parks, indicating that they do not compensate for private deficits through public access. Various barriers, such as lower income limiting leisure time and transportation, shorter residence duration, reduced familiarity with local parks, and adjusted expectations that prioritize other needs over green space, could contribute to explaining this phenomenon. Though this group is a small minority, the population is the clearest empirical case of cumulative disadvantage in the sample and warrants targeted policy attention. 4.3 Perception, behavior, and the limits of objective measurement The similar environmental justice outcomes in Fuzhou and Guiyang were surprising considering differences in measured accessibility. The typology distribution is statistically indistinguishable (x2 = 1.560, p = 0.669). The Advantaged share is 29.6% and 29.4%, respectively, while the Disadvantaged group accounts for 28.2% in both cities. The complementarity correlations are also similar. The complementarity extends robustly to the district level, across all nine districts examined, complementarity remained positive and significant. In contrast, the typology distribution varies significantly across districts within Guiyang (χ2 = 31.289, p = 0.0018), with the Disadvantaged proportion ranging from 19.4% (Guanshanhu) to 35.2% (Huaxi). This suggests that environmental justice differences are more visible at the intra-urban scale than at the city-comparison scale. One explanation is that Fuzhou and Guiyang share similar residential development structures despite differences in public park provision. In post-reform Chinese cities, gated communities have become a key mechanism through which residential status, service quality, and neighborhood amenities are bundled together. Wu (2005)argues that commodity housing enclaves sort residents through housing affordability and service differentiations, producing fragmentation at the city scale but relative homogenization within residential compounds. Tomba (2010) similarly interprets gated residential developments as a mechanism of social and spatial classification. From this perspective, community green space is not merely a local amenity but functions as part of a broader gated housing system. Similar typology distributions across Fuzhou and Guiyang may therefore reflect comparable structures of gated-community development even where objective public park accessibility differs. This convergence also supports the argument by Lättman et al. (2018) that perceived accessibility cannot be reduced to spatial measures. Perceived accessibility captures factors that objective metrics do not, including familiarity, safety, and the importance of green space for residents. When private community green space dominates the daily green experiences, as 93.1% of urban residents have, the city-level difference in public park provision becomes less consequential for how residents perceive and use parks. In addition to behavioral measures, residents’ perceptions of green space quality show how environmental justice positioning influences their awareness and attitudes. As many as 62.8% of the Advantaged group agreed (strongly agree and agree) that private green space reduces their need for public parks, while this group records the highest public park engagement. This stated belief contradicts actual behavior. Behavioral complementarity is stronger than substitution belief. The implication is that residents may underestimate how much their daily green space experience depends on combined access to both private and public provision. These divergences carry recognitional justice implications (Schlosberg, 2007). Justice perceptions vary systematically by typology. Strong majorities (76.4%) endorsed the equity principle that all residents should have equal access to public parks, with Advantaged residents showing the highest agreement (89.6%) and the Disadvantaged group the lowest (65.5%). Similarly, 68.5% agreed that the current distribution is fair, with a 25.4 percentage-point gap between the Advantaged (81.1%) and the Disadvantaged group (55.7%). Those experiencing the most disadvantage express the weakest support for equity principles and the lowest endorsement of current fairness. Planning processes that rely on public consultation will systematically underweight the needs of disadvantaged residents because those residents are less likely to frame their situation in distributive justice terms. Planning evaluations should therefore combine objective accessibility metrics with perceived accessibility data and stratified justice perception data, rather than relying on any single measure. 4.4 Gentrification, environmental justice, and policy directions The cumulative advantage pattern in this study is consistent with the structural preconditions for green gentrification. When public park investments are made in advantaged neighborhoods, it is associated with rising housing prices and displacement pressure on lower-income residents (Wolch et al., 2014). This same housing market position that delivers high-quality private green space attracts further public investment and is associated with compounding both green amenity and price pressure. In Beijing, comprehensive park development triggers significant increases in housing prices and stronger displacement effects in neighborhoods of marginalized residents (Wu and Rowe, 2022). The Fuzhou and Guiyang results suggest the same dynamic operates in second-tier cities, where the Advantaged group now occupies neighborhoods with both strong private and public green space provision. Public park investments should therefore prioritize districts where the typology identified concentrations of Disadvantaged and Public Reliant residents. Planners can apply the typology as a diagnostic tool by cross-classifying residents on private green advantage and perceived public accessibility at the district level. This directs investments towards areas of greatest need, in line with SDG Target 11.7 and Paragraph 37 of the New Urban Agenda. Compared to large comprehensive parks in well-served areas, mini parks at the neighborhood scale fit disadvantaged areas best since they tie more closely to residents’ daily green space use. Park investment should be paired with anti-displacement instruments such as affordable housing protections. Planning standards should disaggregate green space provision into private and public dimensions, rather than aggregating them into per capita targets. Aggregated metrics overstate equity by treating private community green space as equivalent to public park access. The typology developed here offers a disaggregation framework. Together these directions shift the framing of environmental justice. Green provision is no longer the only question. The further question is who can access and remain in green-rich neighborhoods. In gated-community-dominated cities, equitable green space planning is inseparable from housing affordability and residential stability. Progress is measured by whether residents across all housing market levels can access adequate green space, leaving no group without provision. 4.5 Limitation Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings above. First, the analysis relied on self-reported survey data, which can be influenced by recall bias and social desirability effects, especially in reported green space usage, perceived accessibility, and equity perceptions. While perceptions are the key focus of this study, they might not always accurately reflect actual behavior or real-world conditions. Second, comparison with the 7th National Population Census (2020) reveals that female respondents are overrepresented (58%) relative to both Fuzhou (48.7%) and Guiyang (48.9%), and older adults aged 55 and above are substantially underrepresented (5.7%) compared wo the census population aged 60 and above in Fuzhou (16.8%) and Guiyang (13.3%). Older adults are often frequent users of community green spaces and parks in Chinese cities. Their underrepresentation may lead to underestimation of the complementarity pattern. Future research should employ mixed-mode data collection strategies to ensure broader demographic coverage. Third, although objective accessibility is represented through city-scale case selection, the study does not include individual-level objective spatial accessibility measures. Fine-grained spatial accessibility data would allow individual -level assessment of perception-reality mismatch and complement the perceptual analysis. Finally, the cross-sectional design supports associational rather than causal interpretation. The cumulative advantage framework is applied as a theoretically motivated interpretive lens rather than as a directly demonstrated mechanism. Although the regression analysis indicates that the complementarity pattern persists after controlling for available demographic covariates, longitudinal research would be needed to test the causal direction. 5 Conclusion This study examined the relationship between private community green space and public parks in Fuzhou and Guiyang, organized around three research questions. First, private community green spaces were positively associated with public park engagement rather than substituting for it (r = 0.420, p < 0.001), with stronger coupling for mini parks than for comprehensive parks. The pattern is consistent with cumulative advantages rather than compensation, with combined private and publicgreen provision concentrated among well-resourced residents. Secondly, the environmental justice typology identified four groups: Advantaged, Private-Reliant, Public-Reliant, and Disadvantaged. Their distributions are statistically indistinguishable between the two cities, despite differences in objective spatial accessibility. This typology highlights that lived green space experiences do not reflect GIS-measured provision. However, within cities, district-level typology distribution varies. About one-third of residents faced disadvantages in both private and public aspects. Third, justice perceptions varied systematically by typology, with Disadvantaged residents expressing weaker agreement with equity principles and weaker endorsement of current fairness than Advantaged residents, suggesting that distributive disadvantage became harder to articulate for those experiencing it. These findings showed that private green provision is associated with reinforcement rather than compensation of public access advantages. The typology developed in our study offers a diagnostic framework for identifying groups carrying dual disadvantage or latent vulnerability and advancing more just green space provision in cities characterized by gated residential development.
