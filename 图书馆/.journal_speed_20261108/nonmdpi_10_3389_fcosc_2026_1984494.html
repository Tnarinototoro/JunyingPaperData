Abstract

Contemporary socio-ecological polycrisis encompasses several urgent crises, including climate change, biodiversity loss, and related sustainability concerns, stemming from the dissociation between humans and nature. This dissociation results from how we design everyday ecosystems, products, services, and technologies, where nature is treated as a backdrop rather than an active participant. This perspective paper advocates a relational shift in design practice by placing humans embedded in the ecological system with other species, rather than separate from it. Grounded in relational ontology and pluriverse onto-epistemology, this research critiques the conventional anthropocentric design paradigms and proposes a pluriversal alternative that regards multispecies entanglement, values and ownership. Further, it engages with cosmologies and epistemologies of indigenous knowledge, which are understood as living expressions of both relationality (as lived practices) and pluriversal thought. To validate these, this study conducts a small-scale pilot study in a village within Cauvery Wildlife Sanctuary, southern India. Inspired by Wakkary's (2021) 'design with' methodology, the present paper proposes a place-responsive methodology that embraces multispecies and ecological storytelling, relational mapping, and community-led indigenous knowledge. It shows how the relational design approach generates ecocentric learning that reconnects the community and the design practitioners with nature through participation and co-creation. This paper argues that conservation and design should shift, which requires integrating decolonising methods and pluriverse theory into governance. This affirms plural ways of knowing being and relating to nature, supporting Escobar's (2018) vision of 'a world where many worlds fit', fostering cultural integrity, ecological justice, and holistic well-being through relationships rather than control.
