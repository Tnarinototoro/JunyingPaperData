Rain Crow Consulting, Schuyler, VA, United States Article metrics View details Abstract Scholars have proposed the adoption of a contemporary global taboo against bats to prevent the outbreak of diseases to which humans are susceptible (zoonotic diseases). Zoonotic disease outbreaks are driven by environmental change and can result in collective human traumas that span generations. The proposal inspired us to explore the extent and characteristics of traditional wildlife taboos for zoonotic disease risk mitigation. We conducted a literature review to assess the role of zoonotic disease as a catalyst for traditional taboo development and enculturation. Out of concern that contemporary taboos could facilitate or amplify collective human trauma, we also contemplated the ethics of constructing and promoting contemporary wildlife taboos to serve health security aims. In this Perspective, we report on our review of traditional wildlife taboos as they relate to zoonotic disease risk mitigation, noting information shortfalls and observable linkages. We also respond to the call for contemporary wildlife taboos by exploring neocolonial approaches to conservation and achieving public health goals; the dynamics of values, ethics, and morals; and potential adverse impacts on biodiversity conservation. We conclude that the historical role of taboos as risk mitigation measures is not well-established due to data limitations; linkages cannot be readily drawn between specific wildlife species and specific pathogens. However, in general, there appears to be a reasonable likelihood that some taboos arose when communities repeatedly observed human health problems following contact with certain wildlife species. We do not recommend contemporary taboos as risk mitigation measures. If “taboo campaigns” are developed, we urge socio-cultural sensitivity, knowledge, and community participation. To safeguard the affected communities, socio-cultural and ecological impact assessments should be conducted as standard practice. 1 Introduction Osofsky et al. (2023) state that “a global taboo is needed whereby humanity agrees to leave bats alone, not fear them or try to chase them away or cull them, but to let them have the habitats they need and live undisturbed by humans.” Their perspective arises from the observation that: a) bats are known to host a wide range of high-consequence pathogens, some with pandemic potential, and b) that relatively few people worldwide would be inconvenienced if bat consumption and other interactions (e.g., bat guano harvesting) were prohibited. They further emphasize that human behavior changes aimed at preventing zoonotic (wildlife-to-human) disease outbreaks at the source are the most socially equitable risk mitigation measures globally, and thriving bat populations benefit humans via the ecosystem services bats provide, including insect control and crop pollination. They go on to say that many taboos were originally spurred by ecological or health concerns, citing a paper on food taboos by Meyer-Rochow (2009), and conclude that adopting a bat taboo would serve humanity. Although the etymology of ‘taboo’ is not straightforward, there is general agreement that the word is derived from one or more Polynesian words denoting the practice of avoidance for a religious or otherwise traditionally sacred purpose (e.g., “tapu” denoting something holy or sacred, with “spiritual restriction” or “implied prohibition”). In contemporary (present-day) discourse, particularly in Western Culture, the concept of taboo generally refers to topics, settings, individual actions, or practices that are socio-culturally prohibited because they are believed to be harmful or potentially harmful. Taboos are thus protective and adaptive in intent (Duschinsky, 2014; Hong, 2024). We define and contextualize ‘taboo’ and, more explicitly, ‘wildlife taboo,’ to refer to a socio-culturally embedded practice of avoiding direct or indirect contact with specific wildlife species for a societal benefit. The proposal to formulate and institute a contemporary wildlife taboo as a health security measure captured our attention from two primary perspectives: First, we were inspired to explore the extent and characteristics of traditional wildlife taboos as a zoonotic disease (zoonoses) risk mitigation. What is the evidence for zoonotic disease as a catalyst for taboo development and enculturation? From a broader perspective, we were interested in gaining insights into the function of taboos for preventing collective human trauma induced by environmental degradation. Land-use change can drive the emergence of zoonotic pathogens, as well as their spread from wildlife to people, thereby putting human lives and livelihoods at risk (Plowright et al., 2021; Reaser et al., 2022). Zoonotic disease outbreaks, particularly at large scale, can induce and/or amplify multi-dimensional collective trauma responses with impacts that span generations (Convery et al, 2008; Taylor, 2020; Kaubisch et al., 2022). Secondly, we wondered about the ethics of instilling contemporary wildlife taboos. Although it can certainly be argued that a relatively small percentage of the approximately 8 billion people inhabiting Earth engage in the consumption of bats or bat derivatives, the substantial socio-ecological role that bats play in some Indigenous and traditional communities is undeniable. For these people, bats are more than utilitarian features; they are intimately tied to spiritual and cultural identity and well-being (Low et al., 2021; Rocha et al., 2023). Might installing contemporary wildlife taboos in the psyche of Indigenous and traditional peoples be akin to missionaries introducing Western religious constructs, inflicting negative externalities on people who embody nature-based spirituality? Missionaries often sought to shift Indigenous peoples perspectives of wildlife and wild places from sacred to profane, with adverse consequences for social and ecological systems (Onnekink, 2022). “Taboo ethics” also have conservation implications. Authors reviewing the influence of traditional (long-established) wildlife taboos on biodiversity conservation have concluded that species have benefited from human avoidance practices (Colding and Folke, 1997; Landim et al., 2023). For example, Colding and Folke (1997) and Ihinmikaiye et al. (2022) explain that taboos regulate the consumption and use of local resources, functioning as resource management systems that help protect and conserve species. However, what if taboos foster a fear of wildlife and the ecological systems they inhabit? Could taboos instilled as zoonoses risk mitigation measures drive biophobia (dissociation from/aversion to nature) with adverse consequences for conservation? In this Perspective, we report on our review of traditional wildlife taboos as they relate to zoonotic disease risk mitigation, noting information shortfalls and observable linkages. We also respond to the call for contemporary wildlife taboos as a measure to mitigate zoonoses risks by exploring neocolonial approaches to achieving conservation and public health goals; the dynamics of values, ethics, and morals; and potential adverse impacts on biodiversity conservation. 2 Wildlife taboos: historical zoonoses risk mitigation Taboos are established in accordance with prevailing cultural, societal, or spiritual practices to serve a wide range of community functions. For example, taboos may be used to prohibit the harvest and consumption of plants and animals (“totemic species”) that have substantial roles in spiritual cosmologies (Meyer-Rochow, 2009; Janssen et al., 2024). We set out to assess the extent to which traditional taboos may have functioned as zoonoses risk mitigation measures. Our methodological approach is described in Box 1. Data on 816 taboos were harvested from 54 peer-reviewed manuscripts that contained investigation-relevant information (Supplementary 1), although we were challenged by data quality. Most studies we examined were conducted in Africa and Asia. Collectively, the taboos spanned a wide range of species across mammal, bird, reptile, amphibian, fish, mollusk, and crustacean taxonomies. With few exceptions, the taboos imposed controls on food consumption. Many were oriented towards women and relevant to pregnancy. Box 1 Literature review methodology. We identified relevant peer-reviewed literature by searching for “wildlife taboos and zoonotic disease,” “wildlife taboos and zoonoses,” “wildlife taboos and case studies,” “wildlife taboos and lists,” and “wildlife taboos and examples” in digital libraries (Google Scholar, PubMed Central, BioMed, Web of Science) in English and Spanish. The Spanish-language search did not produce viable returns. For the English-language search, in total, we reviewed 1,577 paper titles for project applicability. This led to further review of 446 abstracts and the identification of 54 potentially viable for data collection (Figure 1). In addition to noting the reference source, we cataloged information on the wildlife species at which the taboo was directed (target species) and what specific prohibition applied to that animal (e.g., not killing it). In keeping with the taboo type designations described by Angsongna et al. (2016)food taboos are those that ban the consumption of the entire animal, or certain parts, species specific taboos ban the killing and use of a species at all times, life-history taboos ban the use of species at specific life stages, social taboos ban the use of specific animals or activities, segment taboos ban the use of an animal at given timeframes, and religious taboos ban the harvesting or consumption of specific species. Alexander et al. (2017) describe cultural taboos as social rules rooted in shared beliefs that help regulate how people use natural resources. There were no specific designations for “health taboos.” Because most, if not all, taboos apply to specific groups and segments of a community, we were attentive to the specific age groups, genders, social circumstances, and locations associated with each taboo. To identify possible linkages between taboos and zoonotic disease (Supplementary 1, Column M), to the extent feasible, we utilized the species, mode of contact, locality, and symptoms/conditions (regarding taboo function or violation) as described by the authors. Entering this information into Google Scholar (e.g., by asking what the linkage is between the species, locality, and conditions), we conducted broad information searches for peer-reviewed literature that provided insights into potential linkages between the taboo and zoonotic disease. We identified 134 instances for which there is a reasonable likelihood of association between the taboo and at least one zoonotic pathogen or parasite. In most cases, the symptoms/conditions could be attributed to multiple zoonoses. Due to the paucity of information provided by the authors and the extensive time required to explore possible linkages, we did not attempt to pinpoint the specific cause of illness, only to ascertain that there was a reasonable linkage between the species (or taxonomic group), mode of contact, locality, and condition(s). See Supplementary 1, blue rows. As discussed within the paper narrative, identified linkages should not be interpreted as evidence of the zoonotic origin of the associated taboos. Due to the poor quality of data available in the literature, this exercise only elucidates a reasonable likelihood of potential linkages (hypothetical linkages). We identified 134 instances for which there is a reasonable likelihood of an association (hypothetical linkage) between the health conditions reportedly linked to the taboo and at least one zoonotic pathogen or parasite (Supplementary 1, blue rows). We use the phrase “reasonable likelihood of association” because substantial data limitations prevent definitive evaluation. The taboo-zoonoses associations are too incomplete, broad, and tentative to warrant critical analysis. The statements and references in Column M are illustrative only; they should not be regarded as definitive results of comprehensive scholarly analysis. For the most part, it was impossible to correlate the species of wild animal targeted by the taboo (hereafter, target species) with a specific zoonotic disease attributable to the reported ailments. Challenges arose when determining the target species and potential disease factors associated with taboos. In some cases, the target species was undeterminable because only vague common names were provided, or the common names were provided in an unspecified language. Mistakes or misunderstandings may have also arisen via translation errors. Because many of the articles were written from an anthropological perspective, the authors may not have had the knowledge or motivation necessary to identify the target species. Many papers lacked sufficient information on physical symptoms/conditions associated with taboos, thereby preventing further investigation. In several cases, multiple zoonoses (from pathogens and/or parasites) are attributable to the symptoms/conditions described by the author(s). Information limitations prevented further discernment and specific cause-and-effect determinations. Overall, our literature review revealed a paucity of evidence for the development and enculturation of taboos as zoonoses risk mitigation measures. In many respects, this is not surprising. First, “zoonotic disease” is a contemporary scientific construct. Indigenous and traditional peoples are unlikely to have a cosmology for illness that is readily transferable to scientific principles and terminology (Mji, 2019; Temple of the Way of Light, 2026), thereby hindering information interpretation and documentation by researchers. Secondly, it is feasible that taboos were passed down orally from generation to generation and thus are susceptible to the loss of cultural narratives. Wildlife ethics are often seen as a private matter or a static inheritance from past generations, confounding research capacity (von Essen, 2018). Third, even when enacting contemporary medical practice, the linkages between zoonotic disease and human behavior may not be readily apparent. Zoonotic pathogens and parasites may not have dramatic symptoms and/or there may be substantial lag times between contracting a zoonotic organism and the advent of symptoms (Bean et al., 2013; Rahman et al., 2020). Under such conditions, communities lack an impetus for mitigating risky behavior. Fourth, even where knowledge of contemporary medicine exists, information about the zoonotic diseases circulating within and among communities is often lacking. When conducting a survey to evaluate health perceptions associated with wildlife taboos in Thailand’s Tak and Mae Hong Son provinces, Suwannarong et al. (2024) found that respondents with higher levels of education had a deeper understanding of zoonotic disease risk. They note that some cultural groups routinely interact with wildlife to fulfill various cultural practices but lack an understanding of disease risks. Lastly, the literature we reviewed indicates relatively little topic engagement from scholars in the health sector. Most authors were from social science disciplines and pursued interests in broader socio-cultural paradigms and/or narratives. The work of Angsongna et al. (2016) is a notable exception; the interdisciplinary team conducted a systematic review of resource habitat taboos and human health outcomes in the context of global environmental change. The strongest potential linkages between wildlife species, taboo in a given locality, and a zoonotic condition appear to involve rat lungworm (Angiostrongylus cantonensis), a roundworm parasite that ultimately infects the brain and nervous system of rats. Intermediate hosts include snails that eat infected rat species. The infected snails may then be consumed by people or other animals, such as reptiles, that humans consume raw or undercooked. Although most people are asymptomatic, the parasite can migrate to the brain, causing a wide range of neurological symptoms associated with eosinophilic meningitis (e.g., Uyeda et al., 2016; Mendoza-Roldan et al., 2020; Yong et al., 2022). Golden and Comaroff (2015) claim that the Makira in northeastern Madagascar have food taboos on lemurs because consuming these animals can cause gastrointestinal symptoms; these animals are known to host zoonotic pathogens of the gut, such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium. However, a recent study in Madagascar did not find lemurs to be positive for either parasite (Spencer and Irwin, 2020). We also reviewed accounts of species that were prohibited from being killed due to the belief that taboo violators would suffer illness, even death. For example, Ihinmikaiye et al. (2022) report that the Otuogidi and Otuag-bagi communities prohibit the killing of the Gambian mongoose via taboo, believing that if people fail to adhere to this taboo, they will suffer from severe illness resulting in death. The understanding that contact with these target species can result in deadly illness may reflect a basic conceptualization of pathogen spillover dynamics, even if not viewed through a contemporary scientific lens. Further, even when not intended as zoonoses prevention measures, such wildlife taboos may serve this function by preventing exposure to infectious animals. Despite reported and surmised linkages between taboos and zoonotic disease, some authors explicitly state that disease threat is not a factor in taboo establishment. Instead, they believe taboos strictly result from socioeconomic factors (e.g., resource availability). Further, Wormley and Cohen (2022) take the position that disease concerns are promulgated by individuals, not community consensus. Yet, a contemporary knowledge of zoonoses may be influencing community codes of conduct. Ordaz-Németh et al. (2017) analyzed prices, quantities, and food consumption before and after an Ebola outbreak. They found that a wild meat taboo arose due to awareness that the Ebola virus stemmed from contact with wild animals. 3 Wildlife taboos: contemporary taboos risk mitigation ethics We fully acknowledge the positive intent underlying the proposal for a contemporary wildlife taboo to safeguard human health. In this section, we introduce three primary concerns regarding the ethics of contemporary wildlife taboos as zoonotic risk mitigation measures. Although there may be instances in which contemporary wildlife taboos serve to prevent people from contracting diseases from wildlife, we recognize that taboos have complex roles in socio-cultural and ecological systems (Colding and Folke, 2001; Angsongna et al., 2016). Therefore, the potential negative externalities of taboo adoption must be thoughtfully investigated across these systems, on a case-by-case basis. Ideally, these impact assessments take a participatory approach, engaging community members as valued knowledge keepers and risk managers (Alders et al., 2020). 3.1 We should learn from social traumas imposed in the past People do not, in general, have the right to tell others what they should consider sacred or profane—what is to be socially embraced or avoided (Schwartz, 2021). Nevertheless, there is a long history of such imposition through socio-cultural coercion and subjugation driven by direct conflict, colonialism, and interrelated forms of systemic social oppression (e.g., racism, religious persecution). These collective human traumas have the potential to shatter cultural identity and practice, leading to the destruction of cultural heritage and, in extreme cases, cultural extinction (Hirschberger, 2018; Abrutyn, 2024). Scholars have raised concern about the colonial roots of conservation, as well as neocolonial approaches to conservation in which social constructs are used to manipulate people to achieve conservation and other aims (Büscher, 2025; Sène, 2025). Social marketing, employing commercial marketing techniques to influence behavior change for improved individual and societal welfare, has rapidly grown in popularity as a conservation approach. However, concerns have been raised about the ethics and adverse cultural impacts of conservation-aimed social marketing programs, particularly when administered by Global North organizations to Global South communities and involving Indigenous peoples (Maher and Schmidt, 2025). Could the marketing of a contemporary wildlife taboo have severe socio-cultural pitfalls? Osofsky et al. (2023) believe that human behavior changes aimed at preventing zoonotic disease outbreaks are the most socially equitable risk mitigation measures globally. However, concerns about neocolonial conservation may be directly transferable to “neocolonial One Health” in which Global North belief systems are imposed upon or marketed to Global South peoples for zoonoses risk mitigation. We should learn from the long history of social negligence and egregious misconduct, proceeding with forethought, sensitivity, and critical analysis when considering contemporary taboos as part of a zoonoses risk management portfolio. 3.2 Values, ethics, and morals are context-specific and dynamic Taboos are closely linked to group identity and thus to societal beliefs, values, ethics, and morals (Bénabou and Tirole, 2011; Hong, 2024). As discussed in the previous section, influencing these societal constructs can compel individual and collective behaviors, including codes of conduct (Reaser, 2001; von Essen, 2018). One of the most substantial logistical challenges for establishing a “global taboo” to “let [bats or other wildlife] have the habitats they need and live undisturbed by humans” (quotes per Osofsky et al., 2023) is that values, along with associated ethics (external codified principles) and morals (internal beliefs about right or wrong), are not universal. Values, and thus identities, are shaped by context across a wide spectrum of cultural, socioeconomic, and environmental variables, leading to divergent rather than globally shared ethical and moral expressions. Further, although basic human motivators (e.g., security) are sometimes regarded as “universal truths”, their hierarchical ranking in an individual’s or group’s priority system varies according to a wide range of factors, including family system, community roles, knowledge access, and experiences (Walls and Triandis, 2014; von Essen and Allen, 2016; Jackson and Medvedev, 2024). To further complicate matters, values are dynamic in the same context across time (Foad et al., 2021); Mehta et al., 2025) and are causally influenced by many of the same factors relevant to context variability, often in nonlinear ways. In keeping with the previous section on collective human trauma, values can also be manipulated at individual and collective levels through force and coercion (Hirschberger, 2018; Russo et al., 2022). For a wildlife taboo to effectively mitigate zoonoses risk, it would need to be temporally stable. While it could be argued that some historical taboos have held for a century or more, we recognize that the influences of globalization and postindustrial value complexity are destabilizing forces (Bruskotter et al., 2017; von Essen, 2018). 3.3 There could be adverse impacts on conservation Osofsky et al. (2023) do not intend for a contemporary taboo to instill fear of the target wildlife species or to cause people to chase away or cull the animals. However, we recognize the potential challenges in instituting a wildlife taboo for zoonoses risk mitigation purposes that doesn’t catalyze fear, disdain, disrespect, and/or dissociation. Cultural beliefs, including those instilled through taboos, influence perceptions of animals and can result in wildlife persecution (Williams et al., 2021). Anderson and Reaser (2024) recently explored how the fear of zoonotic disease outbreaks can lead to the culling of wildlife and/or destruction of wildlife habitats when specific species are believed to be sources of disease to which people are susceptible. A social marketing program aimed at enculturating a contemporary wildlife taboo must consider how to promote biophilia (an affinity for wildlife and habitats) while also discouraging contact with certain wildlife species and places they dwell (Kirkey, 2024). The International Alliance against Health Risks in the Wildlife Trade, in partnership with the IUCN Commission on Education and Outreach, has proposed a basic concept for the Love Them & Leave Them campaign to meet the shared goals of conservation and zoonoses risk mitigation. Such a campaign needs to be nuanced to socio-cultural contexts through community-based participatory processes (Reaser et al., 2025). It is also important to evaluate community health perceptions and practices during the campaign development process, as these perceptions are dynamic across communities and through time (Suwannarong et al., 2024). Further, because the specific wildlife species targeted by taboos exist within broader systems (Colding and Folke, 1997), ecosystem-wide ramifications need to be considered. Do ecosystem processes and functions benefit, or do detriments arise? We recommend that such questions be explored in socio-ecological impact assessments prior to the execution of social marketing programs (taboo-based or otherwise) aimed at zoonoses risk management or conservation. 4 Conclusion The historical role of taboos as risk mitigation measures is not well-established due to limitations in data quality; linkages cannot be readily drawn between specific wildlife species and specific pathogens. We are unable to determine whether the taboos were originally spurred by zoonoses concerns or if they effectively mitigated zoonoses risks. However, in general, there is a reasonable likelihood that some prohibitions, particularly on meat consumption, arose when communities repeatedly observed human illness following contact with certain wildlife species. We do not recommend contemporary taboos as risk mitigation measures. However, if “taboo campaigns” are developed, we urge socio-cultural sensitivity, knowledge, and community participation. Otherwise, efforts to prevent one type of trauma could facilitate other traumas. Although the conscious motives for separating people from wildlife may be positive, it is possible that adverse societal conditioning, such as that associated with colonialism, is unconsciously present and needs to be brought to light. Likewise, there is a need to proactively consider the ecological ramifications of campaigns for wildlife population dynamics. For any value-based campaign to be effective, it needs to be context-tailored, and its effectiveness reevaluated over time in anticipation of changes in community values and value structures. “Taboo campaigns” should be fit-to-context and, as standard practice, include socio-cultural and ecological impact assessments.
