Faculty of Economics and Management (FEP),Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia,UKM, Bangi, Selangor, Malaysia 2. School of Business, Shanxi Technology and Business University, Taiyuan, China Article metrics View details Abstract This study examines whether, how, and under what market-structure conditions corporate digital transformation improves environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance. Building on the twin green and digital transformation perspective, this study conceptualizes digital transformation as a dynamic green digital capability rather than merely the adoption of digital technologies. By integrating the Natural Resource-Based View, Stakeholder Theory, and the Porter Hypothesis, we develop a framework in which digital transformation enhances ESG performance by strengthening firms’ data-driven sustainability capabilities and enabling green technological innovation. Using panel data from Chinese A-share listed firms from 2009 to 2023, we find that digital transformation is positively associated with ESG performance, measured by Huazheng ESG ratings. Green technological innovation mediates this relationship, and the empirical results indicate that the mediation is partial and economically modest, suggesting that green innovation is only one mechanism within a broader set of digitalization–ESG channels. Market concentration strengthens the positive effect of digital transformation on ESG performance, indicating that firms in more concentrated industries are better able to appropriate returns from long-term digital and sustainability investments. Additional heterogeneity analyses show that the positive effect of digital transformation is observed across regions and ownership types. In pollution-related industry subsamples, the effect is significant among non-high-polluting firms but not statistically significant among high-polluting firms; however, the between-group difference is not statistically significant. This study contributes to the literature by clarifying the innovation mechanism and market-structure boundary condition through which digital transformation supports comprehensive ESG improvement. The findings suggest that the ESG value of digital transformation depends not only on whether firms adopt digital technologies, but also on whether they develop dynamic green digital capabilities and operate in market environments that support long-term sustainability investment. 1 Introduction Climate change and the transition toward sustainable development have become central challenges for firms, regulators, and capital markets. In response to increasing environmental pressure, China has committed to peaking carbon emissions before 2030 and achieving carbon neutrality before 2060. These national targets have accelerated the need for firms to improve environmental governance, resource efficiency, information disclosure, and broader environmental, social, and governance performance (Lee et al., 2023). At the same time, digital technologies such as big data analytics, artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and the Internet of Things are reshaping corporate operations, resource allocation, and strategic decision-making (Liu et al., 2022). These developments raise an important question: whether and how corporate digital transformation enables firms to convert digital capabilities into improved ESG performance (Chen et al., 2023). This question matters to multiple groups of stakeholders. For corporate managers, digital transformation requires substantial investment in data infrastructure, organizational restructuring, digital talent, and process redesign, but its sustainability value remains uncertain. For investors, ESG ratings have become increasingly important signals for evaluating firms’ long-term risk, legitimacy, and responsible investment potential. For regulators, digital technologies provide new tools for environmental monitoring, information disclosure, and corporate governance supervision. For customers, suppliers, employees, and local communities, firms’ digital capabilities may affect transparency, environmental responsibility, labor protection, product safety, and broader stakeholder responsiveness. Therefore, understanding whether digital transformation improves ESG performance is not only a technical issue, but also a strategic, governance, and public-policy issue. China provides an appropriate and theoretically meaningful context for examining this issue. First, China is simultaneously undergoing digital upgrading and green transition. National strategies related to the digital economy, industrial Internet development, smart manufacturing, carbon peaking, and carbon neutrality have encouraged firms to integrate digital technologies into production, governance, disclosure, and environmental management. This simultaneous pressure for digitalization and sustainability makes China a suitable setting for studying twin green and digital transformation. Second, Chinese A-share listed firms display substantial variation in digital transformation, ESG performance, green technological innovation, ownership structure, regional development, industry pollution intensity, and market concentration. This variation allows us to examine not only whether digital transformation improves ESG performance, but also how this relationship operates through green technological innovation and when it becomes stronger or weaker under different market-structure conditions. Third, although China has distinctive institutional characteristics, it is representative of large emerging economies where firms face simultaneous pressures from environmental regulation, digital-economy development, investor attention, and stakeholder legitimacy demands. Therefore, evidence from China can enrich understanding of how firms in transition economies convert dynamic digital capabilities into sustainability outcomes. At the same time, the Chinese setting should be interpreted as analytically representative rather than universally generalizable: it offers a theoretically rich context for studying large emerging economies undergoing simultaneous digital upgrading, environmental regulation, and ESG institutionalization, while cross-country validation remains an important direction for future research. Digital transformation has increasingly become a strategic priority for firms seeking to improve operational efficiency, organizational adaptability, and sustainability governance. It can fundamentally change production processes and organizational arrangements by streamlining workflows and improving productivity. As an enabling capability, digital transformation can support green technological innovation by reducing information-processing costs, improving coordination efficiency, and facilitating resource allocation for risky and long-term innovation activities (Kraus et al., 2021). From the perspective of the Natural Resource-Based View (NRBV), digital technologies can be understood as strategic resources that help companies develop pollution prevention, product stewardship, and sustainable development competencies (Hart, 1995; Hart and Dowell, 2011). Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies directly support green innovations as they help optimize research and development (R&D) and production processes. In addition, the market-oriented allocation of data as a production factor may further promote green innovation by reducing information asymmetry and easing financing constraints (Wang et al., 2025; Wang H. et al., 2023). At a broader level, digital transformation is reshaping market competition and industry ecosystems. Competition is increasingly shifting from traditional price- and scale-based competition toward data-driven, platform-based, and sustainability-oriented competition. In this context, green technological innovation is not merely a compliance response to environmental regulation; rather, it can become a strategic mechanism through which firms develop differentiated competitive advantages. This logic is consistent with the Porter Hypothesis, which emphasizes that environmental pressure may stimulate innovation and generate compensation effects (Ziaei and Roshani, 2024). In this changed market context, green technological innovation should not be viewed merely as a compliance cost associated with environmental regulation. Instead, consistent with the Porter Hypothesis, it can serve as a strategic mechanism through which firms improve resource efficiency, reduce compliance costs, and create differentiated competitive advantages through innovation compensation effects (Ambec and Barla, 2002; Hart, 1995). Recent studies have begun to examine the relationships among digital transformation, green innovation, ESG performance, and market competition (Asif et al., 2023; Li and Rasiah, 2024; Ren et al., 2024; Su, 2025; Sun et al., 2026). For instance, prior work has explored how digital transformation affects ESG performance through green innovation and internal control, how market competition shapes digitalization–ESG relationships, and how ESG–digital synergies influence corporate decarbonization. These studies provide valuable insights, but they have not fully developed an integrated framework that links dynamic green digital capabilities, green technological innovation, comprehensive ESG performance, and market-structure conditions. Existing research has provided important evidence that digital transformation can improve ESG performance, green innovation, environmental disclosure, and corporate sustainability. However, three important gaps remain. First, prior studies often treat digital transformation mainly as the adoption or disclosure of digital technologies, while paying insufficient attention to its capability-based meaning. From a twin transformation perspective, digital transformation is better understood as a dynamic green digital capability that allows firms to sense sustainability-related information, process environmental and stakeholder data, coordinate internal and external resources, and reconfigure organizational routines for ESG improvement. Second, the mechanism through which digital transformation improves comprehensive ESG performance remains insufficiently explained. Green technological innovation is often examined as an environmental outcome, but ESG performance is broader than environmental performance. It also includes social responsibility, governance quality, information disclosure, and stakeholder responsiveness. Therefore, it is necessary to clarify why and how an environmental innovation channel can contribute to comprehensive ESG ratings. Third, the boundary conditions under which digital transformation generates stronger ESG benefits remain underexplored. Market concentration is especially relevant because it may have two competing effects. On the one hand, concentrated markets may reduce competitive pressure and create monopoly complacency. On the other hand, they may provide resource slack, stable cash flows, and stronger appropriability, allowing firms to sustain long-term digital and ESG investments. To address these gaps, this study is guided by the following research questions: RQ1: Does corporate digital transformation, understood as a dynamic green digital capability rather than mere technology adoption, improve firms’ comprehensive ESG performance? RQ2: Does digital transformation promote green technological innovation, and does green technological innovation subsequently improve ESG performance? RQ3: Does green technological innovation mediate the relationship between digital transformation and corporate ESG performance? RQ4: Does market concentration strengthen or weaken the relationship between digital transformation and ESG performance? RQ5: Do the effects of digital transformation on ESG performance vary across regions, ownership types, and pollution-related industry groups? This study makes three contributions. First, it contributes to the literature on digital transformation and ESG performance by introducing a twin green and digital transformation perspective. Instead of treating digital transformation as simple technology adoption, this study conceptualizes it as a dynamic green digital capability that enables firms to collect, process, integrate, and apply sustainability-related information for ESG improvement. Second, it clarifies the innovation-based mechanism linking digital transformation to comprehensive ESG performance. By decomposing the mediation logic into the effect of digital transformation on green technological innovation and the effect of green technological innovation on ESG performance, this study explains how digital capabilities can be translated into sustainability outcomes while acknowledging that green innovation is only one channel among several ESG-enhancing mechanisms. Third, it identifies market concentration as an important boundary condition. By examining whether market concentration strengthens or weakens the digital transformation–ESG relationship, this study extends the Porter Hypothesis to a market-structure setting and shows that the ESG value of digital transformation depends on firms’ ability to appropriate returns from long-term digital and sustainability investments. 2 Literature review and hypothesis development 2.1 Twin Green and digital transformation The concept of twin green and digital transformation emphasizes the mutually reinforcing relationship between digitalization and sustainability transformation. Rather than viewing digital transformation and green transformation as two separate strategic agendas, the twin transformation perspective suggests that digital technologies can enable firms to redesign production processes, monitor environmental performance, optimize resource use, improve sustainability disclosure, and develop green technological innovation. In this study, digital transformation represents the capability side of the twin transformation. It is conceptualized as a dynamic green digital capability through which firms collect, process, integrate, and apply data related to production, energy consumption, emissions, supply chains, governance, disclosure, and stakeholder expectations. Green transformation, by contrast, refers to the sustainability-oriented outcomes of deploying these digital capabilities, including green technological innovation and improved ESG performance. This perspective helps clarify the relationship between digital transformation and ESG performance. Digital transformation does not automatically generate sustainability outcomes. Its ESG value depends on whether firms can embed digital technologies into environmental management, green innovation, governance transparency, stakeholder communication, and organizational routines. Therefore, the twin transformation perspective provides an overarching logic for examining whether digital transformation improves ESG performance, how green technological innovation transmits this effect, and when market concentration strengthens or weakens the relationship. 2.2 Integrated theoretical framework Building on the twin green and digital transformation perspective, this study integrates the Natural Resource-Based View, Stakeholder Theory, and the Porter Hypothesis to explain how dynamic green digital capabilities are converted into comprehensive corporate ESG performance. Instead of treating these theories as separate explanations, we use them to develop a three-layer theoretical framework that links internal capability formation, external stakeholder pressure, and innovation-based compensation. First, the Natural Resource-Based View provides the capability foundation of the framework. The NRBV emphasizes that firms can achieve competitive advantage by developing capabilities related to pollution prevention, product stewardship, and sustainable development. From this perspective, digital transformation enhances firms’ data-driven environmental management capabilities. Technologies such as artificial intelligence, big data analytics, cloud computing, and industrial Internet platforms enable firms to collect and process environmental information, monitor emissions, optimize energy consumption, improve resource allocation, and coordinate green supply-chain activities. Therefore, digital transformation can provide the internal capability base needed for pollution prevention, resource efficiency, and sustainable product development. Second, Stakeholder Theory explains why firms are motivated to deploy these digital capabilities for sustainability and ESG-related purposes. Firms operate within a network of stakeholders, including governments, investors, customers, suppliers, employees, communities, and the media. As these stakeholders increasingly demand environmental accountability, firms face growing pressure to disclose environmental information, reduce pollution, and improve environmental responsiveness. Digital transformation enhances information transparency and enables firms to trace, process, and communicate environmental data more effectively. Thus, stakeholder pressure transforms digital capabilities from general operational resources into tools for environmental governance and legitimacy management. Third, the Porter Hypothesis explains the innovation mechanism through which digital capabilities and stakeholder pressures are translated into environmental improvements and broader ESG outcomes. According to the Porter Hypothesis, environmental pressure may stimulate innovation and generate compensation effects by improving resource efficiency, reducing compliance costs, and creating new competitive advantages. In the context of digital transformation, digital technologies reduce information asymmetry, coordination costs, and technological search costs, thereby facilitating green technological innovation. Green technological innovation then improves corporate ESG performance through cleaner production, energy conservation, emissions reduction, and resource recycling. Finally, market concentration serves as a boundary condition in this integrated framework. Firms in more concentrated industries often possess greater market power, more stable cash flows, stronger resource slack, and greater strategic discretion. These conditions may allow them to convert digital efficiency gains into long-term environmental investments and green technological innovation. In contrast, firms in highly competitive industries may face stronger short-term cost pressures, limiting their ability to sustain digital and green transformation. Therefore, market concentration may strengthen the positive relationship between digital transformation and corporate ESG performance. Taken together, the three theories form a sequential and complementary framework. The NRBV explains the capability base created by digital transformation; Stakeholder Theory explains the external pressure and legitimacy motivation that direct these capabilities toward ESG governance; and the Porter Hypothesis explains the innovation-based mechanism through which digital capabilities and stakeholder pressure are converted into ESG improvements. Market concentration further conditions this process by shaping firms’ resource slack, strategic discretion, and ability to appropriate returns from long-term sustainability investments. 2.3 Digital transformation and corporate ESG performance Corresponding to RQ1, this subsection develops the direct-effect hypothesis linking digital transformation to comprehensive ESG performance. Consistent with the twin transformation perspective, digital transformation is treated as a dynamic green digital capability rather than simply the adoption of digital technologies. Building on the integrated framework, this subsection develops the direct-effect hypothesis by combining the NRBV and Stakeholder Theory. The Natural Resource-Based View (NRBV) provides an important theoretical foundation for explaining how digital transformation can enhance corporate ESG performance through improved environmental management capabilities and broader sustainability governance. Hart (1995) argues that the source of competitive advantage is gradually shifting to those organizational capabilities that enable environmentally sustainable economic activities. Based on that premise, Hart and Dowell (2011) identify three strategic capabilities: pollution prevention, product stewardship, and sustainable development. Each of these strategies calls for different organizational capabilities, which digital technologies have the power to augment. Prior research generally conceptualizes digital transformation as a multidimensional process involving technology adoption, process reconfiguration, and strategic renewal. Digital transformation, viewed through the lenses of resource-based view and dynamic capability theory, involves more than just the use of digital technologies; it is a strategic endeavor by which companies redefine their resource bases and develop new dynamic capabilities for dealing with environmental changes. At the core of this process is the use of data as a strategic production factor to reshape enterprise operations, decision-making, and business models through technologies such as artificial intelligence, big data analytics, and cloud computing (Vial, 2021). Prior research suggests that digital transformation provides an important capability base for green innovation by optimizing resource allocation, improving information-processing efficiency, and enhancing organizational agility (Kraus et al., 2021). Huang and Lau (2024) argue that digital transformation leads to green innovation quality improvement in Chinese enterprises. The effect is made stronger by executives who have digital knowledge and experience. Wang et al. (2024) found that digital transformation improves ESG performance indirectly by increasing total factor productivity. In other words, their findings disclose a path through which digitalization increases overall operational efficiency and thereby freeing resources for sustainability-related investment (Plekhanov et al., 2023). From the perspective of the Natural Resource-Based View, the ESG value of digital transformation lies in its ability to strengthen firms’ environmentally relevant capabilities. The NRBV argues that competitive advantage can be created through pollution prevention, product stewardship, and sustainable development capabilities. Digital transformation can reinforce these capabilities by enabling real-time environmental monitoring, data-driven resource allocation, energy-efficiency optimization, green supply-chain coordination, and sustainability-oriented process redesign. Therefore, digital transformation should not be treated merely as the adoption of digital technologies, but as a dynamic green digital capability that helps firms reconfigure organizational routines toward sustainability objectives. Stakeholder Theory further explains why firms deploy digital capabilities for ESG improvement. Firms face increasing demands from governments, investors, customers, employees, suppliers, communities, and media for environmental accountability, social responsibility, governance transparency, and credible disclosure. Digital technologies improve the collection, processing, verification, and communication of ESG-related information, thereby enhancing firms’ ability to respond to stakeholder expectations and maintain organizational legitimacy. In this sense, digital transformation strengthens not only environmental management but also social and governance practices through improved transparency, internal control, risk monitoring, and stakeholder communication. Combining the capability logic of the NRBV and the legitimacy logic of Stakeholder Theory, digital transformation can improve comprehensive ESG performance through both internal capability-building and external legitimacy-enhancing channels. Firms with stronger dynamic green digital capabilities are more likely to identify ESG risks, allocate resources to sustainability projects, improve disclosure quality, and respond to stakeholder concerns in a timely manner. Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the following hypothesis: H1Digital transformation, conceptualized as a dynamic green digital capability, is positively associated with corporate ESG performance. 2.4 Green technological innovation as an innovation-based mediation mechanism Corresponding to RQ2 and RQ3, this subsection explains how digital transformation affects green technological innovation, how green technological innovation contributes to ESG performance, and why green technological innovation serves as a mediating mechanism. The NRBV suggests that firms need to develop specific environmental capabilities to achieve sustainable competitive advantage. Digital transformation provides an important capability base for such environmental capability development. By integrating artificial intelligence, big data analytics, cloud computing, industrial Internet platforms, and Internet of Things technologies into production and R&D processes, firms can identify environmental inefficiencies, monitor energy consumption, analyze emission patterns, search for cleaner technologies, coordinate green R&D resources, and redesign production routines. These functions correspond to the NRBV tenets of pollution prevention and sustainable development. The Porter Hypothesis provides a complementary innovation-based explanation. It suggests that environmental pressure can stimulate innovation when firms possess the capabilities needed to respond efficiently. Digital transformation reduces information-processing costs, technological-search costs, and coordination costs, thereby increasing firms’ ability to transform environmental and stakeholder pressures into green technological innovation. In addition, digital transformation improves the allocation of innovation resources by helping firms evaluate green R&D projects, identify commercially viable low-carbon technologies, and coordinate cross-functional innovation teams. Therefore, digital transformation is expected to promote green technological innovation. Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the following hypothesis: H2aDigital transformation positively affects green technological innovation. Green technological innovation contributes to ESG performance through the combined mechanisms proposed by the NRBV, Stakeholder Theory, and the Porter Hypothesis. From the NRBV perspective, green technological innovation enhances pollution prevention, cleaner production, energy conservation, resource recycling, and sustainable product development capabilities. These improvements directly strengthen the environmental dimension of ESG performance by reducing resource consumption, emissions, and environmental risks (Clément et al., 2022). From the perspective of Stakeholder Theory, green technological innovation also helps firms respond to the expectations of regulators, investors, customers, communities, and other stakeholders. Firms engaging in green innovation are more likely to improve environmental disclosure, demonstrate regulatory compliance, strengthen internal governance procedures, enhance sustainability legitimacy, and build trust with external stakeholders. These actions may be reflected not only in environmental scores but also in social responsibility, governance transparency, and disclosure-related aspects of ESG ratings. The Porter Hypothesis further suggests that green technological innovation can generate innovation compensation effects by improving resource efficiency, reducing environmental compliance costs, and creating differentiation advantages. Therefore, green technological innovation is expected to improve comprehensive ESG performance. Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the following hypothesis: H2bGreen technological innovation positively affects corporate ESG performance. Integrating the three theories, green technological innovation can be viewed as an innovation-based pathway through which dynamic green digital capabilities are converted into ESG outcomes. Digital transformation provides the capability foundation emphasized by the NRBV; stakeholder pressure directs these capabilities toward environmental and ESG-related objectives; and the Porter Hypothesis explains how these capabilities and pressures are transformed into green innovation and performance improvement. However, because ESG performance is broader than environmental innovation and also includes social responsibility, governance quality, internal control, disclosure transparency, and stakeholder responsiveness, green technological innovation is expected to serve as an important, but not necessarily exclusive, mediation mechanism through which digital transformation influences ESG performance. Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the following hypothesis: H2cGreen technological innovation mediates the relationship between digital transformation and corporate ESG performance. 2.5 Market concentration as a moderation mechanism: the extended Porter Hypothesis Corresponding to RQ4, this subsection examines market concentration as a boundary condition that may strengthen or weaken the ESG-enhancing effect of digital transformation. The Porter Hypothesis provides a useful theoretical basis for understanding how market structure may shape the environmental consequences of digital transformation. Porter and Linde (1995) argue that properly designed environmental pressures may stimulate innovation and generate innovation compensation effects. Zhang et al. (2024) find that in the context of digital transformation, market structure may influence whether firms allocate digital efficiency gains to short-term price competition or to long-term environmental investment. Market concentration may have two competing effects on the relationship between digital transformation and ESG performance. On the one hand, higher market concentration may reduce competitive pressure and generate a monopoly complacency effect. Firms with strong market power may face weaker external pressure to innovate, improve efficiency, or upgrade environmental practices. From this perspective, higher HHI may weaken firms’ incentives to transform digital capabilities into green innovation and environmental improvement (Giglio et al., 2025). On the other hand, higher market concentration may create a resource slack and appropriability effect. Firms in more concentrated markets usually have stronger market power, more stable cash flows, greater strategic discretion, and a stronger ability to appropriate returns from long-term investments. These conditions are particularly important in the context of digital transformation because digital technologies often require complementary investments in data infrastructure, organizational restructuring, environmental management systems, and green R&D. Firms operating in highly competitive markets may use digital technologies mainly to reduce costs, improve operational efficiency, and defend market share. By contrast, firms in more concentrated markets may be better able to allocate digital efficiency gains toward environmental governance, green technological innovation, and climate-related investments (Sun et al., 2024). In the context of Chinese A-share listed firms, this study expects the resource slack and appropriability effect to dominate the monopoly complacency effect. This expectation can be understood by extending the Porter Hypothesis from a regulation–innovation relationship to a market-structure–capability relationship. The Porter Hypothesis emphasizes that innovation compensation effects depend on whether firms have sufficient incentives and capabilities to transform environmental pressure into innovation and performance improvement. Market concentration shapes this transformation process by affecting firms’ resource slack, strategic discretion, competitive pressure, and ability to appropriate returns from long-term investments (Wang J. et al., 2023). From the NRBV perspective, transforming digital capabilities into ESG performance requires complementary investments in environmental monitoring systems, green R&D, cleaner production processes, supply-chain traceability, and sustainability governance routines. These investments are costly, uncertain, and often associated with delayed returns. Firms in more concentrated industries may have more stable cash flows, stronger resource endowments, and greater bargaining power, which enable them to sustain long-term digital and green investments and appropriate the resulting benefits. Stakeholder Theory further suggests that firms in concentrated industries may face greater visibility and stronger legitimacy pressure. Dominant firms are more visible to regulators, investors, customers, media, and local communities. As a result, they may have stronger incentives to use digital technologies to improve ESG disclosure, environmental governance, and stakeholder responsiveness. Therefore, although higher market concentration may weaken innovation incentives through monopoly complacency, this study expects the resource slack, appropriability, and visibility effects to dominate. Accordingly, market concentration is expected to amplify the ESG benefits of digital transformation. Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the following hypothesis: H3Market concentration positively moderates the relationship between digital transformation and corporate ESG performance, such that the positive effect of digital transformation on ESG performance is stronger in more concentrated markets. The framework is based on the Natural Resource-Based View (Hart, 1995), Stakeholder Theory, and the Porter Hypothesis (Porter and Linde, 1995). 3 Research design 3.1 Sample selection and data sources This study uses panel data for Chinese A-share listed firms from 2009 to 2023. Financial data and corporate governance variables are obtained from the China Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database, while ESG performance is measured using Huazheng ESG ratings. The 2009–2023 sample period covers several important policy and economic events, including the Paris Agreement in 2015, the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, the launch of China’s national carbon emissions trading market in 2021, and the implementation of the 14th Five-Year Plan for the Digital Economy in 2022. This broad sample period allows the analysis to capture substantial variation in firms’ digitalization and ESG practices over time. We exclude observations with missing key variables, firms in the financial sector, and observations with evident data anomalies. The final sample consists of 32,609 firm-year observations. The representativeness of the sample is supported in three respects. First, the sample covers all non-financial A-share listed firms with available data during 2009–2023, rather than focusing on a single industry, region, or ownership type. Second, the sample includes firms from major sectors of China’s real economy, including manufacturing, mining, utilities, construction, transportation, information technology, real estate, wholesale and retail trade, and other services. Third, the industry distribution reported in Appendix Table A1 shows that the sample reflects the sectoral structure of China’s non-financial listed firms, with manufacturing firms accounting for the largest share while other industries remain meaningfully represented. Therefore, the sample is broadly representative of Chinese non-financial A-share listed firms, although the findings should not be generalized directly to unlisted firms or financial institutions. To reduce the influence of extreme values, all continuous variables are winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Industry fixed effects are not separately included in the baseline models because firm fixed effects absorb all time-invariant firm characteristics, including each firm’s industry affiliation. We do not include industry-year fixed effects because doing so would absorb the industry-year-level HHI variable, which is central to the moderation analysis. In addition, year fixed effects control for common macroeconomic and policy shocks. To examine the role of time-varying industry-level market structure, we calculate the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index at the industry-year level and introduce it in the moderation model. The industry composition of the sample is reported in Appendix Table A1. 3.2 Variable Selection 3.2.1 Dependent variable The dependent variable in this study is corporate ESG performance (ESG), measured by Huazheng ESG ratings. Huazheng ESG ratings provide a comprehensive evaluation of listed firms’ environmental, social, and governance performance. Compared with single-dimensional environmental indicators, comprehensive ESG ratings capture firms’ broader sustainability performance, including environmental responsibility, social accountability, governance quality, information disclosure, and stakeholder responsiveness. Following prior studies, this study converts the Huazheng ESG rating categories into a numerical scale from one to 9, with higher values indicating better ESG performance. In the sample, the observed values range from 1 to 8, indicating that no firm received the highest rating during the sample period. Huazheng ESG ratings are widely used in studies of Chinese listed firms because they provide broad coverage of A-share companies and are updated consistently over time. Nevertheless, ESG ratings may vary across providers due to differences in evaluation criteria and weighting schemes. Therefore, the use of a single ESG rating provider is acknowledged as a limitation. 3.2.2 Independent variable The independent variable is corporate digital transformation (Dig). Conceptually, this study treats digital transformation as a dynamic green digital capability rather than the simple adoption of isolated digital technologies. This capability reflects firms’ ability to use digital technologies and data resources to sense environmental and stakeholder demands, process sustainability-related information, coordinate internal and external resources, and reconfigure organizational routines for ESG improvement. Empirically, following Wu et al. (2021),digital transformation is proxied by the frequency of digital-related keywords in firms’ annual reports. This text-based measure captures the extent to which firms disclose and emphasize digital transformation activities. Although it may not fully measure the depth and quality of actual digital implementation, it provides a consistent firm-year proxy for firms’ digital transformation orientation and digital capability development. 3.2.3 Mediating variable The mediating variable is green technological innovation (Greenco). Following Tao et al. (2021) findings. it is measured by the number of green patent applications. Green patent applications capture firms’ innovation output in green products, clean production processes, pollution control, energy conservation, and resource recycling. Following prior research, patent applications are used rather than granted patents because applications can reflect firms’ green innovation activities more timely and are less affected by approval lags. It should be noted that green technological innovation mainly captures the environmental innovation channel. Because ESG performance is a comprehensive construct that includes environmental, social, governance, and disclosure-related dimensions, green technological innovation is expected to be one mediating mechanism rather than the only mechanism linking digital transformation to ESG performance. 3.2.4 Moderating variable Market concentration (HHI) serves as the moderating variable. It is measured using the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index. For each industry-year, HHI is calculated as the sum of squared market shares of all firms in the industry. A higher HHI indicates a more concentrated market structure. This study uses HHI to examine whether market concentration conditions the effect of digital transformation on ESG performance. 3.2.5 Control variables Drawing on existing research literature, this study employs the following variables as controls: Fixed Assets Ratio (FIXED), Cash Assets Ratio (Cashflow), Revenue Growth Rate (Growth), Debt-to-Asset Ratio (Lev), Book-to-Market Ratio (BM), Independent Director Ratio (Indep), Return on Assets (ROA), Largest Shareholder Ownership (Top1), and Management Shareholding (Mshare). All models include firm fixed effects to control for time-invariant firm characteristics and year fixed effects to control for common temporal shocks affecting all firms. 3.3 Model construction Based on the research content, the empirical analysis models are constructed as follows: Model One: Baseline Regression Modelwhere i and t denote company i in year t; represents the intercept; indicates the coefficient for the main independent variable, digital transformation; represents coefficients for control variables; signifies the individual (firm) fixed effect controlling for time-invariant firm characteristics; denotes the time (year) fixed effect controlling for common temporal shocks; and represents the random error term. This result is consistent with the multidimensional nature of ESG performance. The discussion follows the logic of Research Questions → Hypotheses → Findings, and then compares the results with prior studies in both confirmatory and disconfirmatory senses. First, regarding RQ1 and H1, the results show that digital transformation significantly improves corporate ESG performance. This finding supports the argument that digital transformation should be understood as a dynamic green digital capability rather than merely the adoption of digital technologies. Firms with stronger digital capabilities are better able to collect sustainability-related data, monitor environmental risks, improve internal control, optimize resource allocation, enhance disclosure transparency, and respond to stakeholder expectations. This result confirms prior studies that report a positive relationship between digital transformation and ESG performance. For example, Lu et al. (2024) show that digital transformation improves ESG performance among Chinese firms, while Deng et al. (2024) provide evidence from Chinese manufacturing firms. However, this study extends these findings by emphasizing the capability-based nature of digital transformation and by using a broader sample of Chinese non-financial A-share listed firms across multiple industries, regions, and ownership types. Second, regarding RQ2, RQ3, and H2a–H2c, the results show that digital transformation promotes green technological innovation, and green technological innovation improves ESG performance. The bootstrap mediation test further confirms that green technological innovation partially mediates the relationship between digital transformation and ESG performance. Because the direct effect of digital transformation remains significant after green technological innovation is included, the mediation should be interpreted as partial rather than full mediation. This finding confirms the innovation-based logic of the Porter Hypothesis and supports prior studies suggesting that digital transformation can facilitate green innovation. Digital capabilities reduce information-processing costs, improve technological search, strengthen R&D coordination, and support cleaner production solutions. However, the mediation effect is economically modest, accounting for only about 3.33% of the total effect. This finding qualifies prior studies that may implicitly treat green innovation as the dominant channel linking digitalization and ESG performance. The modest mediation effect is theoretically meaningful. Green patent applications mainly capture environmental innovation outputs, whereas comprehensive ESG ratings also include social responsibility, governance quality, information disclosure, internal control, and stakeholder communication. Therefore, digital transformation may improve ESG performance through multiple non-patent channels, including environmental data monitoring, automated ESG disclosure, digital supply-chain management, smart energy systems, governance transparency, and stakeholder communication platforms. Third, regarding RQ4 and H3, the results show that market concentration positively moderates the relationship between digital transformation and ESG performance. The positive interaction between Dig and HHI indicates that firms in more concentrated industries are better able to convert digital capabilities into ESG improvements. This finding contributes to the debate on market structure and sustainability. The monopoly complacency view suggests that concentrated markets may reduce innovation incentives because dominant firms face weaker competitive pressure. However, the results of this study support the resource slack and appropriability view. Firms in more concentrated industries may have more stable cash flows, stronger bargaining power, greater strategic discretion, and stronger ability to appropriate returns from long-term ESG investments. These conditions allow them to invest in digital infrastructure, environmental management systems, green R&D, and ESG disclosure mechanisms. Fourth, regarding RQ5, the heterogeneity analyses show that the positive effect of digital transformation is observed across Eastern, Central, and Western regions and across both state-owned and non-state-owned enterprises. These findings suggest that the ESG-enhancing effect of digital transformation is not confined to a single region or ownership type. The pollution-industry analysis provides a more nuanced result. The effect of digital transformation is significant among non-high-polluting firms but insignificant among high-polluting firms in the subsample regressions. However, the interaction test shows that the difference between high-polluting and non-high-polluting firms is not statistically significant. This suggests that digital transformation may be beneficial across pollution groups, but high-polluting firms may require additional environmental investments, cleaner production technologies, emission-reduction equipment, and regulatory compliance efforts before digital capabilities can be fully converted into ESG improvements. Overall, these findings contribute to the emerging literature on twin green and digital transformation. The results show that digitalization can support green and ESG-oriented transformation, but the “digitalising of the green” should not be understood narrowly as digital-enabled green patenting. Instead, twin transformation operates through a broader portfolio of mechanisms, including green technological innovation, environmental monitoring, smart energy management, digital supply-chain traceability, ESG data systems, internal control improvement, and stakeholder communication. Therefore, the ESG value of digital transformation depends on firms’ ability to develop dynamic green digital capabilities and embed them into organizational routines and sustainability governance. 6 Implications 6.1 Theoretical implications Theoretically, this study contributes to the twin transformation literature by showing how digital transformation and green transformation are connected through dynamic green digital capabilities. The findings suggest that digital technologies create ESG value not simply because they are adopted, but because they enable firms to process sustainability-related data, coordinate resources, promote green technological innovation, and improve ESG governance. This study makes three main theoretical contributions. First, it extends the literature on digital transformation and corporate sustainability by showing that digital transformation can improve comprehensive ESG performance. Digital transformation is not only a technological upgrading process but also an organizational capability that improves information processing, resource allocation, internal control, stakeholder communication, and sustainability governance. These capabilities help firms respond more effectively to ESG-related expectations from regulators, investors, customers, employees, and the public. Second, this study enriches the Natural Resource-Based View by showing that digital transformation can strengthen firms’ green innovation capabilities and thereby contribute to ESG improvement. Although the NRBV mainly emphasizes environmental capabilities such as pollution prevention, product stewardship, and sustainable development, the findings suggest that these environmental capabilities can also contribute to broader ESG performance. Green technological innovation improves not only environmental outcomes but also firms’ sustainability legitimacy, disclosure quality, and stakeholder recognition, which may be reflected in comprehensive ESG ratings. Third, this study extends the Porter Hypothesis by introducing market concentration as a boundary condition. The results show that market concentration strengthens the positive relationship between digital transformation and ESG performance. This finding suggests that the innovation compensation effect depends not only on environmental pressure and technological capability but also on market-structure conditions. Although higher market concentration may reduce competitive pressure and create a monopoly complacency effect, the empirical results suggest that the resource slack and appropriability effect dominates in the context of Chinese listed firms. Firms in more concentrated industries appear to have greater capacity to convert digital efficiency gains into long-term ESG investments and sustainability governance improvements. 6.2 Managerial implications The findings provide several practical implications for corporate managers. From a dynamic green digital capability perspective, managers should not treat digital transformation as a one-time technology investment. Instead, they should build three interrelated capabilities: sensing capabilities to identify ESG risks and stakeholder expectations, seizing capabilities to allocate resources to green innovation and ESG projects, and reconfiguring capabilities to redesign processes, governance routines, and disclosure systems around sustainability objectives. First, managers should build ESG-oriented digital infrastructure. Firms can establish integrated ESG data-management platforms that collect and connect data from production, procurement, energy consumption, emissions, human resources, compliance, finance, and supply-chain systems. Such platforms can improve the accuracy, timeliness, and comparability of ESG information and support data-driven sustainability governance. Second, firms should use digital tools to strengthen environmental monitoring and green technological innovation. Internet of Things sensors, smart meters, real-time emission-monitoring systems, and energy-management systems can help firms track electricity use, fuel consumption, wastewater discharge, waste generation, and carbon emissions. Artificial intelligence and big data analytics can be used for predictive maintenance, production-process optimization, energy-efficiency improvement, and identification of green R&D opportunities. Third, managers should convert digital capabilities into higher-quality green innovation rather than only more green patents. Firms can build digital green R&D platforms that integrate patent databases, lifecycle assessment tools, carbon-footprint analysis, technology-search systems, and cross-departmental collaboration platforms. These tools can help firms evaluate the environmental and commercial value of green innovation projects and avoid symbolic or low-quality patenting. Fourth, firms should embed digital transformation into ESG governance and disclosure. Automated ESG reporting systems, internal-control dashboards, digital audit trails, and supply-chain traceability systems can improve disclosure reliability and governance transparency. These mechanisms are especially important because ESG performance includes not only environmental outcomes but also social responsibility, governance quality, information disclosure, and stakeholder responsiveness. Fifth, managers should adjust digital and ESG strategies according to market-structure conditions. Firms in more concentrated industries should use their resource slack and stable cash flows to invest in long-term digital infrastructure, green innovation, and ESG governance systems. Firms in more competitive industries should prioritize digital applications that simultaneously reduce costs and improve ESG performance, such as smart logistics, energy-saving production systems, automated compliance reporting, and shared digital platforms. 6.3 Policy implications The findings also provide several implications for policymakers. From a policy perspective, the key is to create institutional conditions under which firms can transform digital resources into green innovation and ESG governance capabilities, rather than merely increasing the adoption rate of digital technologies. First, policymakers should promote the coordinated development of digital infrastructure and ESG governance systems. Public investment in industrial Internet platforms, smart manufacturing infrastructure, environmental data platforms, and carbon-accounting systems can reduce the cost for firms to collect, process, and disclose sustainability-related information. Second, governments should encourage firms to use digital technologies for substantive ESG improvement rather than symbolic digital disclosure. Policy instruments such as tax incentives, green credit, innovation subsidies, government procurement, and digital transformation vouchers can be directed toward projects involving energy conservation, emission reduction, clean production, circular economy practices, green supply-chain management, and ESG disclosure improvement. Third, policymakers should support green technological innovation enabled by digital capabilities. Public agencies can establish green technology databases, digital patent information platforms, lifecycle assessment tools, carbon-footprint calculation systems, and industry-level technology-sharing platforms. These tools can help firms identify green innovation opportunities and improve the quality, commercialization, and environmental effectiveness of green patents. Fourth, regulators should improve digital ESG disclosure standards. Standardized ESG reporting templates, digital reporting interfaces, third-party assurance mechanisms, and machine-readable disclosure systems can improve the comparability, transparency, and credibility of ESG information. These measures can also reduce information asymmetry between firms, investors, regulators, and other stakeholders. Fifth, policy design should consider market-structure differences. In highly concentrated industries, regulators should prevent monopoly complacency while encouraging leading firms to assume greater ESG responsibility and invest in green innovation. In highly competitive industries, governments can provide shared digital infrastructure, public ESG data services, green finance support, and technical assistance to help firms overcome resource constraints and convert digital capabilities into ESG improvements. Finally, regional policy support should be differentiated. Western and less digitally developed regions may require stronger public investment in broadband infrastructure, industrial Internet platforms, digital talent training, and green technology services. Central regions undergoing industrial upgrading may benefit from targeted support for smart manufacturing, digital energy management, and green R&D collaboration. Eastern regions can focus more on improving ESG disclosure quality, supply-chain traceability, and advanced green innovation. 7 Conclusion This study examines the impact of digital transformation on corporate ESG performance using panel data from Chinese A-share listed firms from 2009 to 2023. Drawing on the Natural Resource-Based View, Stakeholder Theory, and the Porter Hypothesis, this study develops an integrated framework in which digital transformation improves ESG performance by enhancing firms’ data-driven sustainability management capabilities and promoting green technological innovation, while market concentration conditions the strength of this relationship. The study answers the proposed research questions as follows. For RQ1, the results show that digital transformation significantly improves comprehensive ESG performance. For RQ2, digital transformation is found to promote green technological innovation, and green technological innovation is positively associated with ESG performance. For RQ3, green technological innovation is found to mediate the relationship between digital transformation and ESG performance. Further analysis shows that this mediation is partial and economically modest. For RQ4, market concentration positively moderates the digital transformation–ESG relationship. For RQ5, the positive effect of digital transformation is observed across regions and ownership types, while the difference between high-polluting and non-high-polluting firms is not statistically significant in the interaction test. The empirical results show that digital transformation is significantly and positively associated with corporate ESG performance. This finding remains robust after controlling for firm fixed effects, year fixed effects, potential endogeneity using an instrumental variable approach, the lagged independent variable method, and several robustness tests. The mediation analysis shows that green technological innovation plays a statistically significant but economically modest partial mediating role rather than a full mediating role. This suggests that digital transformation improves ESG performance partly through green innovation, while other channels such as information disclosure, internal control, stakeholder communication, supply-chain coordination, governance transparency, and resource allocation efficiency may also be important. The moderation analysis shows that market concentration strengthens the positive effect of digital transformation on ESG performance, indicating that firms in more concentrated industries are better able to convert digital capabilities into ESG improvements. Additional heterogeneity analyses show that the positive effect of digital transformation is observed across Eastern, Central, and Western regions and across both state-owned and non-state-owned enterprises. The effect is more evident among non-high-polluting firms, although the interaction test indicates that the between-group difference between high-polluting and non-high-polluting firms is not statistically significant. This study has several limitations. First, digital transformation is measured using the frequency of digital-related keywords in annual reports. Although this approach is widely used in the literature, it may capture firms’ digital attention or disclosure rather than the depth and quality of actual digital implementation. Future research could use alternative measures such as IT investment, digital patents, digital talent, or survey-based indicators. Second, green technological innovation is measured using green patent applications. Patent counts may not fully capture the quality, commercialization, or environmental effectiveness of green innovation. Future studies could distinguish between invention patents and utility model patents, examine patent citations, or use data on green technology adoption and emissions reduction outcomes. Third, ESG performance is measured using Huazheng ESG ratings. Future research could use alternative ESG rating providers, sub-dimensional ESG ratings, firm-level emissions data, pollution discharge data, or a composite ESG performance index to improve measurement robustness. Fourth, although this study uses fixed effects, an instrumental variable approach, and lagged independent variables to mitigate endogeneity concerns, causal identification can be further strengthened. Future studies may exploit quasi-natural experiments, policy shocks, or staggered digital infrastructure programs to identify causal effects more precisely. Finally, this study focuses on Chinese A-share listed firms. Future research could examine whether the findings apply to other institutional contexts and compare how regulatory systems, capital markets, and digital infrastructure shape the digitalization–ESG performance relationship.
