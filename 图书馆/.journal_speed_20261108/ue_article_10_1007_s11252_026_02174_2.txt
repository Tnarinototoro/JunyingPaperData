This study aimed to quantify aboveground biomass, carbon stocks, and their illustrative economic valuation in an urban Tiny Forest located in southeastern Brazil. A complete tree inventory was conducted across a 2.76-ha area, including all living trees with a diameter at breast height (DBH) ≥ 5 cm. Aboveground biomass was estimated using an allometric equation developed for urban trees, carbon stocks were calculated using a conversion factor of 0.47, and an illustrative economic valuation was derived from a reference price in the voluntary carbon market. The inventory recorded 539 trees belonging to 68 species and 24 botanical families, with native species accounting for 79.0% of the individuals. Total aboveground biomass was estimated at 95.09 Mg (34.45 Mg ha⁻¹), corresponding to 44.69 Mg of carbon (16.19 Mg C ha⁻¹) and 163.88 Mg CO₂e. The illustrative economic valuation of the stored carbon was US$3,349.64. Native species accounted for 86.46% of the estimated carbon stock, with Moquilea tomentosa and Handroanthus impetiginosus among the main contributors. Carbon storage varied with species composition and tree structural characteristics, indicating that abundance alone does not determine species contributions to carbon stocks. These findings demonstrate that small urban forest remnants can represent measurable carbon reservoirs and provide local-scale information to support urban green infrastructure planning. However, the estimates represent carbon stocks at a single point in time and do not establish carbon sequestration rates or potential revenue from certified carbon credits. Biophysical Economics Environmental Economics Forest Ecology Urban Ecology Ecosystem Services Urban Economics Introduction The rapid growth of cities drives urban expansion and favors the formation of densely populated and highly polluted urban centers (Rauf et al. 2024 ). In this context, global climate change represents one of the major contemporary challenges, as it intensifies the effects of urbanization, including the increasead urban temperatures and impacts on environmental quality and human well-being (Silva et al. 2026 ). These challenges require the evaluation of effective strategies capable of reducing environmental impacts and promoting urban resilience (Bhat and Lone 2023 ). Urbanization, together with human activities such as industrialization and road traffic, contributes significantly to greenhouse gas emissions, intensifies climate change on a global scale (Clark et al. 2020 ). Among greenhouse gases, carbon dioxide (CO₂) represents one of the main contributors to the recent global warming trends (IPCC 2021 ). Therefore, climate mitigation strategies involve both reducing emission and increasing the capacity of terrestrial ecosystems to retain carbon through vegetation-based approaches (Ndlovu et al. 2024 ). Among these strategies, urban nature stands out, composed of street tree planting, green areas, and urban forests, represents an important nature-based solution for improving urban environmental conditions and supporting ecosystem functions (Privitera et al. 2024 ). Within this context, the concept of Tiny Forest has emerged as a specific approach to establishing small-scale forest ecosystems in urban areas (Bleichrodt et al. 2017 ). Unlike general urban forest remnants, Tiny Forests are intentionally established forest patches characterized by high planting density and a predominance of native species, aiming to accelerate ecological processes within limited spaces. These areas have been associated with potential benefits such as biodiversity enhancement, microclimate regulation and carbon storage, although the magnitude of these benefits depends on local conditions, species composition and management practices (Egerer and Suda 2023 ). The implementation and conservation of urban forests, including Tiny Forests, are aligned with global sustainability goals by promoting resilient and accessible green spaces in cities (Balduino Junior et al. 2025 ). In particular, Sustainable Development Goal 11 emphasizes the importance of inclusive, safe and sustainable urban environments, reinforcing the role of urban vegetation in improving environmental quality and human well-being (United Nations 2015 ). Therefore, quantifying the structural characteristics and carbon storage of these areas is essential to understand their contribution to urban green infrastructure. Considering the strategic role of green areas in climate change adaptation, quantifying plant biomass is essential for estimating carbon stocks stored in vegetation and understanding the contribution of urban forests to carbon storage (Yang et al. 2022 ). These estimates can support the assessment of ecosystem functions associated with vegetation and provide information for environmental planning, restoration initiatives and potential economic valuation approaches (Robertson et al. 2023 ). Reliable biomass estimates are essential for quantifying carbon stocks and understanding the structural contribution of urban vegetation to carbon storage (Borsah et al. 2023 ). Plant functional traits, including structural characteristics related to growth strategies and resource acquisition, can influence biomass accumulation and productivity patterns across environmental gradients (Chaturvedi et al. 2025 ). In addition, accurate biomass and carbon assessments provide relevant information for evaluating potential environmental values associated with vegetation-based initiatives (Fernández-Guisuraga et al. 2024 ). Therefore, the adoption of accessible and reproducible methods is necessary to characterize the carbon storage potential of urban green areas. Traditional forest inventories represent a practical approach for quantifying biomass and carbon stocks in small urban forests, generating information that supports ecological restoration, conservation planning and urban green infrastructure management. Although Tiny Forests have gained increasing attention as nature-based solutions in urban environments, quantitative assessments of their aboveground biomass and carbon stocks remain limited. Existing studies have mainly emphasized biodiversity, environmental education and urban ecological benefits, while information regarding biomass accumulation and carbon storage, particularly in small municipalities, is still scarce. This knowledge gap limits the understanding of the contribution of Tiny Forests to urban green infrastructure planning. Small municipalities often have limited space and resources for implementing large forest restoration projects. Therefore, evaluating small urban forests can provide relevant local-scale information about their capacity to accumulate biomass and store carbon, supporting decision-making regarding urban vegetation strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify aboveground biomass, carbon stock and an illustrative economic valuation associated with carbon storage in an urban Tiny Forest located in a small municipality in southeastern Brazil. We hypothesized that the evaluated urban Tiny Forest represents a measurable carbon reservoir, with carbon storage patterns influenced by species composition and tree structural characteristics. Materials and methods Study area The study was conducted in a 2.76 ha urban forest patch located in the central area of Jerônimo Monteiro, southern Espírito Santo State, Brazil (Fig. 1 ). According to the Köppen classification, the regional climate is tropical savanna (Aw), characterized by a dry winter season (Alvares et al. 2013 ). The municipality has a mean elevation of approximately 120 m and an average annual precipitation of 1,732.8 mm, concentrated mainly between October and April. Temperatures generally range from 19 °C to 26 °C. The regional terrain is predominantly undulating to gently undulating, with Latosols and Argisols among the main soil classes (Incaper, 2020 ). Fig. 1 Full size image Map of the study site where samples, counts, measurements, and data collections were carried out The forest patch is embedded within an urban landscape comprising residential buildings, roads, and adjacent vegetated areas. Tree cover is heterogeneously distributed across the site, with greater canopy continuity in the northern and central portions and a mixture of trees, buildings, access routes, and open spaces throughout the area. This spatial configuration forms a mosaic of vegetated and built surfaces within the urban matrix (Fig. 1 ). Detailed records of the site’s establishment and management history were unavailable. To characterize historical changes in vegetation cover, satellite imagery available through Google Earth was visually examined for the period from 2003 to 2024. Tree cover and buildings were already present in 2003, whereas the southern portion of the site contained relatively open areas. Subsequent images indicated changes in vegetation distribution, with greater canopy continuity observed in 2024, particularly in previously sparsely vegetated areas. Forest mensuration A complete tree census was conducted between September and November 2024 within the 2.76-ha study area. All living trees with a circumference at breast height (CBH) ≥ 15.71 cm, equivalent to a diameter at breast height (DBH) ≥ 5 cm, measured at 1.30 m above ground level, were included. This threshold was adopted to characterize established trees and estimate their aboveground biomass and carbon stocks. Individuals below the inclusion threshold were not measured; therefore, their contribution to total aboveground biomass and carbon stocks was not accounted for. All inventoried individuals were identified to species level by a botanist. Species that could not be identified directly in the field were sampled for subsequent identification at the Herbário Capixaba (CAP), affiliated with the Universidade Federal do Espírito Santo, Brazil. Taxonomic classification followed the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group system (APG IV, 2016 ), and species were classified as native or exotic to Brazil according to the Flora e Funga do Brasil database ( 2026 ). Field data collection was supported by a georeferenced map accessed through the Avenza Maps application. The measured variables included CBH and total tree height (H). Tree height was measured directly using a graduated ruler positioned alongside each tree. Circumference measurements were converted to DBH assuming a circular stem cross-section, and basal area (BA) was calculated accordingly. For multi-stemmed trees with stems originating below 1.30 m, the equivalent diameter (Deq) was calculated to represent the combined cross-sectional area of the stems: $${D_{eq}}=\sqrt {\sum\nolimits_{{i=1}}^{n} {(DBH} } {)^2}$$ (1) Where: Deq is represents the equivalent diameter (cm); DBH is the diameter (cm) of the i-th stem and (n) is the total number of stems. Although direct height measurements were performed using a graduated ruler, uncertainties may arise from difficulties in identifying the highest point of the crown or positioning the pole vertically, particularly for taller trees. Such uncertainties may propagate into aboveground biomass estimates. Estimation of aboveground biomass Aboveground biomass (AGB) was estimated using diameter at breast height (DBH), total tree height (H), and wood density (ρ). Wood density values were obtained using the getWoodDensity function from the BIOMASS package (Réjou-Méchain et al. 2017 ) in the R environment (R Core Team 2026 ), which uses the Global Wood Density Database (Chave et al. 2009 ; Zanne et al. 2009 ) as its reference. Whenever available, species-level wood density was used. For taxa without species-specific information, wood density was assigned hierarchically using genus-level values and, subsequently, family-level values. Individual-tree AGB was estimated using the allometric model proposed by Velasco and Chen ( 2019 ) for tropical urban trees (Eq. 2 ): $$\:AGB=0.1054{\left(H\:x\:\rho\:\:x\:{DBH}^{2}\right)}^{0.9417}$$ (2) Where AGB is aboveground biomass (kg of oven-dry matter tree⁻¹), H is total tree height (m), ρ is wood density (g cm⁻³), and DBH is diameter at breast height (cm), following the variables and units specified in the original model. The model proposed by Velasco and Chen ( 2019 ) was selected because it was developed for tropical urban trees and incorporates DBH, total tree height, and wood density as biomass predictors. Its application was considered appropriate for the vegetation assessed in this study because trees growing in urban environments may exhibit architecture and growth conditions that differ from those found in forest environments. Estimation and valuation of carbon stock Carbon stock was estimated from the aboveground biomass (AGB) calculated for each individual tree. A carbon fraction of 0.47 was applied to dry aboveground biomass, following the default value recommended by the IPCC ( 2006 ) for tropical and subtropical aboveground forest biomass. The IPCC reports a range of 0.44–0.49 for this parameter, accounting for variation in carbon concentration. Carbon stock was calculated according to Eq. 3 : $$\:C=AGB\:x\:0.47$$ (3) Where C is the aboveground carbon stock (kg C) and AGB is the aboveground biomass (kg). To express the carbon stock as carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e), carbon mass was converted using the molecular weight ratio of CO₂ to C (44/12), according to Eq. 4 : $$\:{CO}_{2}e=C\:x\:\frac{44}{12}$$ (4) Where CO 2 e is the carbon dioxide equivalent (kg CO 2 e) and C is the aboveground carbon stock (kg C). For the economic valuation, CO 2 e values were converted from kilograms to metric tons and multiplied by the carbon price adopted in this study. A price of US$20.44 tCO 2 e⁻¹ was used, corresponding to the average 2024 price reported for Afforestation, Reforestation and Revegetation (ARR) projects in the voluntary carbon market by Ecosystem Marketplace (2025). ARR was adopted as the reference category because it is directly related to the establishment and maintenance of woody vegetation. The economic valuation was calculated according to Eq. 5 : $$\:CV=\:{CO}_{2}e\:x\:CP$$ (5) Where CV is the estimated carbon value (US$), CO 2 e is the carbon dioxide equivalent stock (tCO 2 e), and CP is the carbon price (US$ tCO 2 e⁻¹). The resulting monetary estimate represents an illustrative gross valuation of the standing carbon stock and should not be interpreted as the market value of certified or tradable carbon credits. The present study quantified carbon stored at the time of the inventory rather than a carbon sequestration rate. The generation of carbon credits requires additional procedures and criteria, including the establishment of a baseline, demonstration of additionality, assessment of permanence and leakage, monitoring, reporting and verification, as well as consideration of project and transaction costs. The methodological steps developed in this study are summarized in a flowchart (Fig. 2 ), which presents the procedures carried out for data collection, processing, and analysis, facilitating the understanding of the methodological sequence adopted. Species abundance was unevenly distributed, with the ten most abundant species accounting for 57.88% of all individuals (Fig. 3 A). Triplaris americana L. and Psidium guajava L. were the most abundant species, with 54 and 52 individuals, respectively, followed by Moquilea tomentosa Benth. (48 individuals) and Handroanthus impetiginosus (Mart. ex DC.) Mattos (43 individuals). At the family level, Bignoniaceae and Fabaceae had the highest numbers of individuals, whereas Fabaceae showed the greatest species richness (Fig. 3 B). Tree density was 195 individuals ha⁻¹, with a basal area of 5.92 m² ha⁻¹. Based on the carbon price adopted in this study, the standing carbon stock corresponded to an illustrative gross economic value of US$3,349.64. Native species accounted for 38.64 Mg C, representing 86.46% of the total aboveground carbon stock, a proportion higher than their relative abundance. The illustrative economic values associated with native and exotic species were US$2,896.19 and US$453.45, respectively. Aboveground biomass varied considerably among species, with Moquilea tomentosa presenting the highest estimated biomass (18,587.22 kg), followed by Cedrela odorata (8,613.70 kg), Handroanthus impetiginosus (6,966.49 kg), and Enterolobium contortisiliquum (5,298.41 kg). Together, these four species represented 22.45% of the inventoried individuals but accounted for 41.50% of the total estimated aboveground carbon stock. Conversely, Triplaris americana and Psidium guajava , the two most abundant species, collectively represented 19.67% of the individuals but contributed only 3.86% of the carbon stock. The relationship between mean DBH, carbon stock, and species abundance is presented in Fig. 4 . Moquilea tomentosa combined a relatively high mean DBH with a large number of individuals and exhibited the highest carbon stock among the inventoried species. Cedrela odorata exhibited a higher mean DBH but fewer individuals, whereas Handroanthus impetiginosus showed a substantial carbon stock associated with greater abundance and a lower mean DBH. Bubble size represents the number of individuals per species Discussion Carbon storage in the urban forest patch was unevenly distributed among tree species, with substantial differences in their contributions to the total stock. Species abundance did not necessarily correspond to carbon storage, as illustrated by the contrasting contributions of Handroanthus impetiginosus and Psidium guajava , which had similar numbers of individuals, and by the predominance of Moquilea tomentosa in the estimated carbon stock. The contribution of each species reflected the combination of its abundance and the biomass estimated for its constituent trees, reinforcing the importance of community composition and tree structure when assessing carbon stocks in urban vegetation (Murtala et al. 2019 ; Felsemburgh et al. 2024 ). Comparable differences have been reported in Brazilian urban environments. Broi Junior et al. ( 2025 ) associated the contribution of Handroanthus species to carbon stocks with the dimensions of the inventoried trees, while Felsemburgh et al. ( 2024 ) found that Mangifera indica contributed more to carbon storage than the more abundant Handroanthus serratifolius in street-tree vegetation in Santarém, Pará. Together, these observations indicate that species-level carbon stocks must be interpreted considering both individual tree biomass and population abundance. Tree dimensions and wood density are integral components of the allometric equation used in this study (Velasco and Chen 2019 ). Their association with estimated biomass therefore reflects, in part, the mathematical structure of the model and should not be interpreted as independent statistical evidence of their influence. Differences in species composition, tree architecture, and growing conditions may also affect the applicability of allometric relationships to urban vegetation (McHale et al. 2009 ). The model proposed by Velasco and Chen ( 2019 ), developed for tropical urban trees, provides a methodological basis more closely aligned with the characteristics of the inventoried vegetation than equations calibrated primarily for natural forests. Nevertheless, its application to a different urban tree community remains subject to uncertainty. At the community level, the estimated aboveground carbon stock of 16.19 Mg C ha⁻¹ characterizes the carbon stored in living trees within the 2.76-ha urban forest patch. Santos et al. ( 2013 ) reported a total stock of 68.5 Mg C in an urban park in Rio Branco, Acre, whereas Viana et al. ( 2023 ) estimated approximately 586.4 Mg C across six urban squares in Cáceres, Mato Grosso. Although both values exceed the total stock recorded in the present study, direct comparisons are constrained by differences in inventoried area, species composition, tree dimensions, sampling thresholds, and biomass estimation methods. Methodological choices, particularly the selection of allometric equations, can substantially affect urban biomass estimates, as differences in model applicability and tree structural characteristics may introduce uncertainty (Dapsopoulou and Zianis 2025 ). Carbon stocks expressed per unit area provide a more informative basis for comparison, provided that differences in inventory procedures and the spatial definition of the sampled area are considered. In the present study, carbon stock per hectare was calculated using the entire 2.76-ha site, including buildings, access paths, and open spaces. Consequently, the estimate is not directly equivalent to values calculated exclusively for tree-covered surfaces. The absence of documented information on stand age and establishment history further limits comparisons involving vegetation development and carbon accumulation over time. Carbon stocks in urban vegetation reflect the combined influence of species composition, tree abundance, structural characteristics, and local environmental conditions (Murtala et al. 2019 ; Viana et al. 2023 ). Comparisons among urban green spaces therefore require more than differences in total carbon stocks or tree density to establish meaningful distinctions in their carbon storage characteristics. Native species accounted for most inventoried individuals and the majority of the estimated aboveground carbon stock. Their contribution was associated with their representation in the community and the biomass of the sampled trees, rather than demonstrating an inherently greater carbon storage capacity than exotic species. Differences in abundance, tree dimensions, and species-specific characteristics prevent such a conclusion without additional analyses. Nevertheless, conserving established trees, including native species, can help maintain existing carbon stocks within urban landscapes. The potential ecological contribution of native vegetation also includes functions related to biodiversity and habitat provision, although these aspects were not directly evaluated in the present study. The carbon quantified in this study represents the amount stored in living aboveground biomass at the time of the inventory and should not be interpreted as a carbon sequestration rate. Estimating sequestration would require repeated forest inventories or growth-based assessments capable of quantifying changes in carbon stocks over time. Urban trees may also contribute to local temperature regulation through shading and evapotranspiration, as well as stormwater interception and runoff reduction (Dowtin et al. 2023 ; Yin et al. 2024 ). However, these functions were not measured, and their magnitude cannot be inferred from aboveground carbon stock alone. The present findings are therefore restricted to the characterization of carbon storage and its distribution within the inventoried tree community. The economic valuation of the estimated carbon stock provides a complementary perspective on the contribution of this urban forest patch. Based on the reference price of US$20.44 per tCO₂e for afforestation, reforestation, and revegetation projects (Ecosystem Marketplace 2025), the standing aboveground carbon stock corresponded to an illustrative gross value of US$3,349.64. This estimate should not be interpreted as potential revenue from carbon-credit transactions, since existing carbon stocks are not equivalent to verified carbon removals. The issuance of marketable credits requires compliance with specific standards, including baseline establishment, additionality, monitoring, verification, permanence, and leakage assessment (Verra 2025 ). The monetary estimate therefore provides a reference for the economic interpretation of stored carbon without establishing the feasibility of a certified carbon project. Several sources of uncertainty must be considered when interpreting the biomass and carbon estimates. Although the allometric model proposed by Velasco and Chen ( 2019 ) was developed for tropical urban trees, differences in tree architecture, species composition, and local growing conditions may affect its applicability to the study area (McHale et al. 2009 ). Tree height measurements obtained using a graduated ruler may be affected by instrument alignment and difficulties in identifying the highest point of the crown. Additional uncertainty arises from wood density values assigned through taxonomic reference databases, particularly when species-specific information is unavailable (Réjou-Méchain et al. 2017 ). The minimum inclusion threshold of DBH ≥ 5 cm excludes smaller trees from the estimates, while the fixed carbon fraction of 0.47 does not account for variation in carbon concentration among species and tissues (IPCC 2006 ). Advances in remote sensing offer opportunities to improve the characterization of urban tree structure and address some of these measurement limitations. UAV-based photogrammetry and LiDAR can provide detailed information on tree height and canopy structure, complementing conventional field measurements in areas with irregular crowns and heterogeneous vegetation (Zhou et al. 2022 ). Their effective application depends on appropriate acquisition and processing procedures and validation against field measurements. Combining these technologies with repeated forest inventories and locally evaluated allometric models could strengthen estimates of urban carbon stocks and support assessments of their temporal variation. Formal uncertainty propagation and confidence intervals were not calculated in the present study. Accordingly, the estimated biomass, carbon stock, and associated economic value should be interpreted as approximate quantities rather than exact measurements. Future investigations should quantify uncertainties related to field measurements, wood density, allometric model parameters, and carbon conversion factors (Réjou-Méchain et al. 2017 ). Repeated forest inventories would also allow changes in carbon storage associated with tree growth, mortality, and recruitment to be evaluated. More consistent monitoring and uncertainty assessment would strengthen the scientific basis for incorporating small urban forest patches into carbon management and urban environmental planning. Conclusions The evaluated urban Tiny Forest represents a measurable reservoir of aboveground biomass and carbon, supporting the study hypothesis and demonstrating the contribution of this vegetation to carbon storage at the local scale. Differences in carbon stocks among species reflect the combined influence of floristic composition and tree structural characteristics, indicating that species abundance alone does not determine their contribution to carbon storage. The illustrative economic valuation provides a monetary reference for the carbon stored in the evaluated forest, but does not represent financial returns or certified carbon credits. The findings contribute to understanding the role of small urban forest remnants as components of green infrastructure, particularly in small municipalities where local information on biomass and carbon stocks remains limited. However, the results are restricted to a single site and a single inventory, preventing conclusions about carbon sequestration rates or long-term climate mitigation effectiveness. Further studies across different urban environments, combined with repeated inventories, are needed to assess the persistence of carbon stocks and support broader urban vegetation planning strategies.
