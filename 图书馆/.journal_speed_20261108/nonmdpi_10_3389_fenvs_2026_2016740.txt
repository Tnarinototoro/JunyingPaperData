FoAP-Formation et Apprentissage Professionnels, InstitutAgro Dijon, CNAM Paris, ENSTA-10 Institute Polytechnique de Paris, Brest, France See more Article metrics View details Abstract Introduction: Agricultural-waste-derived activated carbons offer a sustainable alternative to conventional adsorbents; however, most studies rely on single biomass precursors and evaluate adsorption using only one model pollutant. Methods: In this study, a hybrid activated carbon (HAC) derived from banana peel and olive pomace was developed and evaluated for the removal of four structurally distinct dyes: methylene blue (MB), methyl orange (MO), crystal violet (CV), and Eriochrome Black T (EBT). The effects of adsorbent dosage, pH, and contact time were investigated, followed by adsorption isotherm, kinetic, and regeneration analyses. Results: An adsorbent dosage of 12 g/L and pH 2 provided the most favorable adsorption conditions. The hybrid activated carbon was found to have a high removal efficiency of 97.71% for MB, 98.41% for MO, 98.70% for CV, and 98.95% for EBT, respectively, under the optimized conditions to remove different dyes with different chemical structures. The equilibrium data were generally better described by the Langmuir model, while the adsorption kinetics showed stronger agreement with the pseudo-second-order model. HAC exhibited maximum adsorption capacities of 130.34, 99.70, 112.42, and 114.52 mg/g for MO, MB, CV, and EBT, respectively, with hybridization enhancing the adsorption capacities toward MO and EBT compared with the individual activated carbons. Regeneration experiments showed that HAC retained adsorption functionality after ten cycles, although removal efficiencies decreased to approximately 42%–56%. Discussion: The results in this study validate the potential of olive pomace and banana peel-based hybrid activated carbon as a waste derived, reusable material for the removal of dyes from contaminated wastewater, thereby valorizing agricultural waste and promoting sustainable resource utilization. 1 Introduction The discharge of contaminated wastewater into aquatic environments has been accelerated by industrialization and rapid urbanization worldwide (Ahmed et al., 2025). The textile, leather, cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and dye manufacturing sectors are among the top sources of water pollution resulting from the release of synthetic dyes into wastewater (Kocaman, 2025). The world-wide consumption of dyes is around 7 × 10^7 tons per year with 10%–15% of the produced dyes being lost during the manufacturing and dyeing operations before entering aquatic systems (Ramamurthy et al., 2024). In developing countries, the poor wastewater treatment facilities lead to the direct discharge of a significant amount of untreated industrial effluents into rivers and sources of ground water (Monsalvo, 2015). The United Nations (UNESCO, 2017) reported that almost 80% of wastewater is released into the environment without treatment, additionally the World Health Organization (WHO, 2021) mentioned that over 2 billion people depend on untreated water. Efficient wastewater treatment technologies are needed to prevent hundreds of thousands of deaths per year from waterborne diseases and toxic exposure caused by polluted water (Ramteke and Rathod, 2025). Synthetic dyes pose an additional challenge due to their complex aromatic structures, which render them resistant to chemical, thermal or photolytically degradation (Khandelwal et al., 2024). Dyes can be harmful even in concentrations as low as 1 mg/L, reducing light penetration and negatively affecting photosynthetic activity and dissolved oxygen levels in aquatic ecosystems (Khandelwal et al., 2024; Nandasana and Ghosh, 2024). Thus, substantial number of dyes are also known to possess carcinogenic, mutagenic and toxic proprieties to both land and aquatic organisms (Soyinka et al., 2026). Among these, Methylene blue (MB), methyl orange (MO), Eriochrome Black T and crystal violet (CV) are all dyes commonly used in textile, leather, cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and dye manufacturing industries, with the textile sector alone responsible for 80% of global dye consumption. Furthermore, these dyes possess oncogenic as well as low biodegradability properties (De et al., 2022; Oladoye et al., 2022; Velusamy et al., 2022; Jain, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Additionally, these dyes represent major dye classes, including cationic thiazine (MB) and triarylmethane (CV) dyes, as well as anionic azo dyes such as MO and EBT, thereby allowing the evaluation of adsorbent performance across structurally and chemically diverse pollutants (Sukumaran and Ramalingam, 2006; El-Sayed et al., 2018; Silva et al., 2023; Fujisawa, 2026). These dyes have, therefore, raised awareness and research on the development of sustainable and efficient treatment technologies (Hamed Latef and Jwaid, 2025). Multiple methods for wastewater treatment and dye removal have been studied and applied such as coagulation-flocculation, membrane filtration, advanced oxidation, ion exchange, and biological degradation (Kyzas et al., 2013; Marin et al., 2018; Moradihamedani, 2022; Okeke et al., 2022; Ali et al., 2025). These techniques can be highly effective at removing pollutants with an adsorption range of 50%–90% under optimum conditions (Javeed et al., 2023; Dang et al., 2026). Although they demonstrate several drawbacks, including the production of sludge, fouling of membranes, the need for complex operating procedures, and the fact that they can only remove a certain amount of pollutants (Zhang et al., 2024; 2026; Ahmed et al., 2025). Therefore, adsorption has become one of the most promising technologies of wastewater treatment due to its simplicity, low cost, high efficiency, and operational flexibility (Kyzas et al., 2013). The highly porous structure and the high surface area of activated carbon, combined with its substantial number of adsorption sites which can interact with molecules of dyes, make it one of the most effective adsorbent materials (Kyzas et al., 2013). The high production and regeneration cost of commercial activated carbon, however, has stimulated interest in the production of activated carbon and other low-cost adsorbents derived from agricultural wastes and biomass residues (Patel et al., 2025). Agricultural biomass materials are renewable, low cost, biodegradable, and rich in lignocellulosic compounds (cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin), making them an attractive precursor to produce activated carbon (Ullah et al., 2024). In recent years, different wastes materials such as rice husk, coconut shell, sugarcane bagasse, fruit peel, date pits, and olive residues have been studied to use as an alternative adsorbent precursor (Bhatnagar et al., 2015; Kalamkar et al., 2024; Nata et al., 2024). The production of activated carbon from agricultural waste provides two benefits to the environment, as it helps to overcome the problem of biomass disposal and to obtain sustainable material for removing pollutants (Derashri et al., 2024). Banana peel has been the focus of significant attention as one of the various agricultural wastes studied because of its high availability and good adsorption properties (Gupta et al., 2025). Worldwide banana production is more than 120 million tons per year which results in copious amounts of peel waste, about 30%–40% of the total banana mass (Feng and Yang, 2024). Previous studies reported methylene blue removal efficiency up to 90% using banana peel derived activated carbons under the optimum conditions (Gupta et al., 2020). Furthermore, olive pomace, one of the largest byproducts of olive oil processing, is a promising precursor for activated carbon production (Agboola et al., 2019; Alonso-Fariñas et al., 2020). The olive pomace produced by the Mediterranean countries leads to a substantial amount averaging 15 million tons of waste per year, with a high organic content and phytotoxic substances, which generate significant environmental disposal problems (Zabaniotou et al., 2015; Ameziane et al., 2024). When olive pomace is not disposed of properly it could lead to contamination of soil and water, which underlines the need to develop sustainable practices to manage the waste (Kinab and Khoury, 2015). Activated carbon production and dye adsorption processes are among the most promising applications of olive pomace, owing it to its high carbon content of approximately 90wt%, widespread availability, and strong potential for conversion into efficient and sustainable adsorbent materials (Agboola et al., 2019; Martínez-Alvarenga et al., 2024). Despite these advancements, the development of efficient, versatile, and scalable biomass-derived adsorbents suitable for real wastewater systems remain a significant challenge. Although biomass-derived activated carbons have been extensively investigated, several critical limitations remain insufficiently addressed. Most existing studies focus on single-source precursors, which restrict the ability to tailor adsorbent properties through synergistic material design (Luo et al., 2022; Serafin and Dziejarski, 2023). In addition, adsorption performance is commonly evaluated using a single model dye, typically methylene blue, limiting the assessment of adsorbent versatility toward structurally and chemically diverse pollutants (Mohammadi et al., 2017; Namal and Kalipci, 2019; Bakar et al., 2021). Consequently, the applicability of such materials to real textile effluents, where dyes with different molecular structures, charges, and functional groups may coexist, remains uncertain. To better reflect this multifactorial complexity under controlled experimental conditions, four representative model dyes from distinct structural and ionic classes were selected in the present study: methylene blue (cationic thiazine), crystal violet (cationic triarylmethane), methyl orange (anionic azo), and Eriochrome Black T (anionic azo). The use of these structurally diverse model dyes enables the adsorption performance of the developed material to be assessed across different dye chemistries rather than against a single model pollutant, while still maintaining controlled and reproducible experimental conditions. Furthermore, limited attention has been given to the development of hybrid adsorbents derived from complementary biomass sources, particularly those capable of combining distinct structural and functional properties. In this context, the integration of banana peel and olive pomace offers a promising yet underexplored approach, where banana peel provides abundant surface functional groups, while olive pomace contributes a highly porous carbonaceous structure, potentially enhancing adsorption capacity and selectivity. Moreover, the utilization of these abundant agricultural by-products supports sustainable waste valorization and promotes the development of environmentally friendly adsorbents within the framework of a circular economy. Therefore, this study aims to develop a sustainable hybrid activated carbon (HAC) derived from banana peel and olive pomace and to evaluate its performance toward structurally diverse dyes, including cationic dyes (methylene blue and crystal violet) and anionic dyes (methyl orange and Eriochrome Black T). Batch adsorption experiments were conducted to investigate the effects of pH, contact time, adsorbent dosage, and initial dye concentration, while adsorption isotherm, kinetic, and reusability analyses were performed to provide a comprehensive evaluation of adsorption efficiency, selectivity, and reusability. 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Raw materials and chemicals Olive pomace was obtained from a local oil mill in (Lebanon, Koura) and banana peels from a local fruit shop (Lebanon, chekka) were selected as lignocellulosic precursor materials for activated carbon production. Prior to preparation, the materials were manually cleaned to remove visible impurities and contaminants. The adsorbates used in the adsorption experiments included methyl orange (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany, 85%), methylene blue (Anmol Chemicals, India, 99%), crystal violet (Surechem Products Ltd, England, C.I. 42555), and Eriochrome Black T (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany, 99%), Additionally, the chemicals used throughout the experiments, including potassium hydroxide (KOH), hydrochloric acid (EMSURE, Germany, 99%) and sodium hydroxide (Sigma-Aldrich, France, 85%), sodium chloride (Honeywell, Germany, 99.5%). Distilled water was used for solution preparation, washing procedures, and experimental dilution. 2.2 Instruments and equipment The experimental work was carried out using a drying oven (Binder APT. line FD E2, Binder, Germany), a muffle furnace (LT 5/11, Nabertherm, Germany), a microplate reader (Epoch, BioTek, United States of America), an analytical balance (ATX224, Phoenix Instrument, Germany), a centrifuge (90,000-3 P Oil Test Centrifuge, Stanhope-Seta, Germany), a benchtop pH meter (HQ440D, HACH-LANGE, S.L.U, Germany), a shaking incubator (BJPX-100N, BIOBASE BIOLAND Co., Ltd, China), and a high-speed grinder (SF-3584, Sonifer, China), ATR-FTIR spectrometer (TENSOR 27, Bruker, Germany), thermogravimetric analyzer (TG 209F1 Libra, NETZSCH, Germany),Laser scattering particle size distribution analyzer (LA-950, HORIBA, Ltd., Japan). 2.3 Preparation of activated carbons 2.3.1 Preparation of olive pomace activated carbon (OP-AC)2.3.1.1 Drying and pretreatment The raw olive pomace was first screened to retrieve the kernels from the remaining olive waste to prepare them into activated carbon. After that, the recovered material was dried in a drying oven at 110 °C for 24 h to eliminate the moisture and improve reproducibility. Biomass pretreatment is typically conducted under drying conditions of 105 °C–110 °C to remove water that is physically adsorbed in the biomass, but not thermally degraded, whilst allowing some thermal degradation of the lignocellulosic components (Qu et al., 2025). The dried material was ground to a finer particle size using a laboratory grinder to provide a more uniform and homogenous precursor and provide greater surface exposure to the material during later processing steps. 2.3.1.2 Pre-Carbonization The prepared powder was transferred into porcelain crucibles and covered with aluminum foil to reduce direct contact with atmospheric oxygen during heating. Pre-Carbonization was conducted under limited oxygen conditions using a heating rate of 5 °C/min and maintained at 250 °C for 30 min. The selected Pre-carbonization temperature was chosen because it prepares a carbon-rich precursor ready for activation (Qu et al., 2025). After Pre-carbonization, the material was allowed to cool to room temperature. 2.3.1.3 Chemical activation The carbonized material was treated with chemical activation by using 20% (w/v) potassium hydroxide (KOH) at the ratio of 2g:3g solid to liquid (Şirazi and Aslan, 2021; Qu et al., 2025). This mixture was left to impregnate for 24 h to ensure the impregnation of the activating agent into the carbon matrix and to promote the formation of the pores. After impregnation, the thermal activation was carried out in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 20 min with a heating rate of 2 °C/min (Qu et al., 2025). The range of temperatures used is approximately 500 °C–600 °C, which was chosen as it has been reported as promoting volatile release, pore enlargement and surface area development, but with minimal structural collapse of the activated carbon (Şirazi and Aslan, 2021). 2.3.1.4 Washing and post-treatment After activation, the material was cooled to room temperature and washed four times with 10% hydrochloric acid to remove the traces of the activating agents and inorganic content. Then, the procedure of repeated washing was carried with distilled water out until the wash solution was neutral (pH ≈ 7) and clear to ensure the removal of residual KOH, soluble salts, ash and other inorganic residues. The activated carbon was dried at 110 °C for 24 h to remove the moisture and kept in an airtight container for further use. 2.3.2 Preparation of banana peel activated carbon (BP-AC)2.3.2.1 Drying and pretreatment Banana peels were divided into smaller pieces, washed thoroughly with distilled water to remove adhering impurities and dried at 110 °C for 24 h following the same drying conditions described for OP-AC (Section 2.3.1.1). The dried material was then ground using a laboratory grinder to obtain a homogeneous precursor. 2.3.2.2 Pre-Carbonization The prepared powder was transferred into covered porcelain crucibles and carbonized under limited oxygen conditions using a heating rate of 5 °C/min and maintained at 250 °C for 30min, following the procedure described for OP-AC. 2.3.2.3 Chemical activation Chemical activation was performed using a 20% KOH solution at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 2g:3g (w/w) for 24 h. The impregnated samples were subsequently thermally activated at 550 °C for 20 min using a heating rate of 2 °C/min, under the same activation conditions applied for OP-AC (Al-sareji et al., 2024; Sreńscek-Nazzal et al., 2024). 2.3.2.4 Washing and post-treatment After cooling, activated samples were washed 4 times using 10% hydrochloric acid and repeatedly rinsed with distilled water until neutral pH conditions (pH ≈ 7) were achieved. The final activated product was dried at 110 °C for 24 h and stored in sealed containers until further experimentation. 2.3.3 Preparation of hybrid activated carbon (H-AC) Both OP-AC and BP-AC were physically mixed in 1:1 mass ratio and a physical hybrid activated carbon was prepared as shown in Figure 1. The mixture was placed in the shaker for 4h until the particles were evenly mixed. The hybrid adsorbent was prepared and kept in sealed containers and was employed in all the adsorption and regeneration experiments. Each activated carbon sample was dispersed in distilled water, and the measurements were carried out at a circulation speed of level 7 and an agitation speed of level 7, with ultrasonic treatment applied before analysis (Mori et al., 2007). The refractive indices were set at 1.550–1.550i for the sample and 1.333 for water, and the distribution was expressed on a volume basis (Mori et al., 2007). The median size (D50), mean size, mode size, and the diameters at 10% and 90% cumulative undersize (D10 and D90) were recorded for each sample. 2.4.2 Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) The thermal stabilities of the activated carbon samples were determined by the NETZSCH TG 209F1 thermogravimetric analyzer. Nitrogen atmosphere was used for the analysis of about 4.5–7.7 mg of each activated carbon sample at a heating rate of 10 k/min between 30 °C and 500 °C. The mass changes of the samples were measured continuously with temperature and the mass left after heating to 500 °C was obtained from the TG curves. 2.4.3 Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) The surface functional groups of the activated carbon were characterized using an ATR-FTIR spectrometer. The spectra were recorded over the range of 4,000–400 cm-1 at a resolution of 4 cm-1, using 32 scans for both the sample and background. The spectra were obtained in transmittance mode, with atmospheric compensation enabled. The characteristic bands were identified based on their corresponding wavenumber ranges and assigned to the relevant surface functional groups. 2.4.4 Point of zero charge (pHpzc) The point of zero charge (pHpzc) of OP-AC, BP-AC, and H-AC was determined using the pH drift method with 0.4 g of each activated carbon being added to a series of distilled water solutions adjusted to different initial pH values. The suspensions were agitated at 250 rpm and 25 °C for 24 h. After equilibration, the final pH of each suspension was measured and recorded. The difference between the initial and final pH values (pH2 − pH1) was calculated and plotted against the initial pH (pH1). The pHpzc was determined from the intersection of the resulting curve with the line where pH2 − pH1 = 0. 2.5 Dye preparation and calibration curves Individual stock solutions of methyl orange (MO),methylene blue (MB),crystal violet (CV), and Eriochrome black T (EBT) were prepared by dissolving 50 mg of dye in 50 mL of distilled water to obtain an initial concentration of 1,000 mg/L. Working concentrations were subsequently prepared by dilution. The absorbance of each dye solution was measured using UV–Vis spectrophotometry at its corresponding maximum wavelength. As outlined in Section 2.6, these parameters include adsorbent dosage, solution pH, contact time and isotherms and kinetics. All parameters were investigated individually while keeping the experimental conditions constant to enable a proper comparison of the results. The adsorption capacity and removal efficiency were used as quantitative evaluation indicators for the adsorption performance. The adsorption kinetic models pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order were used to analyze the kinetic data, while the Langmuir and Freundlich models were used for the equilibrium models. The coefficient of determination (R2) was calculated to evaluate the goodness of fit for the kinetic and isotherm models, and the adsorption capacities were compared to experimental ones. Data was analyzed statistically using JASP (Version 0.19.8.1). Using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), the mean values obtained under different experimental conditions were compared; with a significance level of 95% (p < 0.05). Each adsorption experiment was conducted three times with the appropriate mean ± SD given. 3 Results and discussion The results of the experiments are reported and explained in this section. The characterization of the prepared adsorbents is presented followed by an evaluation of the effect of adsorption parameters, isotherms, kinetics and statistical analysis to have a thorough understanding of the adsorption process. 3.1 Laser diffraction particle size analysis From the particle size analysis, all three adsorbents exhibited a broad unimodal particle-size distribution ranging from approximately 1–300 μm, with a tail toward the finer particle sizes. The median particle size (D50) was 57.51 µm for OP-AC, 83.07 µm for BP-AC, and 47.08 µm for H-AC, while the corresponding mean particle sizes were 64.37, 87.03, and 58.17 µm, respectively. The mode particle size was 125.11 µm for BP-AC and 82.94 µm for H-AC. The D90 value was 162.28 µm for BP-AC and 126.50 µm for H-AC. OP-AC showed intermediate values, with D10 = 9.74 µm, D90 = 131.42 µm, and a mode particle size of 94.89 µm. The differences in particle size are relevant to the interpretation of the adsorption behavior of the prepared adsorbents. Based on the median particle sizes, the order was H-AC < OP-AC < BP-AC, indicating that H-AC had the smallest median particle size. Smaller particles may provide a greater external surface area and improve access to adsorption sites (Nandasana and Ghosh, 2024). However, adsorption capacity does not depend solely on particle size or external surface area, but is also influenced by other material properties, including surface chemistry and porosity (Nandasana and Ghosh, 2024). 3.2 Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) The TGA results of BP-AC, OP-AC, and H-AC, presented in Supplementary Figures S1-S3, respectively, revealed relatively small mass losses over the temperature range studied. For BP-AC, the total mass loss was 4.84%, with a remaining mass of 88.20% at approximately 500 °C. The OP-AC sample exhibited two consecutive mass-loss stages, occurring between approximately 28 °C–84 °C (4.64%) and 95 °C–164 °C (4.55%). The residual mass of OP-AC at 500 °C was 82.32%. Similarly, H-AC showed mass losses of 4.78% and 3.10% over the temperature ranges of approximately 31 °C–87 °C and 95 °C–165 °C, respectively, with a residual mass of 83.25% at approximately 500 °C. The relatively high residual masses of the three activated carbons suggest that the thermal treatment conditions did not result in substantial mass loss (Akuwueke et al., 2024). The mass-loss profile of H-AC showed behavior similar to that of OP-AC and BP-AC, with an initial mass loss at lower temperatures, which could be attributed to the removal of moisture and volatile compounds, followed by relatively stable behavior up to 500 °C. 3.3 Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) The FTIR-ATR spectra of OP-AC, BP-AC, and H-AC are presented in Figures 3a–c, respectively. The main surface functional groups were identified from the characteristic absorption bands in order to assess the general chemical nature of the activated carbon surfaces. FIGURE 3 ATR-FTIR spectra of (a) olive pomace activated carbon (OP-AC), (b) banana peel activated carbon (BP-AC), and (c) hybrid activated carbon (H-AC). For H-AC, several distinct bands were observed across the spectrum. The band at approximately 3,653 cm-1 may be assigned to O–H stretching vibrations, suggesting the presence of surface hydroxyl groups. The bands at 2,981 and 2,889 cm-1 were associated with the C–H stretching vibrations of aliphatic groups, while the band at approximately 1,574 cm-1 was attributed to aromatic C=C stretching and may also include contributions from conjugated C=O groups. The region between 1,392 and 1,251 cm-1 was associated with C–H deformation and C–O/C–O–C vibrations. Additional bands at approximately 1,154 and 1,082 cm-1 were assigned to C–O stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of oxygen-containing surface functionalities. The band around 830 cm-1 was attributed to out-of-plane aromatic C–H bending. Overall, these bands indicate the presence of hydroxyl, aromatic, and oxygen-containing functional groups commonly reported in biomass-derived activated carbons (Fletcher et al., 2007). The distribution of functional groups in the OP-AC spectrum (Figure 3a) was similar to that observed for H-AC (Figure 3c). The band at approximately 2,981 cm-1 was attributed to C–H stretching, while the band at approximately 1,619 cm-1 was associated with aromatic C=C stretching and/or conjugated carbonyl groups. The bands at approximately 1,391 and 1,252 cm-1 were assigned to C–H deformation and C–O/C–O–C vibrations, respectively. The bands at approximately 1,155 and 1,085 cm-1 were attributed to C–O stretching vibrations, while the band at approximately 831 cm-1 was assigned to out-of-plane aromatic C–H bending. Similar bands have also been reported for activated carbons derived from olive pomace, mainly within the ranges of 1,570–1,620, 1,200–1,250, 1,000–1,150, and 800–850 cm-1. The BP-AC spectrum also exhibited several characteristic bands. The band at approximately 1,568 cm-1 was attributed to aromatic C=C stretching and may also include contributions from conjugated carbonyl groups. Bands at approximately 1,157 and 1,090 cm-1 were associated with C–O stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of oxygen-containing surface functionalities. Additional bands at 878, 811, 751, and 701 cm-1 were assigned to out-of-plane aromatic C–H bending vibrations. Further bands were observed at lower wavenumbers, including 553, 532, and 442–432 cm-1. In the higher-wavenumber region, no pronounced broad O–H stretching band was observed, suggesting a lower contribution of hydroxyl-containing groups in BP-AC compared with H-AC and OP-AC. Overall, BP-AC exhibited predominantly aromatic and oxygen-containing surface functionalities, although their distribution differed from those observed for H-AC and OP-AC (Kwiatkowski, 2012). Overall, the three adsorbents exhibited aromatic and oxygen-containing functional groups, indicating that several surface functionalities were retained after activation while the materials remained predominantly carbonaceous. These functional groups may contribute to adsorption through mechanisms such as hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and interactions with aromatic structures of pollutants (Kwiatkowski, 2012). The similarities among the spectra indicate that the three activated carbons possess broadly comparable aromatic and oxygen-containing surface functionalities after activation. 3.4 Point of zero charge (pHpzc) Figure 4 shows the pH2 − pH1 curves were intersected with the zero line to obtain the pHpzc values. Langmuir affinity constants (KL) were found to be different for the dyes, indicating different adsorption affinities towards each adsorbent. For example, H-AC exhibited relatively low KL values for MO (0.018 ± 0.0015 L/mg) and EBT (0.020 ± 0.005 L/mg), whereas different values were observed for MB (0.0049 ± 0.0003 L/mg) and CV (0.0060 ± 0.0002L/mg). The KF values of MO and EBT for H-AC (2.98 ± 0.98L g-1 and 2.99 ± 0.67L/g) were generally higher than those of the individual activated carbons, further confirming the improved adsorption capability of H-AC observed from the Langmuir analysis. Moreover, all Freundlich adsorption intensity values (n = 1.25–1.59) were larger than unity, indicating favorable adsorption within the investigated concentration range. Values of n between 1 and 10 are generally associated with favorable adsorption processes, moderate surface heterogeneity, and strong adsorbate–adsorbent interactions (Na, 2020). The R2 values of Langmuir ranged from 0.978 to 0.999, while the R2 values of Freundlich ranged from 0.920 to 0.983. However, the Freundlich model also provided high correlation coefficients, especially for MO and CV adsorption, suggesting that both homogeneous and heterogeneous adsorption processes contributed to the overall adsorption mechanism. The hybrid activated carbon demonstrated balanced adsorption performance across the four investigated dyes compared with the individual adsorbents. It exhibited the maximum adsorption capacities for MO and EBT while maintaining competitive adsorption capacities for MB and CV. These results indicate that physically blending OP-AC and BP-AC successfully combined the beneficial surface properties of both activated carbons, producing a more versatile adsorbent for the removal of dyes with different molecular structures and charge characteristics. The improved performance of H-AC is likely related to the complementary pore structures, surface functionalities, and accessible adsorption sites provided by both OP-AC and BP-AC after blending (Martínez-Alvarenga et al., 2024; Tran et al., 2025). Overall, all three activated carbons demonstrated high adsorption potential for the investigated dyes. H-AC exhibited enhanced adsorption capacities for MO and EBT while maintaining competitive adsorption performance for MB and CV, indicating a more balanced adsorption behavior across dyes with different molecular characteristics. Accordingly, the sustainability potential of the proposed biomass-based activated carbon is assessed across these three interconnected dimensions. 4.1 Environmental sustainability The sustainability value of the developed hybrid activated carbon is not only because of its high efficiency of dye removal but also due to valorization of agricultural waste and the reuse of the material in wastewater treatment. Olive pomace and banana peels, which are by-products of agricultural and food-processing activities, can be transformed from waste into useful absorbent materials. This approach can help reduce the environmental impact associated with agricultural residues while promoting a more circular and efficient use of resources (Gupta et al., 2025; Alrowais et al., 2026). The high removal efficiency obtained for EBT (98.95%), CV (98.70%), MO (98.41%), and MB (97.71%) further strengthen the environmental value of the proposed material. These results are not just about the fact that a high percentage of the dye was removed, but also the same hybrid material was effective against dyes of different chemical structure as shown in Table 2. This indicates the adsorbent may be used for wastewater containing mixtures of contaminants and thus does not necessarily need to be specially formulated for each contaminant. From a sustainability perspective, a higher adsorption capacity can help optimize the resource efficiency of treatment process if one material can be used for several treatment purposes. The decrease of dye concentrations prior to the discharge of wastewater also directly contributes to SDG 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation) by helping to prevent the release of pollutants into aquatic environments (Bakar et al., 2021; Bel Hassen et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025; Mujtaba et al., 2026). Another important environmental consideration is the post adsorption treatment of the material. The results of the regeneration experiment revealed that the hybrid activated carbon was able to maintain about 42%–56% of its adsorption capability after 10 adsorption–regeneration cycles. While this is a decrease from previous absorption performance, it demonstrates that the material can be used over many cycles, potentially minimizing the use of fresh adsorbent and limiting the production of spent adsorbent. This reusability supports SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), specifically Target 12.2, which aims to use natural resources efficiently and sustainably, as well as Target 12.5, which focuses on reducing waste generation through prevention, reduction, recycling, and reuse (Feng and Yang, 2024; Firoiu et al., 2025; Lei et al., 2025). The proposed approach may also indirectly contribute to SDG 13 (Climate Action) by reducing the need for virgin raw materials and the frequency of absorbent replacement. However, since greenhouse gas emissions and the carbon footprint of the production process were not directly evaluated, this contribution should be considered a potential climate benefit rather than a demonstrated reduction in emissions. 4.2 Social sustainability The proposed material contributes to social sustainability by connecting agricultural activities, waste management and water treatment through the beneficial use of agricultural residues. Social sustainability in agricultural encompasses the relationships between agricultural activities, local communities, livelihoods, resources, and institutions (Janker et al., 2019). In this context, converting agricultural by-products into adsorbents provides a locally available resource for addressing water-quality challenges. The use of locally available agricultural by-products is of relevance as they depend on the agricultural activity in the area. The local production of the treatment materials would contribute to the reduction of reliance on externally produced adsorbents and would provide opportunities for local treatment and processing systems. This may be especially useful when the existing treatment material is not readily available or when transportation is a limiting factor. This can contribute to more accessible treatment solutions while creating opportunities for local value chains and employment related to the collection and processing of biomass residues. The beneficial use of agricultural residues also promotes resource-efficient practices and promotes awareness of circular environmental management. In addition, improved dye removal can contribute to healthier water environments by reducing the amount of potentially harmful contaminants entering receiving waters. The proposed approach therefore primarily supports SDG 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation) through improved wastewater quality, while also contributing to SDG 3 (Good Health and Wellbeing), particularly Target 3.9, through the reduction of pollution-related risks. 4.3 Economic sustainability The economic sustainability of the hybrid activated carbon is mainly linked to its capability to derive treatment functionality from low-value agricultural residues. Olive pomace and banana peels cannot be produced for their use as adsorbent feedstock but are rather by-products of the agricultural and food-processing industries. As a result of their transformation into activated carbon, they can be repurposed from a waste stream and help to lower the need for virgin precursor materials which creates opportunities for greater resource efficiency within agricultural value chains (Boyle et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025). The economic value of the proposed adsorbent should also be considered over its service life rather than only in terms of its initial production cost. Its demonstrated reusability over multiple adsorption reaching 42%–56% removal efficiency even after 10 cycles as shown in Figure 10. Regeneration cycles could potentially reduce the frequency of adsorbent replacement and, consequently, the quantity of material required over the treatment period. However, the decrease in the adsorption efficiency has shown that there is a practical limit to regeneration. A worthwhile economic evaluation would then be required to decide if the energy, chemicals, labor and equipment needed for regeneration are outweighed by the savings from reuse. At a larger level, production of activated carbon from agricultural residues may generate more economic activities around collection, processing, activation and wastewater treatment of residues. These potential resource-efficiency and local value chain benefits align with SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), specifically Target 8.4. In addition, the development of a waste-derived treatment material represents an innovative approach that aligns with SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure) (Priya et al., 2025; Alrowais et al., 2026). Overall, the sustainability potential of the proposed biomass-based activated carbon results from the combination of effective dye removal, agricultural waste valorization, and demonstrated reusability. The approach therefore provides a promising link between agricultural waste management and wastewater treatment. Nevertheless, further validation, using life-cycle and techno-economic assessments, is needed to determine its environmental, economic and social performance and its feasibility at larger scales. 5 Conclusion 6 A hybrid activated carbon derived from banana peel and olive pomace was successfully developed as a sustainable adsorbent for the removal of structurally diverse dyes from aqueous solutions. The optimum adsorbent dosage was 12 g/L, while the highest adsorption performance was obtained under strongly acidic conditions, particularly at pH 2, which may limit practical application because of the need for pH adjustment. Equilibrium analysis showed that the Langmuir model generally provided the best fit, with maximum adsorption capacities of 130.34, 99.70, 112.42, and 114.52 mg/g for MO, MB, CV, and EBT, respectively, while the pseudo-second-order model better described the adsorption kinetics. The hybrid adsorbent showed improved adsorption capacities for MO and EBT compared with the individual banana-peel- and olive-pomace-derived activated carbons, supporting the benefit of combining complementary biomass precursors. Although H-AC remained reusable after ten adsorption–regeneration cycles, removal efficiencies declined to approximately 42%–56%, highlighting the need to improve regeneration performance and long-term stability. Further studies should evaluate H-AC using real textile effluents containing dissolved salts, surfactants, competing organic matter, and mixed contaminants. Moreover, the sustainability assessment presented in this study remains primarily conceptual, as direct data on emissions and process costs were not generated; therefore, techno-economic and life-cycle assessments are needed to quantify the environmental and economic feasibility of large-scale application.
