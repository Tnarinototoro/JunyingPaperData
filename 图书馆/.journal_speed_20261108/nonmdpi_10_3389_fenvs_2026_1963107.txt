Geosciences Department, College of Science, United Arab Emirates University, Al Ain, United Arab Emirates See more Article metrics View details Abstract Introduction: Groundwater is the primary dependable freshwater source in the arid coastal catchments of western Saudi Arabia, where natural recharge is limited and occurs mainly through episodic rainfall and flash‐flood infiltration along wadi systems. This study assesses groundwater potential in the Wadi Marwani basin (≈2,347 km2), a major tributary of the Khulais system southeast of Rabigh, by integrating multi‐source remote sensing, GIS, and aeromagnetic data. Methods: Lithology was mapped from Sentinel‐2 imagery using support vector machine (SVM) classification, while structural fabrics were constrained using Sentinel‐1C SAR lineaments and aeromagnetic structural analysis. Terrain attributes (e.g., elevation, slope, drainage and flow indices, topographic wetness, and ruggedness) derived from a 30 m digital elevation model (DEM), together with NDVI, soil moisture, rainfall, and 10 m land‐cover data, characterized the surface conditions that control infiltration and storage. Aeromagnetic data were reduced to the pole, upward continued, and enhanced using the modified Elliott function (MEF) filter and Center for Exploration Targeting (CET) grid analysis. Source Parameter Imaging (SPI) was then applied to estimate relative depth to magnetic basement. Results: Surface and magnetic lineaments consistently define a dominant N–S to NNW–SSE trend with subsidiary NW–SE and NE–SW sets that remain coherent at depth, reflecting reactivated Pan–African structures. A magnetically subdued, elongate basement depression along the central wadi axis, widening westwards, coincides with high lineament density and thick Quaternary alluvium, indicating favorable conditions for groundwater accumulation. The integrated groundwater potential map divides the basin into five classes; comparison with existing wells yields an AUC of 0.789, indicating acceptable to good predictive skill. Discussion: The most promising zones lie along the central wadi corridor and the western alluvial plain. The integrated workflow is readily transferable to other basement‐controlled arid catchments. 1 Introduction In most arid and semiarid countries, aquifer storage is the only freshwater reserve that can realistically be used year after year (Adimalla et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020). Saudi Arabia illustrates the point clearly: fresh renewable water outside the aquifer system is scarce, and because rainfall is both sparse and erratic, replenishment occurs mainly during infrequent floods that move down otherwise dry wadi channels. Recharge is therefore restricted in space to the channel network and in time to a handful of storm events. The resulting imbalance between abstraction and replenishment has produced a persistent national water deficit, felt most acutely in the domestic and agricultural sectors (Omar and Moussa, 2016). Conservation schemes have accordingly been introduced in farming districts that depend almost wholly on aquifer supplies and on episodic runoff. Safeguarding these supplies has become a policy priority because irrigation, which accounts for most of the national demand, relies almost entirely on groundwater and increasingly draws on stressed reserves (Al-Ahmadi and El-Fiky, 2009). Both diminishing volumes and deteriorating chemical quality constrain further development of the resource (Morris et al., 2020), a concern of national significance wherever no alternative supply exists. Direct exploration of extensive and largely inaccessible desert catchments is seldom feasible, so satellite observation coupled with GIS processing has become the usual first-pass for identifying areas with potential for groundwater occurrence. Optical, radar, and elevation datasets permit consistent regional mapping of the surface variables that condition infiltration and storage: rock type, structural discontinuities, slope, drainage organization, vegetation vigor, soil moisture, and land cover. Each variable acts as a proxy for recharge capacity or aquifer yield (e.g., El Osta et al., 2022; El-Badrawy et al., 2024; 2026). These proxies are typically weighted and combined using either multi-criteria decision frameworks, most commonly the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) of Saaty (1980), or supervised machine learning (ML) algorithms calibrated on documented well occurrences (Rana et al., 2022; Hasan et al., 2025; Rana et al., 2025). In either case, map performance is normally quantified against withheld field observations by means of receiver operating characteristic statistics (Fawcett, 2006). These methods have been widely applied across the Arabian Peninsula. Within Saudi Arabia they have underpinned assessments of groundwater chemistry and aquifer behaviour in the western coastal catchments (El Osta et al., 2022; Alqarawy et al., 2022; Masoud et al., 2022; Alqarawy, 2023), geomorphometric characterisation of the Makkah Al-Mukarramah basins and their runoff response (Niyazi et al., 2020), flash-flood hazard evaluation in Asir (Salih and Hassablla, 2024) and vulnerability mapping of coastal aquifers (El Osta et al., 2026). Equivalent frameworks have been reported from other dryland terrains, among them the Ifal Basin in the north-west of the Kingdom (Elawadi et al., 2013) and the Kraaipan granite–greenstone belt of Botswana (Ranganai et al., 2017). Groundwater potential mapping has progressed from expert-weighted overlay and AHP workflows to hybrid frameworks that combine remote sensing, GIS, and ML. AHP-based geospatial analysis has been used to classify agriculture-dominated terrain into potential zones in Bangladesh (Rana et al., 2022). Later studies coupled remote sensing with ML algorithms (Rana et al., 2025) and applied ML and geospatial approaches in industrial areas under heavy abstraction pressure (Hasan et al., 2025). Ensemble ML models and Explainable AI tools, such as SHAP, now allow the contribution of each conditioning factor to be quantified. The same terrain and hydrological descriptors (slope, ruggedness, wetness, and drainage density) are used in flash-flood susceptibility mapping, which is relevant in arid basins because flash floods are the main recharge mechanism. Surface-based models, whether MCDA or ML, still cannot resolve aquifer geometry. This limitation has led to the integration of potential-field geophysics with radar, optical, and morphometric data to constrain basement relief and fracture corridors and so reduce spatial uncertainty (Araffa et al., 2020; El-Badrawy et al., 2024; 2026). Surface variables, however, constrain only the uppermost part of the flow system. Where the aquifer rests on crystalline basement, both the thickness of the saturated sediment and its lateral continuity are set by basement topography and by the faults that dissect the basement. These subsurface characteristics cannot generally be determined directly from optical or conventional radar remote-sensing data alone. Integrating potential-field geophysics with satellite data, therefore, provides new information rather than duplicating existing knowledge. Workflows that combine the two have produced groundwater potential models that are more physically defensible and better validated in dryland settings (El-Badrawy et al., 2024; 2026; Araffa et al., 2020). Airborne magnetic surveying suits structurally governed aquifer systems because it images basement relief, fault zones and intensely fractured domains (El-Badrawy et al., 2024). The measured anomalies arise chiefly from the susceptibility difference between crystalline basement and the weakly magnetic sediments that bury it (El-Badrawy et al., 2023; 2026). Systematic changes in the amplitude and wavelength of those anomalies can accordingly be translated into changes in basement geometry, and hence into variations in sediment thickness, storage volume and flow direction (Araffa et al., 2020). Modern edge-enhancement operators isolate faults and other first-order structural boundaries, features that may either transmit or obstruct subsurface flow (El-Badrawy et al., 2024; 2026). Where the magnetic response is complex and laterally variable, fracturing or hydrothermal alteration of the basement is commonly implied, with a corresponding gain in secondary porosity and permeability that bears directly on recharge, storage and hydraulic continuity. A magnetic survey does not, of course, sense water itself; what it delivers is a structural and lithological framework which, read together with geological and hydrogeological observations, underpins a more physically grounded appraisal of groundwater potential (Araffa et al., 2020). Improvements in instrumentation, low-altitude acquisition, and processing algorithms have increased the spatial resolution of aeromagnetic grids, so that concealed structures can now be mapped with greater confidence (Hasan et al., 2024; Nagy et al., 2026). Magnetic data are therefore valuable for both reconnaissance and detailed site appraisal. No study has yet combined surface and subsurface evidence for the Wadi Marwani catchment. Earlier work here and in adjacent basins has dealt mainly with hydrochemistry and water-quality indexing (Al-Ahmadi and El-Fiky, 2009; El Osta et al., 2022; Alqarawy et al., 2022; Masoud et al., 2022; Alqarawy, 2023) or with catchment geomorphometry and aquifer vulnerability (Niyazi et al., 2020; El Osta et al., 2026). Those investigations describe the quality and the fragility of the resource, but they leave the geometry of the container itself unresolved: the depth and relief of the magnetic basement, the lateral continuity of the faults that partition the alluvial aquifer, and the degree to which structural corridors concentrate recharge from episodic floods all remain unconstrained. Since the productive horizon at Wadi Marwani is a comparatively thin alluvial body draped over deformed crystalline rocks, a model assembled from surface predictors alone cannot indicate where storage capacity is greatest. The novelty of this study lies in integrating surface recharge indicators with subsurface geophysical constraints rather than in introducing a new weighting algorithm. By combining Sentinel-1/2 observations and DEM-derived hydrological variables with aeromagnetic structural mapping and SPI-derived basement relief, the proposed framework evaluates both recharge favourability and the concealed geometry of the aquifer system. To the best of our knowledge, no previous investigation has applied this integrated surface–subsurface framework to groundwater potential assessment in the Wadi Marwani catchment. The present contribution closes that gap by interpreting aeromagnetic data jointly with multi-source satellite imagery and GIS analysis across the Wadi Marwani catchment. Five objectives are pursued: (i) mapping surface lithological units using Sentinel-2 imagery and delineating structural lineaments from Sentinel-1 SAR data; (ii) deriving the topographic, hydrological and hydro-environmental variables that govern runoff generation and infiltration from elevation and multispectral data; (iii) resolving the subsurface structural framework and the depth to magnetic basement by advanced edge-detection filtering, CET grid analysis and Source Parameter Imaging; (iv) fusing the surface and subsurface predictors to produce a catchment-wide groundwater potential map; and (v) testing that map against the documented well inventory by ROC analysis. No previous study has combined remote-sensing surface indicators with aeromagnetic basement constraints in this catchment. The resulting procedure can be transferred to other poorly documented, basement-floored catchments bordering the Red Sea. 2 Geological and hydrological settings Makkah Al-Mukarramah Province occupies roughly 141,216 km2 of western Saudi Arabia and functions as both a hydrological unit (Figure 1). Some 43 coastal wadis drain the province; these channels transmit episodic floods and, locally, constitute the principal sites of renewable recharge (Niyazi et al., 2020). The Wadi Marwani catchment lies in the southern Hijaz, southeast of Rabigh and roughly 90 km northeast of Jeddah (Figure 1). Its Quaternary aquifer holds appreciable reserves and sustains extensive cultivation on the coastal plain; as one of the larger feeders of the Khulais Basin it also contributes to domestic supply and vegetable production for Jeddah. Climatically the area is arid, in keeping with the Red Sea littoral (Alqarawy, 2023). FIGURE 1 Setting and geological framework of the Wadi Marwani catchment. (a) Position of the catchment on the eastern side of the Red Sea in western Saudi Arabia. (b) Generalized surface lithological map obtained by support vector machine (SVM) classification of Sentinel-2 data. Relief within the catchment is highly variable (Figure 1). The wadi floor forms a low-relief plain at about 1–400 m above mean sea level, whereas the bordering highlands rise to approximately 1,668 m. The trunk channel follows a sinuous, generally east-to-west course towards the Red Sea (Alqarawy et al., 2022; Alqarawy, 2023). Unconsolidated alluvium, ranging in caliber from sand to pebbles, blankets much of the catchment. Three broad rock associations are represented (Figure 1): Precambrian basement, Tertiary volcanic rocks, and Quaternary sediments. Alkali olivine basalt of the Rahat Group dominates the headwater sector, associated locally with pyroclastic material, feldspathoidal lavas, and rocks of intermediate composition (Al-Ahmadi and El-Fiky, 2009); these volcanic rocks span the Paleocene to the Miocene. Sericite–chlorite schist of the Neoproterozoic Samran Group represents the oldest exposed rock unit in the study area. The area also contains relatively undeformed post-tectonic plutonic rocks, including granite, quartz diorite, quartz monzodiorite, syenite, and gabbro. Alluvium beneath the wadi floor is derived from weathering and erosion of the surrounding highlands. It grades from coarse gravel in the upstream reaches into sand and clay downstream along the trunk channel (El Osta et al., 2022; 2026), and the resulting aquifer forms a renewable supply for irrigation, household use, and drinking water at several localities. Its chemistry is shaped by a combination of rainfall infiltration, seepage, mineral dissolution along the flow path, leakage from neighboring or contaminated ground, biological activity, residence time, and surface runoff (Masoud et al., 2022). 3 Materials and methods 3.1 Remote sensing data Groundwater-favourable ground in the Wadi Marwani catchment was delineated by combining satellite imagery with GIS analysis. Linear features were detected automatically with the LINE module of PCI Geomatica, which highlights the prevailing fault and fracture directions; the extraction parameters are listed in Table 1. Azimuthal distributions were plotted as rose diagrams in RockWorks 2018, and lineament frequency per square kilometre was gridded in ArcGIS 10.8 to locate ground of elevated structural permeability. The DEM was used to derive slope, delineate watershed boundaries, and extract channel networks. All derived hydrological grids were produced in ArcGIS 10.8 and used to interpret runoff behaviour and identify probable recharge zones. 3.1.3 Hydrological and terrain attributes A depression-filled version of the DEM was processed with the ArcGIS Hydrology toolset to obtain the D8 flow-direction grid, the associated flow-accumulation grid and the channel network, from which drainage density was derived as total channel length per unit area (Horton, 1932; Strahler, 1957). Three further indices were computed. The topographic wetness index, TWI = ln(α/tanβ), where α is the upslope contributing area per unit contour width and β is the local slope angle, indicates the tendency of a cell to accumulate water (Beven and Kirkby, 1979). The stream power index, SPWI = α × tanβ, expresses the erosive capacity of concentrated flow (Moore et al., 1991); the abbreviation SPWI is adopted here so that it is not confused with Source Parameter Imaging (SPI), the magnetic depth estimator introduced in Section 3.2.3. The terrain ruggedness index (TRI) is the mean absolute elevation difference between a cell and its eight neighbours and quantifies topographic heterogeneity (Riley et al., 1999). All grids were generated at the native 30 m resolution. 3.1.4 Vegetation and surface moisture assessment The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and a surface moisture index [MI = (B8A- B11)/(B8A+ B11)] were computed from Sentinel-2 multispectral scenes. Geometric registration and radiometric correction were carried out beforehand in the Sentinel Application Platform (SNAP). 3.1.5 Land use and land cover mapping Land use and land cover were taken from the Esri Global Land Cover product, a 10 m classification built from Sentinel-2 data. Its resolution allowed cultivated land, built-up ground, bare soil and open water to be distinguished, which in turn informed the interpretation of surface hydrology and recharge conditions. 3.1.6 Rainfall data Mean annual rainfall was obtained from the PERSIANN-CCS product (Precipitation Estimation from Remotely Sensed Information using Artificial Neural Networks–Cloud Classification System; Hong et al., 2004), downloaded from the CHRS Data Portal (Nguyen et al., 2019) for [2020–2024]. PERSIANN-CCS estimates rainfall from geostationary infrared imagery by classifying cloud patches according to cloud-top temperature, areal extent and texture, and it is distributed on a regular 0.04° grid (≈4.4 km). Each cell therefore covers about 18 km2, and the layer resolves the regional precipitation gradient across the catchment but not local variation. The grid was resampled to the common 30 m cell and clipped to the catchment outline (Section 3.3); resampling changes the cell size but adds no spatial detail. Every raster was reprojected to UTM Zone 37 N on the WGS-84 datum so that the layers registered exactly. Taken together, the radar, multispectral, and elevation products supply the hydrogeological and structural basis required for groundwater prospecting in the catchment. 3.2 Aeromagnetic data The magnetic dataset used here forms part of the national survey flown for the Saudi Arabian Ministry of Energy by the Bureau de Recherches Géologiques et Minières (BRGM) between 1960 and 1989. Readings were taken along N50°E flight lines separated by about 210 m at a mean terrain clearance of roughly 100 m. The gridded field was processed in the wavenumber domain (Spector and Grant, 1970), and the wavenumber domain was applied to the gridded field, followed by reduction to the pole (RTP), which repositions anomalies over their sources and reduces the effects of the Earth’s inclined magnetic field and facilitates the spatial correlation of magnetic anomalies with their sources. The RTP grid gives a clearer picture of the subsurface magnetic pattern and forms the basis of the structural interpretation presented below. Magnetic processing proceeded along two complementary lines. The first addressed structural interpretation by means of the advanced MEF edge filter. Upward continuation was applied to the RTP grid (Blakely, 1996) to attenuate shallow, short-wavelength content and enhance the regional field. Suppressing high-frequency noise in this way makes the broader patterns produced by deeper sources easier to recognize, and the resulting grids portray the distribution of susceptibility contrasts at successive levels, which is useful for isolating structures relevant to groundwater occurrence. Lateral variation in source depth was examined in the same way. Each successive continuation level removes more of the shallow response and emphasizes deeper sources, producing a depth-dependent view of the magnetic architecture. Continuation heights of 1, 2, and 3 km were used, emphasizing sources deeper than approximately 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5km, respectively (Jacobsen, 1987; Nagy and Eldosouky, 2026). The continued grids improve the visibility of deep-seated anomalies and strengthen the structural interpretation; in hydrogeological work, they are particularly helpful for identifying the dominant structural directions and the faults likely to govern flow. Comparable processing sequences have proved effective in earlier groundwater investigations (Araffa et al., 2020; El-Badrawy et al., 2024; 2026). The second line of processing estimated depth to magnetic basement by Source Parameter Imaging (SPI) after Smith et al. (1998); throughout this paper, SPI denotes the magnetic depth estimator, the terrain stream power index being written SPWI to avoid ambiguity. The purpose was to identify ground underlain by comparatively thick sediment, where storage conditions should be most favourable. 3.2.1 High-precision edge detection Locating the margins of magnetic sources is central to magnetic interpretation because those margins mark subsurface geological discontinuities. Numerous edge-enhancement operators have been proposed and applied to improve the resolution of faults, lithological contacts and dyke-like bodies in magnetic grids ( Pham et al., 2022 ; Ibraheem et al., 2019 ; 2022 ; 2023 ). Geologically, such edges usually coincide with boundaries between rock units of differing physical properties, susceptibility above all ( Jorge et al., 2023 ). Because recent high-resolution filters have made structural mapping from magnetic data considerably more dependable, an advanced edge detector was applied to the present grid in order to place source boundaries more accurately and to provide a firmer basis for geological interpretation: Modified Elliott function of total horizontal gradient (MEF) Alvandi et al. (2024) introduced the MEF filter, which was used here to trace the lateral limits of potential-field sources situated at a range of depths. The operator steepens magnetic gradients and raises the effective resolution of the grid, so that source margins and the structures associated with them stand out more distinctly and faults, contacts and other discontinuities can be interpreted with greater confidence. The Structural Complexity (SC) algorithm of Holden et al. (2010) is especially effective at picking out places where several structures converge or lie close together — settings that can control recharge, storage and flow and that therefore make attractive targets. CET analysis works from variations in image texture to resolve lithological contacts, magnetic edges and other geological boundaries. Processing begins with a moving-window standard deviation of the magnetic grid, which captures local variability. Phase symmetry is then computed to accentuate ridge-like responses, these are isolated by amplitude thresholding, and skeletonization reduces each ridge to a single central line. Peaks in the phase-symmetry image are finally vectorized into lineaments. The lineaments so obtained generally correspond to faults, lithological contacts and dykes; interpreting them clarifies the geological framework at regional as well as local scale and helps identify structurally favourable ground for groundwater exploration (Holden et al., 2012; Hasan et al., 2024). 3.2.3 Depth estimation method Depth to magnetic basement was estimated by Source Parameter Imaging. SPI derives source depths automatically from processed magnetic grids and returns a continuous depth image that can be read as a picture of subsurface structure (Smith et al., 1998). It combines the analytic signal with the second vertical derivative, which constrains source geometry and supports construction of a geologically plausible model. The SPI response of two is largely independent of source geometry, whereas it becomes considerably more geometry-dependent for three-dimensional sources (Li and Pilkington, 2016). One practical advantage of SPI is that reduction to the pole is not required, because the depth solutions are largely insensitive to magnetic inclination and declination. However, the results are easier to interpret where the regional geology is reasonably well known (Thurston and Smith, 1997). A step-like source is normally assumed, following the formulation of Blakely and Simpson (1986) as later implemented by Thurston and Smith (1997). Here SPI served to constrain the base of the deeper aquifer and to outline ground of greater sediment thickness, which should offer enhanced storage. 3.3 Integration of the thematic layers and groundwater potential modelling AHP was used instead of supervised ML because the available well inventory (64 wells) is small, clustered along the wadi channels and presence-only. These properties would introduce spatial autocorrelation and require arbitrary pseudo-absences. AHP also provides transparent, reproducible weights and keeps the well data independent for validation. Every layer produced in Sections 3.1, 3.2 was reprojected to UTM Zone 37 N (WGS-84), resampled to a uniform 30 m cell and clipped to the catchment outline so that the stack was geometrically consistent. Continuous layers were then reclassified into ordinal classes. The highest ranks were assigned to conditions that promote infiltration, storage, and hydraulic continuity: gentle slopes, low relief, dense lineament networks, permeable Quaternary lithologies, high soil moisture, and a weak magnetic response indicative of thicker sedimentary cover. Steep gradients, dense drainage developed on impermeable substrates and shallow magnetic basement were ranked lowest. Relative weights for the nine layers were obtained from a pairwise comparison matrix constructed after Saaty (1980) and normalized so that the weights sum to 100; the adopted values are listed in Table 2, and the full matrix with its consistency statistics in Table 3. Weights were taken as the normalized principal eigenvector of the matrix, and consistency was tested with CI = (λmax − n)/(n − 1) and CR = CI/RI, where n = 9 and RI = 1.45 is the random index for a nine-criterion matrix (Saaty, 1980). The matrix gives λmax = 9.053, CI = 0.0066 and CR = 0.0045, well below the 0.10 threshold, so the pairwise judgments are consistent. The judgments follow those applied to the same nine criteria by El-Badrawy et al. (2026), which allows the two studies to be compared directly (Section 5.3). TWI, SPWI and TRI (Section 3.1.3) were not entered as separate criteria. They are computed from the same DEM as the slope and drainage-density layers: TWI and SPWI are explicit functions of the local slope (tan β) and of the upslope contributing area (α), which is taken from the flow-accumulation grid that also defines the channel network, and TRI measures local elevation contrast, which slope already expresses. Entering them would count the same terrain information several times and increase the combined weight of topography relative to the rainfall, lithological and magnetic criteria, contrary to the AHP assumption that criteria are independent. These indices were therefore used to interpret runoff and infiltration behavior (Section 4.1.5) and not as weighted layers. The ranked layers were finally combined by weighted overlay in ArcGIS 10.8 to yield the groundwater potential index. The ROC curve traces true-positive against false-positive rates over the full range of decision thresholds, and the area beneath it (AUC) expresses the probability that the model ranks a randomly selected productive site above a randomly selected unproductive one. An AUC of 0.5 indicates no predictive skill; values of 0.7–0.8 are generally considered acceptable, 0.8–0.9 excellent, and above 0.9 outstanding. The catchment well inventory was used as the test dataset, and a confidence envelope was computed around the mean curve to show the uncertainty associated with the limited sample size. No field-confirmed dry or unproductive wells were available, so the map was evaluated with a presence–background design in which the negative class consisted of pseudo-absence points rather than verified groundwater absences. The 64 wells formed the positive class. An equal number of pseudo-absence points (64) was sampled at random, without replacement, from valid cells of the 30 m modelling grid in which all conditioning factors had data, excluding cells within two pixels (≈60 m) of any known well; a fixed random seed (42) was used for reproducibility. The continuous AHP groundwater potential index was extracted at all 128 points for the ROC analysis. Because the AHP weights were fixed before validation, no model was fitted to these points, and the pseudo-absences affect only the AUC estimate and not the map. The pseudo-absences are presumed-absence (background) locations; some may fall on productive ground that has not been drilled, which lowers the attainable AUC, so the reported value is conservative. The ROC analysis evaluates the AHP index directly, and no machine-learning model was involved. Figure 2 summarizes the complete workflow. Aspect governs incident solar radiation and hence evapotranspiration, soil moisture and vegetation cover, and therefore acts indirectly on recharge. Slopes facing north and north-east receive less insolation and lose less water to evaporation than south-facing slopes, so they tend to retain more soil moisture. Contours extracted from the DEM (Figure 5f) add further detail on relief. Close spacing in the uplands signals steep gradients and rugged ground, whereas the wide spacing of the western plains reflects subdued relief. The contour pattern outlines the principal drainage divides, valley systems and closed depressions, and so clarifies regional drainage organisation and surface-water routing. 4.1.4 Hydro-environmental controls on recharge Rainfall, soil moisture, the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) and land use/land cover (LU/LC) together describe the hydro-environmental setting that governs recharge and occurrence within the catchment (Figure 6). Each layer represents a different component of the system atmospheric input, near-surface moisture, vegetation response and surface character so that read jointly they provide a sound basis for judging how groundwater potential varies in space. Higher totals are concentrated in the west and south-west, while much of the centre and north-east receives appreciably less. Rainfall, therefore, can contribute unevenly to recharge. The wetter sectors may sustain stronger episodic replenishment wherever topography, lithology, and structure allow water to enter the subsurface, whereas the drier sectors can be expected to contribute comparatively little directly. The block pattern in Figure 6a reflects the native 0.04° cells of the rainfall product (Section 3.1.6), not rainfall variation at that scale. The short east–west rows of high values near 22°14′N and 22°03′N follow the grid rows rather than the relief, and they are therefore interpreted as artifacts of the gridded product rather than real rainfall features. The rainfall layer is accordingly read only as a regional gradient. Where these rows cross the catchment, they raise the rainfall rank of the affected cells, and the potential classes there should be interpreted accordingly. 4.1.4.2 Soil-moisture distribution Soil moisture (Figure 6b) is strongly heterogeneous, spanning approximately −0.316 to 0.265, with alternating high and low zones that are most obvious in the central and southern parts of the catchment. Elevated values identify ground where water persists near the surface for longer, which may reflect the joint influence of rainfall, surface accumulation, soil texture, vegetation, and local relief; low values generally correspond to drier, more exposed terrain. Hydrogeologically, the wetter patches may indicate enhanced infiltration or localized ponding; however, soil moisture alone is not evidence of shallow groundwater. 4.1.4.3 Normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) NDVI ranges from about −0.039 to 0.427 (Figure 6c), consistent with generally sparse vegetation. The higher values are confined to discrete patches and narrow linear tracts, mainly in the south and south-west, while most of the catchment returns low values. This pattern follows from the arid climate and the restricted plant cover it permits. Locally elevated NDVI may indicate greater surface-water availability, better moisture retention, more favourable soils or vegetation supported by shallow groundwater, so the index serves as a useful indirect guide to moisture availability alongside the soil-moisture and rainfall layers. 4.1.4.4 Land use/land cover Land cover (Figure 6d) is dominated by a small number of classes from the Esri 10 m scheme: water (class 1), trees (2), flooded vegetation (4), crops (5), built area (7), bare ground (8) and rangeland (11). Rangeland is much the most extensive, covering the centre, north and east of the catchment, whereas bare ground prevails in the west and south-west over the Quaternary alluvial plain. Built-up ground forms narrow, broken strips along the main channels, and water, trees, flooded vegetation and crops appear only as scattered patches, chiefly in the lower reaches. The pattern matters hydrologically because cover type modulates infiltration, runoff, evapotranspiration and recharge: permeable, sparsely developed surfaces admit water where rainfall or runoff is sufficient, sealed surfaces divert it to runoff, and vegetated or irrigated land alters the balance through additional water use. 4.1.5 DEM-derived hydrological and terrain attributes Six additional raster layers were derived from the DEM to characterize the routing, concentration and retention of surface water across the catchment (Figure 7). Together they translate the static topography described in Section 4.1.3 into a process-oriented description of the surface hydrological system. The workflow adopted here was designed to sharpen the appraisal of the resource and to delimit prospective ground with greater confidence. Aeromagnetic data were used first to define the basement configuration and to locate concealed fault systems, since these elements establish the hydrogeological framework and determine how far geological structure governs occurrence, storage and movement. Figure 8 shows the total magnetic intensity (TMI) field (Figure 8a) together with its reduced-to-the-pole equivalent (Figure 8b) for Wadi Marwani. TMI values lie between roughly −476 and 533 nT. Broad, subdued anomalies characterise the western sector, whereas the centre and east return a more heterogeneous, higher-amplitude field marked by short-wavelength features and alternating highs and lows, most conspicuously over exposed Precambrian basement and Tertiary volcanic rocks. Reduction to the pole recentres the anomalies over their sources and simplifies their geometry; amplitudes on the RTP grid span approximately −1222 to 774 nT. A belt of high-amplitude response occupies the western to south-western part of the catchment, low-amplitude response is concentrated in the south-east, and the central and northern sectors carry densely packed intermediate to high anomalies with complex lateral variation. Comparison with the geological map (Figure 1) shows that the magnetically variable domains follow the Precambrian basement and Tertiary volcanic outcrops, while the quiet domains coincide largely with the Quaternary alluvial plain. FIGURE 8 (a) TMI and (b) RTP fields of the study area. Such responses are expected where susceptibility contrasts between crystalline basement and weakly magnetic cover dominate the magnetic field (El-Badrawy et al., 2023; 2026). Strong anomalies in the west and centre are most readily explained by shallow or structurally raised basement together with highly magnetised volcanic units, whereas the subdued signal along the wadi axis and across the western alluvial plain points to a thicker, less magnetic sedimentary section. Equivalent links between anomaly amplitude, basement relief, and sediment thickness have been reported from other basement-floored dryland basins, in which magnetically quiet ground marks the deeper sedimentary pockets that commonly host the better aquifers (El-Badrawy et al., 2026; Elawadi et al., 2013; Ranganai et al., 2017). Read hydrogeologically, the opposition between magnetically quiet ground along the trunk drainage and stronger anomalies over the flanking basement implies a basin geometry in which a comparatively thick sedimentary package underlies the central wadi corridor. Such a configuration favours a laterally continuous Quaternary aquifer, provided recharge and hydraulic connectivity are adequate. The structurally raised, strongly magnetic blocks flanking the wadi may conversely act as flow barriers or lateral limits, although they can also feed the system where fracture networks reach the surface and concentrate infiltration (Elawadi et al., 2013; Araffa et al., 2020). Upward-continued RTP grids at 1, 2 and 3 km are shown in Figure 9; each successive level removes more of the shallow contribution and emphasizes broader, deeper-seated patterns. Adopting the customary approximation that continuation to a height z favours sources at about z/2 (Jacobsen, 1987; Nagy et al., 2026), the levels used here correspond to effective source depths of roughly 0.5–1.5 km. The 1 km grid (Figure 9a) is still strongly variable, carrying numerous short-wavelength highs and lows through the center and east of the catchment. By 2 km (Figure 9b) most of the finer detail has been suppressed and smoother, more continuous magnetic domains prevail, and at 3 km (Figure 9c) the field is thoroughly regionalized, broad highs and lows having replaced the earlier small-scale texture. One spatial arrangement recurs at all three levels: a magnetically quiet western to south-western sector, a structurally complex central belt, and a comparatively heterogeneous eastern sector. Because the central and eastern anomalies remain distinct as continuation height increases while localized shallow features fade, the most persistent sources are inferred to lie beneath those sectors. In contrast, the West is characterized by weaker and laterally smoother responses. FIGURE 9 Upward-continued RTP fields at (a) 1 km, (b) 2 km and (c) 3 km across the study area. In groundwater terms, these deeper magnetic patterns are consistent with a structurally controlled basin in which shallow signals are increasingly masked by regional basement architecture at greater continuation depths. The continuation sequence, therefore, provides a first-order view of source depth distribution. The loss of short-wavelength anomalies from 1 to 3 km suggests that many of the shallow magnetic contrasts are confined to near-surface lithologic variations, whereas the persistent regional anomalies reflect deeper basement blocks and larger structural domains. This supports the interpretation that the basin contains deeper magnetic sources beneath the central and eastern sectors and helps identify structurally controlled zones that may host thicker sedimentary accumulation and groundwater storage. Interpretation based only on RTP and upward-continued grids remains subjective and can yield ambiguous or non-unique models (Alvandi et al., 2025), so further signal processing is needed. Edge detectors are valuable in this respect because they sharpen anomaly margins and make source boundaries, faults, fractures, lithological contacts, and other discontinuities easier to trace. Such features matter in both mineral and groundwater work, since they largely control how subsurface fluids move and where they collect (Eldosouky et al., 2024). The MEF operator was accordingly applied to the RTP grid and to each upward-continued level. Relative to conventional enhancement techniques, it returns higher-resolution output and resolves shallow as well as deep magnetic boundaries more accurately, including subtle features that standard RTP images do not reveal (Hasan et al., 2024). Its response to weak or concealed contrasts is particularly useful because such signals may correspond to fault zones, fracture systems, or changes in basement configuration that condition flow, storage, and recharge. Comparable filters have already proved effective at extracting significant structures from complex magnetic data and at improving the recognition of features linked to groundwater potential (Dadi et al., 2024). That these orientations survive from the reduced-to-the-pole grid through to the 3 km continuation indicates basement-scale discontinuities rather than near-surface noise. The same three directions emerge independently from the Sentinel-1 surface lineament analysis (Figure 4); agreement between two methodologically distinct datasets furnishes an internal consistency check that single-technique studies rarely afford. This hierarchy accords with the tectonic development of the Arabian Shield. Neoproterozoic terrane accretion imposed a pervasive N–S to NNE–SSW grain, subsequently overprinted by NE–SW and NW–SE shear and fault systems during late Pan-African transpression and transtension (Nehlig et al., 2002; Johnson and Woldehaimanot, 2003). Cenozoic rifting of the Red Sea–Gulf of Aden system exploited that inherited anisotropy, border faults striking N–S to NNW–SSE and deformation being transferred onto NE–SW and NW–SE accommodation zones along the continental margin (Younes and McClay, 2002; Bosworth et al., 2005). The fracture corridors that govern secondary permeability in the Wadi Marwani basement are therefore long-lived and repeatedly reactivated rather than isolated features, which raises the probability that they are hydraulically connected at depth. 5.2 Basement architecture, sediment thickness and aquifer geometry On the reduced-to-the-pole grid (Figure 8) the catchment separates into magnetically variable domains that follow exposed Precambrian basement and Tertiary volcanic rocks, and magnetically quiet domains that follow the Quaternary alluvial plain. Since the anomalies here are controlled almost entirely by the susceptibility contrast between crystalline basement and essentially non-magnetic cover, the quiet domains are best read as thicker sedimentary fill above a structurally depressed basement (Elawadi et al., 2013; Araffa et al., 2020; El-Badrawy et al., 2024; 2026). The SPI solutions (Figure 13b) render this explicit, resolving an elongate depression that follows the central wadi axis and widens westwards towards the coastal plain. The hydrogeological implication is that storage capacity and structural permeability are spatially decoupled but locally superimposed. Basement lows supply the accommodation space for a laterally continuous alluvial aquifer, whereas the intersecting fault and fracture corridors supply the vertical and lateral hydraulic connectivity needed to recharge and drain it. Where the two coincide along the central Wadi Marwani corridor and in the structurally complex western sector both conditions for a productive aquifer are satisfied simultaneously. Conversely, the shallow-basement domains of the eastern and north-eastern margins are expected to host thin, compartmentalised aquifers that are sensitive to localised drawdown, a behaviour also reported from comparable basement terrains (Ranganai et al., 2017). 5.3 Value of integrating surface and subsurface predictors Models built only from surface proxies carry the implicit assumption that recharge conditions determine productivity. In basement-controlled arid catchments that assumption holds only in part, because aquifer thickness is fixed by basement relief, which no optical or radar sensor can detect. The aeromagnetic layers therefore add information orthogonal to the remote-sensing predictors rather than duplicating them, and integrated workflows of this kind have consistently outperformed surface-only models in dryland settings (El-Badrawy et al., 2024; 2026). The area under the ROC curve obtained here (AUC = 0.789) indicates acceptable to good discrimination between productive and unproductive ground, but it is lower than the values reported for some comparable integrated studies, for example, AUC = 0.934 for the Bahariya Oasis (El-Badrawy et al., 2026). Three factors may account for this difference. First, the validation wells in Wadi Marwani are strongly clustered along the wadi channels (Figure 14), so the negative class is poorly sampled, and the effective contrast the model is asked to resolve is a within-alluvium contrast rather than an alluvium-versus-plateau contrast. Second, the Wadi Marwani aquifer is a shallow, fracture-influenced alluvial system in which yield varies over short distances, whereas the Bahariya system is a thick, laterally continuous sandstone aquifer whose productivity is more predictable from regional predictors. Third, well presence records where water has been sought and found, not where the resource is greatest, so it is an imperfect proxy for potential. The 95% confidence band shown in Figure 15 makes the resulting uncertainty explicit and should be interpreted alongside the point estimate. 5.4 Implications for groundwater management The results provide a spatially explicit basis for three management actions. For well siting, the very-high and high potential classes along the central wadi axis and the western alluvial plain define first-order drilling targets, with priority given to sites where high lineament density coincides with the deeper SPI solutions. For managed recharge, the same structural corridors identify locations where recharge dams, spreading basins and check structures could convert episodic flash floodwater into aquifer storage rather than allowing it to discharge to the Red Sea; flash-flood generation in comparable western Saudi catchments is well documented (Salih and Hassablla, 2024). For monitoring, the compartmentalized eastern sector warrants closer observation, well spacing, because thin, structurally isolated aquifer blocks respond rapidly to abstraction and are the most vulnerable to over-exploitation and to the quality deterioration already reported for the basin (Al-Ahmadi and El-Fiky, 2009; Alqarawy, 2023; El Osta et al., 2026). 5.5 Limitations and future work Several limitations constrain the interpretation. Potential-field inversion is inherently non-unique, and the SPI depths are not constrained by borehole data within the basin. They should therefore be interpreted as relative basement relief rather than absolute depths. The aeromagnetic data were acquired during a national survey flown between 1960 and 1989, and the line spacing limits the resolution of short-wavelength structures. The thematic layers are proxies for, not measurements of, recharge and storage; NDVI and soil moisture in particular are influenced by irrigation return flow, and the resulting model predicts favourability rather than yield, water quality or sustainable abstraction. Validation is positional only: no transmissivity, specific-capacity or water-level data were available for quantitative correlation with the potential index. Future work should therefore (i) acquire geoelectrical or transient electromagnetic profiles along the interpreted basement lows to calibrate the SPI depths and resolve the saturated thickness, (ii) incorporate measured well yields and hydrochemical data so that favourability can be related to productivity and quality, (iii) compare the AHP model with ensemble ML classifiers (e.g., random forest and gradient boosting) using spatially stratified cross-validation, and quantify predictor contributions with SHAP, and (iv) evaluate specific managed-aquifer-recharge sites within the highest-priority corridors. 6 Conclusion Multi-source remote sensing, GIS and aeromagnetic data were combined to characterize the structural framework and the groundwater potential of the Wadi Marwani catchment, western Saudi Arabia. The main findings are as follows. SVM classification of Sentinel-2 imagery separated Precambrian basement, Tertiary volcanic–sedimentary units, and Quaternary alluvium. The latter are interpreted as the principal shallow aquifer beneath the wadi floor and the western plain, based on their distribution and supporting geological and hydrogeological evidence. Lineaments obtained from Sentinel-1C SAR and magnetic lineaments obtained by MEF edge detection and CET grid analysis share a dominant N–S to NNE–SSW grain with subordinate NW–SE and NE–SW sets. Because these directions survive to the 3 km continuation level, they represent basement-scale discontinuities inherited from Pan-African accretion and reactivated during Red Sea rifting. Source Parameter Imaging resolves an elongate basement depression along the central wadi axis that broadens westwards and coincides with magnetically subdued responses, high lineament density and the thickest Quaternary cover the combination that maximizes storage capacity and hydraulic connectivity simultaneously. The integrated potential map divides the catchment into five classes. The very-high and high classes form continuous corridors along the trunk channels and the western alluvial plain and enclose most of the existing wells. ROC validation gives AUC = 0.789, corresponding to acceptable-to-good predictive performance. Basement architecture derived from aeromagnetic data therefore supplies predictive information that surface proxies cannot, and the workflow can be transferred to other arid, basement-floored catchments along the Red Sea margin. Practically, the map supports targeted drilling, siting of floodwater-harvesting and recharge structures, and prioritized monitoring of the structurally compartmentalised eastern sector. Verification by geoelectrical or transient electromagnetic surveying and by measured well yields is recommended before development decisions are taken.
