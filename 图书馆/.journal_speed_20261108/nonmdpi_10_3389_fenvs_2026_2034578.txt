IFPEN, Rueil-Malmaison, France See more Article metrics View details Editorial on the Research Topic Gas hydrates and their implications for global changes: climate, environment, and energy Gas hydrates constitute an important component of the Earth’s shallow geological carbon reservoir. Predominantly composed of methane trapped within frozen water cages, gas hydrates occur in marine sediments and permafrost environments where appropriate pressure and temperature conditions prevail. Their significance, however, extends beyond the amount of carbon stored within their crystalline structure. Gas hydrates are highly dynamic components of coupled geological, hydrological and biogeochemical systems in which methane is generated, transported, trapped, recycled and released to the seafloor and potentially to the atmosphere. Understanding these processes is increasingly important in the context of climate-driven environmental changes, geohazards related to submarine sediment stability, Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) activities, and the potential utilization of hydrate-bearing sediments as an energy resource. The Research Topic ‘Gas Hydrates and their Implications for Global Changes: Climate, Environment, and Energy’ brings together complementary approaches for investigating hydrate-bearing complex geological systems (Figure 1). A central objective is to connect observations, experiments and numerical approaches across spatial and temporal scales (pore-to basin-scale), from sediment properties and methane transport to regional hydrate distribution and environmental forcing. The complexity and rich internal dynamics of gas hydrate systems emerges from biogeochemical and mechanical interactions among both shallow microbial and deep-sourced thermogenic methane generation rates, migration efficiency, kinetically-controlled hydrate formation and dissociation, sediment properties, hosting geological structures, and external forcing affecting their thermodynamic stability field. FIGURE 1 An overview of interdisciplinary approaches to study natural gas hydrate systems: from mechanistic understanding of permafrost-hosted hydrates and methane transport to specialized pressure-holding cores allowing for in-situ measurements of hydrate system properties presented in the Research Topic ‘Gas Hydrates and their Implications for Global Changes: Climate, Environment, and Energy’. This dynamic perspective is exemplified by Burton and Seol, who synthesize current understanding of the gas hydrate recycling process occurring in natural systems. Increased rates of sedimentation shift the local thermodynamic equilibrium and result in the migration of the base of the gas hydrate stability zone (BGHSZ) upwards with respect to the sediment column. This process causes previously formed hydrate to dissociate, releasing methane that migrates upward and being subsequently reincorporated into newly formed hydrate layers. Repeated recycling can therefore redistribute methane and progressively enrich hydrate accumulations close to the BGHSZ. Burton and Seol show that recycling can be driven by processes operating over very different spatial and temporal scales, including sediment burial, sea-level change, tectonic uplift and salt diapirism. Understanding hydrate accumulation therefore requires linking pore-scale formation and dissociation processes with the geological evolution of entire sedimentary systems. The importance of the regional geological framework is demonstrated by Iemelianov et al. for the Black Sea gas hydrate system. Integrating geological, geophysical, geomorphological and oceanographic information within a GIS-based approach, the authors assess spatial variability in gas hydrate susceptibility on a basin scale. Their analysis emphasizes the role of geological structures, e.g., faults, as potential migration pathways, together with depositional and geomorphological conditions controlling hydrate occurrence in the region. This study illustrates why broad-scale assessments require consideration of methane transport and geological architecture in addition to the pressure-temperature limits of hydrate stability. At a much smaller spatial scale, Brunet et al. identify evidence for relic gas hydrate-filled fractures at unusually shallow sediment depths near the sulfate-methane transition zone (SMTZ) at the Hikurangi subduction margin. Their observations demonstrate that hydrate formation can interact with methane transport and early diagenetic processes close to the seafloor. The authors suggest that fracture-hosted hydrate may also respond differently to environmental perturbations than conventional pore-filling hydrate and provide evidence for localized pathways through which methane can be redistributed within shallow sediments of the Hikurangi margin. In a practical sense, reliable characterization of hydrate-bearing sediments requires preservation of their in-situ physical state. Gao et al. review advances in pressure-holding core transfer and testing technologies developed for natural gas hydrate reservoirs. Conventional recovery can induce depressurization, hydrate dissociation and alteration of sediment structure, potentially obscuring the properties that control natural hydrate-system behavior. The authors argue that pressure-preserving technologies provide an essential bridge between field observations and laboratory characterization. Moreover, increasing automation, integrated measurements, and improved pressure-core analytical capabilities should further constrain the physical parameters required for understanding and modeling hydrate reservoirs. Furthermore, this Research Topic extends from marine environments to the cryosphere through the experimental study of Shang et al. Their investigation of gas breakthrough in progressively thawing permafrost demonstrates threshold-dependent behavior controlled by temperature-dependent unfrozen water content, tensile resistance and confinement. According to the authors, gas breakthrough becomes increasingly favorable during thawing, providing experimental constraints on conditions under which methane can migrate through degrading permafrost, essential to improve the numerical models of permafrost-associated gas hydrate systems. These results highlight an important distinction between hydrate destabilization or gas generation and eventual methane release suggesting that environmental forcing does not necessarily translate directly into atmospheric methane emissions because transport is regulated by the evolving physical properties of the host sediment. Together, these contributions highlight natural gas hydrate systems not as static deposits bounded by a phase-equilibrium surface, but as highly dynamic components in a variety of geological settings. Environmental and geological forcing constantly modifies gas hydrate stability and sediment properties; the resulting system response controls methane, CO2, and higher hydrocarbon storage, recycling, transport, and the potential release to the ocean or the atmosphere in case of shallow systems. Processes operating from individual pores and fractures to sedimentary basins must therefore be considered together when evaluating the response of hydrate systems to the ongoing and future rates of the climate change. Future progress in the field will depend on close integration of geophysical and geochemical observations, pressure-preserved sampling, laboratory experiments, and process-based numerical models. Such integration is required to distinguish transient responses from long-term carbon-cycle feedbacks, quantify methane inventories and fluxes, and evaluate geohazards. The studies assembled in this Research Topic provide complementary steps toward this goal and demonstrate that understanding gas hydrates under global change requires a genuinely interdisciplinary and multiscale view of the coupled gas-hydrate-sediment system.
