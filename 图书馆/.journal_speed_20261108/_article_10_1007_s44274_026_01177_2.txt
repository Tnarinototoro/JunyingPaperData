Conventional heat risk assessments often rely on temperature thresholds or surface heat mapping, but these approaches may overlook the biological sensitivity, socio-spatial inequality, urban form, health-system capacity, and governance conditions that determine who is harmed, where, and why. This paper presents a framework-oriented integrative synthesis that develops the Urban Heat–Exposure–Health (UHEH) axis as a conceptual and translational structure for organizing heat-health risk in rapidly urbanizing cities. Drawing on evidence from urban climate science, environmental epidemiology, public health, spatial analytics, heat vulnerability research, and urban governance, the framework links urban form, thermal exposure, demographic sensitivity, social vulnerability, health outcomes, and institutional response pathways. Rather than presenting a fully implemented predictive model or empirical validation study, the paper clarifies the data streams, analytical relationships, equity indicators, and validation requirements needed for future operational heat-health modeling. The UHEH framework identifies how satellite, in situ, and modeled thermal data can be combined with socio-spatial vulnerability indicators, urban morphology metrics, and health outcome proxies such as emergency department visits, hospital admissions, ambulance calls, and mortality records. It also maps key mechanistic pathways, including cardiovascular strain, dehydration, renal stress, air-pollution interactions, sleep disruption, and cumulative exposure. However, the framework is not presented as a validated predictive model; rather, it provides a structured basis for future empirical testing, local calibration, comparison with existing heat vulnerability approaches, and validation against observed health outcomes. By integrating exposure, vulnerability, biological response, and governance into a single analytical pathway, the framework supports the design of heat-health early warning systems, real-time dashboards, policy triggers, targeted cooling interventions, and equity-centered adaptation strategies. The paper contributes a structured foundation for future empirical testing, model calibration, cross-city comparison, and decision-support development aimed at reducing heat-related morbidity and mortality in rapidly urbanizing cities. Heat Stress Urban Policy Public Health Environmental Health Urban Ecology Housing and Built Environments 1 Introduction The interplay between rapid urbanization and climate warming exacerbates heat exposure risks, posing significant public health challenges. Urban areas exhibit a marked increase in ambient temperatures due to the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect, driven by the extensive conversion of natural landscapes into impervious surfaces. This phenomenon not only increases local temperatures but also amplifies the vulnerability of urban dwellers to heat-related health issues, especially among vulnerable populations such as the elderly and those suffering from chronic illnesses (Peng et al., 2011) [ 1 , 2 , 3 ]. Projections indicate that by 2050, approximately 68% of the world’s population will reside in urban areas, further straining urban health infrastructure and increasing susceptibility to extreme heat [ 2 ]. Traditional reliance on simple temperature metrics or UHI mapping studies often fails to capture the multifaceted nature of heat-related health risks. Such approaches typically overlook critical socioeconomic variables that mediate vulnerability to heat exposure, including poverty, access to healthcare, and community resilience. Studies indicate that health risks in urban areas are disproportionately greater among marginalized groups due to factors including limited access to adaptive resources like air conditioning and inadequate housing designs [ 1 , 4 ]. Moreover, models that predict future health impacts solely from temperature measurements may not account for behavioral adaptations, changes in morbidity rates, and technological advancements in public health response [ 5 , 6 , 7 ]. Reframing heat exposure as a socio-environmental health hazard highlights the intersection of environmental factors, health outcomes, and governance issues. Research consistently shows that urban residents, particularly lower-income individuals, experience heightened vulnerability to heat-related mortality due to insufficient coping mechanisms and healthcare access [ 8 , 9 , 10 ]. Vulnerable populations often reside in areas with higher UHI intensities, which increase health risks. Thus, addressing heat-related health impacts necessitates a multifaceted approach that integrates socio-economic, geographical, and environmental data to inform public health interventions effectively [ 8 , 11 , 12 ]. Transitioning from conventional hazard mapping to health risk intelligence involves using advanced data analytics and modeling to identify and predict areas at high risk of heat exposure. Modern studies employ techniques that integrate demographic statistics, socioeconomic variables, and environmental data (e.g., green space availability) to create risk maps that inform targeted intervention strategies [ 5 , 13 ]. Such methodologies underscore the importance of understanding spatial and temporal variations in heat exposure and their relationships to public health outcomes. For instance, integrating real-time climate data and health records can enhance early warning systems and improve response efforts during extreme heat events, potentially reducing morbidity and mortality rates associated with heat [ 14 , 15 ]. Climate justice is a critical consideration, particularly regarding the vulnerability experienced in the Global South, where rapid urbanization compounds the impacts of climate change ([ 16 ], [ 17 ]). Communities in these regions often prioritize development without adequate consideration of climate resilience, resulting in fragmented urban policies that fail to adequately address heat-related health risks [ 9 , 15 ]. Efforts to improve climate adaptation must address both ecological and socio-economic vulnerabilities by ensuring equitable resource distribution and prioritizing interventions for the most affected populations. Studies emphasize that improving urban infrastructure to enhance cooling accessibility and resilience planning is vital for safeguarding public health in these burgeoning urban centers [ 18 , 19 ]. This paper develops a framework-oriented integrative synthesis of the interactions among urban heat, climate change, social vulnerability, biological sensitivity, and public health governance in rapidly urbanizing cities. It does not present original empirical analysis, primary data collection, model implementation, or empirical validation. Rather, it clarifies the data streams, analytical relationships, equity dimensions, and governance pathways required for future operational heat-health risk prediction. The proposed Urban Heat–Exposure–Health (UHEH) framework builds on, rather than replaces, established work on urban heat vulnerability, climate-health risk, and socio-spatial exposure assessment. Previous studies have shown that heat vulnerability is shaped by interactions among exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity, and that urban heat risk is unevenly distributed across neighborhoods and social groups [ 13 , 20 , 21 ]. Recent assessments also emphasize the need for heat- and place-specific vulnerability analysis, as generalized vulnerability frameworks may overlook intra-urban thermal variability, population-specific sensitivity, and mismatches between heat exposure and health outcomes [ 22 ]. The novelty of the UHEH axis lies not in claiming that heat, vulnerability, and health are newly connected, but in organizing these established relationships into an operational sequence linking urban form, thermal exposure, social vulnerability, biological health pathways, model-ready data streams, validation requirements, and governance triggers. In this sense, the framework offers an integrative and translational structure for future empirical modeling, decision-support design, and equity-centered heat-health governance in rapidly urbanizing cities. 1.1 Methodological approach and scope of the integrative synthesis This paper adopts a framework-oriented integrative narrative synthesis rather than an empirical study, PRISMA-compliant systematic review, or meta-analysis. It does not present original primary data, statistical estimation, model implementation, or empirical validation of the Urban Heat–Exposure–Health (UHEH) framework. Instead, the review identifies, organizes, and connects major strands of evidence related to urban heat exposure, social vulnerability, biological heat–health pathways, predictive modeling, early warning systems, and urban governance to develop a conceptual structure for future empirical testing and decision-support applications in rapidly urbanizing cities. Because systematic reviews require transparent and reproducible reporting of search strategies, screening procedures, eligibility criteria, and information sources, this manuscript does not claim systematic-review status unless those standards are fully met [ 23 , 24 ]. Literature searches focused on peer-reviewed studies, reviews, and major climate–health assessment papers published primarily between 2000 and 2025. Search terms included combinations of “urban heat island,” “heat vulnerability,” “heat-health risk,” “extreme heat,” “urban climate,” “social vulnerability,” “heat-related morbidity,” “heat-related mortality,” “early warning systems,” “machine learning,” “predictive modeling,” “urban resilience,” and “climate justice.” Searches were conducted across interdisciplinary scholarly platforms commonly used in environmental health, climate science, public health, and sustainability research, including Google Scholar, Scopus, PubMed/Medline, Web of Science, and major publisher databases. Additional studies were identified through backward and forward citation tracing. Sources were selected thematically when they addressed at least one of the following domains: urban thermal exposure and heat-island processes; heat vulnerability and socio-spatial inequality; physiological or epidemiological heat–health mechanisms; heat-related morbidity or mortality; predictive modeling and machine learning; heat-health warning systems; urban planning and green infrastructure; or equity-centered adaptation in rapidly urbanizing settings. Sources focused solely on non-urban heat exposure, lacking a health or vulnerability component, or providing insufficient methodological detail were not prioritized for synthesisThe synthesis proceeded thematically rather than statistically and was organized into six analytical domains: urban thermal environments, biological and social vulnerability, mechanistic heat–health pathways, data and modeling architecture, early warning and decision-support systems, and governance-oriented adaptation. These domains were integrated into the proposed UHEH framework linking urban form, thermal exposure, social vulnerability, biological response, health-system burden, and governance feedback. Although this approach cannot eliminate selection bias, it improves transparency by clarifying how evidence was identified, prioritized, and incorporated into framework development. The UHEH framework should therefore be interpreted as a conceptual and translational structure rather than a validated predictive model. Future studies should evaluate its performance, transferability, and public-health utility using city-specific thermal data, urban morphology metrics, vulnerability indicators, and observed morbidity, hospital admissions, ambulance call-outs, and mortality outcomes. Validation should compare UHEH-based approaches against temperature-only, heat-index-only, exposure-only, and conventional heat-vulnerability-index models before operational implementation in public-health systems. . 2 Urban thermal environments and heat exposure dynamics Urban environments are increasingly vulnerable to heat-related impacts due to rapid urbanization and climate change. Understanding the intricacies of urban thermal environments is crucial for predicting heat-related health risks, particularly in disadvantaged communities that often bear the brunt of these changes. 2.1 Urban heat island processes The Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect refers to the phenomenon where urban areas exhibit significantly higher temperatures than their rural counterparts. This increased temperature is primarily due to human activities and the prevalence of impervious surfaces that absorb and retain heat [ 25 , 26 ]. Factors contributing to the UHI effect include urban density, land-use practices, and historical socio-spatial inequalities, such as “redlining,” which has marginalized certain neighborhoods, leaving them more vulnerable to extreme heat [ 26 , 27 ]. Vulnerable populations, particularly low-income and minority communities, experience heightened heat exposure due to living conditions exacerbated by UHI effects [ 27 , 28 ]. Such socio-economic disparities in heat-related health outcomes demonstrate the intersectionality of environmental and social justice issues within urban heat dynamics [ 25 , 26 ]. 2.2 Land surface temperature vs. air temperature Understanding the differences between land surface temperature (LST) and air temperature (T_a) is crucial for assessing heat exposure risks in urban settings. LST can often exceed air temperature, especially during the day when surfaces heat rapidly [ 29 , 30 ]. Satellite imagery has proven effective in monitoring LST variability and has revealed that urban areas experience significant fluctuations due to differences in land cover and surface materials [ 31 , 32 ]. Research shows that urban areas often have a significantly higher mean LST than surrounding rural areas, particularly during peak heat events. This discrepancy underscores the importance of using both LST and T_a measurements when assessing heat-wave-related health risks [ 30 , 32 ]. Studies indicate a correlation between higher urban temperatures and increased incidence of heat-related illnesses, underscoring the importance of monitoring these temperatures for public health [ 2 , 33 ]. However, the literature also shows an important measurement tension. Land surface temperature and air temperature are both used in urban heat studies, but they do not measure the same exposure pathway. Land surface temperature captures the radiative temperature of urban surfaces, whereas near-surface air temperature reflects the canopy-layer environment more directly experienced by people [ 20 ]. Although land surface temperature and air temperature may show similar spatial patterns, their relationship is influenced by micro-scale site characteristics and weather conditions, which can weaken the direct translation of satellite-derived surface hotspots into human heat exposure [ 20 ]. This limitation suggests that future heat-health models should integrate satellite, in situ, and modeled thermal data rather than relying on a single exposure metric. 2.3 Urban morphology and thermal retention Urban morphology, including building design, density, and land-use configuration, substantially influences urban temperature retention. High-density urban areas with numerous impervious surfaces tend to retain heat more effectively, resulting in prolonged exposure to high temperatures—particularly at night, when cooling is hindered [ 26 , 33 ]. Studies indicate that neighborhoods with greater vegetation can serve as cool islands within the urban context, thereby mitigating the adverse effects of heat [ 25 , 34 ]. Furthermore, the inequitable distribution of green spaces often results in socioeconomic disparities in thermal comfort, as residents of lower-income neighborhoods typically have limited access to these resources [ 28 , 34 ]. The integration of green infrastructure is crucial in urban planning to mitigate thermal retention issues [ 26 , 27 ]. 2.4 Microclimate heterogeneity Urban areas characterized by microclimate heterogeneity exhibit significant variations in temperature and humidity over short distances, often influenced by local land cover [ 34 , 35 ]. This microclimatic complexity means that certain neighborhoods may be more susceptible to heat than others, consequently impacting residents’ health as they navigate these temperature variations [ 27 , 28 , 36 ]. Understanding microclimate variability requires attention to local temperature measurements and modeling to accurately predict health risks and environmental impacts. For example, residential areas with a higher proportion of trees tend to have lower thermal readings and, hence, lower heat exposure risk [ 35 , 37 , 38 ]. 2.5 Seasonal and diurnal heat patterns Seasonal and diurnal variations in heat patterns drastically affect urban heat dynamics. During heat waves, urban environments often exhibit stark contrasts between daytime and nighttime temperatures, which are exacerbated by the structural materials used in urban design that retain heat [ 33 , 38 , 39 ]. Studies illustrate that the frequency of heat waves is increasing due to climate change, leading to greater health risks associated with elevated nighttime temperatures, which hinder recovery from daytime heat stress [ 36 , 40 ]. Consequently, targeted public health initiatives that account for these seasonal trends and diurnal shifts are essential for minimizing health risks during peak heat periods [ 2 , 39 , 40 ]. These processes are often compromised when exposed to elevated temperatures, particularly in urban areas that experience intensified conditions due to the urban heat island effect [ 41 ]. The physiological adaptations include enhanced sweating and cutaneous blood flow, which are vital for dissipating heat. However, as climate change continues to raise temperatures, these adaptations may reach physiological limits, leading to increased health risks such as heat exhaustion and heat strokes more frequently [ 41 ]. Moreover, the body’s natural heat tolerance mechanisms may interact unfavorably with other factors, such as aging and pre-existing health conditions, thereby complicating the picture of human vulnerability under heat stress [ 41 , 42 ]. 3.2 Heat-related morbidity and mortality mechanisms The health impacts of extreme heat are well-documented, indicating a strong correlation between heat events and increased morbidity and mortality, especially among vulnerable populations such as the elderly, those with chronic illnesses, and low-income households [ 43 , 44 ]. The physiological strain imposed by heat increases susceptibility to cardiovascular and respiratory illnesses, with emerging research suggesting that high humidity exacerbates these effects by limiting the body’s ability to cool itself through evaporation [ 45 ]. Notably, climate projections indicate that mortality rates from heat exposure will increase, particularly if proactive public health measures are not implemented, and vulnerable populations lack access to adequate cooling resources [ 44 , 46 ]. 3.3 Demographic sensitivity factors Demographic characteristics play a critical role in an individual’s vulnerability to heat. Factors such as age, socioeconomic status, and pre-existing health conditions significantly contribute to one’s risk profile during heat events. Research indicates that elderly individuals exhibit diminished thermoregulatory efficiency, making them particularly vulnerable to heat-related illnesses [ 42 ]. Children are also heat-vulnerable because their thermoregulatory capacity, hydration dependence, outdoor activity patterns, and reliance on caregivers can increase their risk of dehydration, heat exhaustion, and delayed response during extreme heat events [ 47 ] (Sugg et al., 2023). In addition, socioeconomically disadvantaged groups often lack access to cooling technologies, such as air conditioning, thereby increasing their susceptibility to heat stress [ 44 ]. Studies highlight that racial and ethnic minorities residing in urban heat islands often face amplified risks due to greater exposure and limited resources to adapt to extreme heat conditions [ 44 , 46 ]. Socioeconomically disadvantaged groups may also lack access to air conditioning, high-quality housing, tree canopy, and reliable healthcare, which increases their susceptibility to heat stress [ 44 ]. In addition, racial/ethnic minority and low-income communities often experience higher ambient or surface temperatures because of uneven vegetation cover, impervious surface concentration, housing inequities, and historically unequal urban development patterns. Recent studies of thermal inequity in the United States show that neighborhood income and racial/ethnic composition are associated with measurable differences in urban heat exposure, indicating that marginalized communities may face both greater exposure and reduced adaptive capacity [ 48 , 49 ]. This distinction is important because heat injustice operates not only through who is more vulnerable to heat, but also through who is more likely to live, work, and move through hotter urban environments. A critical limitation in the vulnerability literature is that demographic indicators are often treated as fixed proxies for risk, even though their importance varies by city, climate, housing context, and health-system access. Age, poverty, race/ethnicity, social isolation, housing and transportation, and access to adaptive resources are repeatedly used to represent heat vulnerability, but their relative influence can differ across urban settings and populations [ 13 , 36 ]. For example, Lehnert et al., [ 36 ] found that heat-health vulnerability patterns observed in Georgia may not be directly transferable to regions with different climates or populations that are not acclimatized. This means vulnerability indicators should not be transferred uncritically from one city to another without local calibration and validation. 3.4 Housing quality, informal settlements, and exposure Housing quality significantly affects how individuals experience heat stress. Informal settlements, characteristic of many rapidly urbanizing regions, frequently lack adequate infrastructure to mitigate heat, including adequate ventilation and efficient cooling systems [ 50 ]. These housing inadequacies can exacerbate heat exposure, as poorly designed living spaces may trap heat, leading to unsafe indoor temperatures. Furthermore, the historical context of housing policies has often marginalized certain groups, placing them at greater risk for heat-related health outcomes [ 50 ]. Urban areas with high impervious surface densities also contribute to elevated temperatures, underscoring the importance of integrating green spaces and reflective materials into urban planning to reduce heat exposure [ 41 , 50 ]. 3.5 Behavioral adaptation and cooling access Behavioral adaptations can significantly mitigate the adverse effects of heat exposure. Access to cooling resources, such as air conditioning and public cooling centers, is paramount to reducing heat-related morbidity. However, inequities in resource distribution can lead to significant disparities in access to cooling across demographic groups [ 44 ]. Public health strategies must focus on increasing awareness among vulnerable populations about heat risks and the importance of preventive measures, such as hydration and avoiding outdoor activities during peak heat hours. Effective warning systems can also play a crucial role in prompting behavioral changes that help reduce heat stress exposure and improve health outcomes [ 44 , 51 ]. The UHEH axis provides a conceptual framework for understanding these risks and the interactions between urban planning, environmental exposures, and social factors. 4.1 Theoretical logic and causal structure of the UHEH axis The theoretical contribution of the UHEH axis lies in its formalization of urban heat risk as a causal sequence rather than as a static overlay of temperature and vulnerability indicators. Existing heat vulnerability studies have shown that exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity shape spatially uneven health risks, but many approaches remain index-based and vary widely in indicator selection, weighting, and validation methods [ 20 , 21 ]. Recent work further emphasizes that heat vulnerability must be hazard-specific and place-specific because intra-urban thermal variability, demographic sensitivity, and mismatches between place of residence and place of exposure can alter observed risk patterns [ 22 ]. The UHEH axis builds on this literature by organizing heat-health risk into a causal chain in which urban form modifies thermal load, thermal load produces physiological stress, social vulnerability amplifies exposure and limits adaptive capacity, health-system capacity mediates morbidity and mortality, and governance feedback can reduce or intensify future risk. This causal structure can be expressed through four analytical propositions. Urban morphology, including impervious surface cover, building density, road networks, vegetation loss, and limited ventilation, increases localized thermal load and prolongs nighttime heat exposure. Thermal load does not uniformly translate into health outcomes; its effects are mediated by biological factors, including age, chronic disease, hydration status, medication use, occupational exposure, and physiological heat tolerance. Social vulnerability modifies both exposure and response capacity through housing quality, poverty, racial and ethnic inequality, access to cooling, access to healthcare, and social isolation. Governance interventions, including early warning systems, cooling centers, housing regulations, urban greening, and emergency preparedness, can serve as feedback mechanisms that alter future exposure, vulnerability, and health system demand. This structure converts the UHEH axis into a testable framework in which each link can be operationalized through measurable variables, modeled using epidemiological or machine-learning approaches, and evaluated against observed health outcomes. This framing also addresses a limitation in many existing heat-vulnerability studies. Although exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity are widely used as organizing concepts, studies differ substantially in how these dimensions are measured, weighted, and combined into indices [ 20 , 21 ]. Li et al., [ 21 ] showed that heat vulnerability assessments use different conceptual frameworks, indicators, and mathematical procedures, while Bao et al., [ 20 ] noted that quantitative heat vulnerability assessments remain methodologically diverse. Consequently, two studies may identify different high-risk areas even when analyzing similar urban contexts. The UHEH framework does not remove this uncertainty, but it makes the causal assumptions explicit and identifies where future empirical validation is required. Compared with conventional heat-vulnerability and climate-health frameworks, the UHEH framework does not simply classify vulnerable populations or map thermal exposure. Its intended contribution is to organize these components into a sequential and testable pathway linking urban form, thermal exposure, social vulnerability, biological response, health outcomes, validation requirements, and governance feedback. Existing heat-vulnerability indices often differ in indicator selection, weighting methods, spatial units, and validation practices, which can limit comparability and policy translation. The UHEH framework addresses this gap by making the causal assumptions explicit, identifying measurable variables at each stage, and specifying where future empirical validation, baseline comparison, equity assessment, and governance activation should occur. Its value therefore lies in translational structuring and testability, not in claiming that the relationship among heat, vulnerability, and health is newly discovered or already empirically proven. 4.2 Urban form → Thermal load → Human stress Urban areas are particularly susceptible to the urban heat island (UHI) effect, where city temperatures exceed those of surrounding rural areas due to anthropogenic heat and altered land surfaces (Tan et al., 2009). This phenomenon intensifies human exposure to heat, leading to adverse health outcomes. Studies show that the thermal load in densely built environments correlates with increased incidences of heat-related illnesses, especially during extreme heat events (Tan et al., 2009). For instance, research in Shanghai identified higher mortality rates associated with prolonged heat exposure during significant heat waves (Tan et al., 2009). These findings suggest that urban forms characterized by limited green spaces and high building densities inherently increase thermal stress among residents. 4.3 Environmental exposure–health outcome signal pathways Environmental exposure to elevated temperatures precipitates a range of health outcomes, including heat-related morbidity and mortality. Evidence indicates that high ambient temperatures can exacerbate pre-existing health conditions, such as respiratory and cardiovascular diseases [ 41 , 52 ]. Moreover, air quality issues associated with urban pollution further compound these risks by affecting vulnerable populations [ 53 ]. Particulate matter and gaseous pollutants contribute to respiratory distress and other health complications [ 41 , 52 ]. Thus, the pathways linking environmental exposure to health outcomes are complex and influenced by multiple factors, including urban pollution, physical health status, and social determinants of health. 4.4 Social vulnerability amplification The risks posed by heat exposure are not uniformly distributed across populations. Research emphasizes that social vulnerability amplifies health risks associated with heat (Li et al., 2021). Marginalized communities often reside in heat-prone areas with limited access to cooling resources such as air conditioning and green spaces [ 54 ]. Historical factors, including redlining and systemic inequalities, play a critical role in shaping these disparities (Li et al., 2021). For instance, studies have shown that neighborhoods impacted by historical redlining exhibit higher temperatures and greater health risks during heat waves (Li et al., 2021). Addressing these vulnerabilities requires targeted interventions that consider the social determinants of health. 4.5 Feedback through urban planning and policy Effective urban planning and policy are crucial in mitigating the impacts of heat exposure. Policies that promote green infrastructure—such as parks, urban forests, and reflective surfaces—can significantly reduce urban heat and enhance community resilience to heat waves [ 55 ]. Additionally, integrating health impact assessments into urban development plans can ensure that environmental and health considerations inform decision-making [ 55 ]. As urban areas continue to evolve, ongoing evaluation of spatial planning and infrastructure development is necessary to reduce heat exposure and associated health risks. 4.6 Equity-centered adaptation To effectively mitigate heat-related health risks, an adaptation strategy centered on equity must be adopted. This involves tailoring interventions to address the needs of the most vulnerable populations, thereby ensuring equitable access to resources and support during heat events [ 56 ]. Equitable adaptation approaches can include fostering community engagement, increasing public awareness of heat risks, and expanding access to cooling centers (Li et al., 2021). Additionally, urban planners and policymakers should prioritize the development of social networks that empower communities and facilitate collective action for heat resilience [ 57 ]. Recognizing and addressing historical injustices in urban planning can lead to more sustainable and equitable solutions for heat-related health risks. These propositions make the UHEH framework empirically testable while preserving its present role as a conceptual and translational structure for future validation. 5 Mechanistic pathways linking heat exposure to health outcomes In rapidly urbanizing cities, heat exposure poses considerable health risks, exacerbated by the UHI effect. Multiple pathways link climate-induced heat stress to adverse health outcomes, underscoring the need for integrated urban planning and public health strategies. 5.1 Thermal load and cardiovascular stress Thermal load from extreme heat poses significant cardiovascular risks, particularly within urban environments. As cities experience heightened temperatures due to global climate change and UHI effects, the physiological stress on cardiovascular systems escalates. Individuals often face increased heart rates and elevated blood pressure, with studies indicating that mortality rates from cardiovascular diseases rise with each incremental degree of temperature increase [ 41 , 59 ]. For instance, Kovats et al., observed a 3.3% increase in all-cause mortality for every 1 °C rise in temperature above 21.5 °C, highlighting the direct relationship between thermal load and cardiovascular outcomes in urban settings [ 59 ]. Given these findings, adapting urban environments to mitigate heat—such as increasing vegetation and improving building design—becomes imperative to protect cardiovascular health. 5.2 Dehydration, renal strain, and metabolic stress The interplay of heat exposure and dehydration underscores a critical health risk associated with urban living, particularly among vulnerable populations. Chronic exposure to high temperatures depletes body fluids and can lead to acute kidney injury (AKI) and heat stress nephropathy [ 60 ]. Dehydration triggers a cascade of metabolic consequences, including renal strain and exacerbated pre-existing health conditions [ 61 ]. Glaser et al., [ 60 ] emphasize that extreme heat events associated with climate change and urbanization significantly increase dehydration cases and chronic kidney disease. Thus, effective public health strategies must include education on hydration, particularly during extreme weather events, especially in urban areas where heat stress is exacerbated by the built environment (Tan et al., 2009; [ 60 ]). 5.3 Heat–air pollution interaction effects The interaction between heat exposure and air pollution complicates health risk assessments in urban scenarios. The urban environment intensifies both heat and pollutant exposures, as heat waves are associated with elevated atmospheric pollutants—such as ground-level ozone—potentially leading to increased incidence of respiratory and cardiovascular diseases ([ 62 ]; Tan et al., 2006). Research has shown that urban areas experience significant spikes in morbidity during simultaneous heat and high pollution episodes, necessitating integrated public health interventions (Li & Bou‐Zeid, 2013; Tan et al., 2006). In this context, it is crucial to develop strategies that not only address heat-related health outcomes but also consider the compounding effects of air pollution during heat waves (Li & Bou‐Zeid, 2013; [ 63 ]). 5.4 Nighttime heat and sleep disruption Increasing nighttime temperatures linked to climate change have profound implications for sleep quality, a crucial factor in overall health. Urban areas experience slower nighttime cooling rates due to heat-retaining surfaces, which contribute to sleep disturbances that can exacerbate stress and metabolic dysregulation (Tan et al., 2009; He et al., 2024). Research has shown that inadequate sleep leads to increased vulnerability to a range of health issues, including cardiovascular diseases and metabolic disorders [ 47 ]. Thus, urban planning must incorporate designs that promote effective cooling strategies and enhance nighttime recovery for residents subjected to consistent thermal stress [ 62 ]. 5.5 Cumulative heat exposure effects Cumulative exposure to high temperatures is a significant public health concern, particularly as climate projections indicate an increase in the frequency and intensity of heat waves [ 62 , 64 ]. Evidence suggests that repeated exposure to extreme heat can lead to a cumulative risk effect, where chronic exposure exacerbates the impacts of single heat events [ 61 , 65 ]. This feedback loop underscores the importance of longitudinal studies and targeted interventions for vulnerable populations, as understanding individual and community heat-exposure patterns can inform the development of effective adaptation strategies [ 65 ]. The creation of green spaces and improved urban infrastructures can mitigate the effects of cumulative heat exposure, thus safeguarding public health in densely populated areas [ 66 ]. The Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) Land-Surface Temperature (LST) products have been a significant asset in this area, providing daily, tile-based LST data that is crucial for inferring ambient temperature variations, particularly in metropolitan areas prone to Urban Heat Islands (UHIs) [ 67 ]. Studies indicate that using these data sources can enhance predictive capabilities for heat risk, particularly when LST is used as a proxy for heat exposure (Li et al., 2021). Furthermore, recent developments in remote sensing techniques enable a more precise assessment of heat exposure in urban settings. For example, some studies have integrated remote sensing data with climatological models to project heat-related morbidity and mortality across various urban settings [ 68 ]. This comprehensive approach helps identify spatial patterns and trends in heat exposure, which are essential for urban health planning. The evidence base also shows that no single thermal dataset is sufficient for heat-health prediction. Satellite-derived land surface temperature is useful for mapping spatial variability in urban heat, but it may not fully capture air temperature, humidity, wind, indoor exposure, shaded microclimates, or nighttime recovery conditions. Weather-station data provide standardized observations but may fail to resolve neighborhood-scale thermal variation, especially where station density is low [ 20 ]. Modeled data can improve spatial and temporal coverage, but they depend on assumptions that require local validation. These limitations support a multi-source thermal data architecture rather than reliance on a single temperature product. 6.2 Health indicators and proxy outcomes Identifying relevant health indicators is pivotal for linking thermal data to specific health outcomes. Comparable public health modeling work in the Global South shows that predictive systems are most useful when linked to infrastructure constraints, local monitoring capacity, and actionable governance pathways [ 16 ]. Heat stress has been linked to an increase in various morbidity and mortality indicators, particularly amongst vulnerable populations such as the elderly, those with pre-existing health conditions, and outdoor workers [ 69 ]. Previous research has confirmed that extreme heat events are associated with increased respiratory and cardiovascular mortality, particularly in urban areas during heat waves [ 70 ]. Additionally, empirical studies have attributed a substantial portion of heat-related mortality to increased temperatures driven by anthropogenic climate change in cities [ 71 ]. Early warning systems (EWS) that communicate heat-related health risks are crucial in mitigating these effects. Several studies have shown that populations with timely information about heat events exhibit reduced heat-related morbidity and mortality, underscoring the significant role of effective public interventions [ 72 ]. However, vulnerability indices also have important limitations. Their outputs depend heavily on indicator selection, weighting decisions, spatial scale, and data availability. Bao et al., [ 20 ] emphasized that heat vulnerability assessments should be validated against observed health outcomes, while Li et al., [ 21 ] showed that methods for constructing urban heat vulnerability assessments vary widely across studies. Reid et al., [ 13 ] also noted that using health data to validate heat vulnerability measures is an important next step for identifying local and regional differences in vulnerability drivers. This creates uncertainty when indices are used for policy decisions without local health-outcome validation. 6.3 Socio-spatial vulnerability indices The development and application of socio-spatial vulnerability indices are essential for understanding the dynamic interplay between climate-driven health risks and urbanization. introduced a Heat Vulnerability Index integrating socio-demographic characteristics and land cover data, demonstrating that specific regions, even those experiencing high ambient temperatures, do not uniformly exhibit high vulnerability to heat health risks [ 73 ]. This heterogeneity necessitates tailored responses in urban planning and health service provision, especially in rapidly urbanizing regions where socio-economic factors significantly modulate vulnerability [ 74 ]. Emerging methodologies in this area involve leveraging machine learning and data science to refine indices and analyze socio-demographic data alongside thermal measurements. By addressing vulnerabilities in high-risk populations, cities can strategically allocate resources and implement targeted public health interventions to minimize heat-related impacts [ 75 ]. Recent work on green infrastructure also emphasizes its role in improving microclimate resilience, biodiversity, and sustainability outcomes, especially when adapted to local ecological and resource conditions [ 76 ]. 6.4 Urban form metrics Quantifying urban form metrics—such as land-use patterns, vegetation cover, and spatial configuration—provides insight into how urban structure influences localized temperature elevations. Research indicates that areas with denser development and limited green space experience greater heat exposure and associated health risks [ 77 ]. Additionally, urbanization creates distinct microclimates, necessitating a focus on urban design strategies that incorporate green spaces to mitigate UHI effects [ 31 ]. The interaction between urban morphology and heat exposure warrants thorough investigation, particularly in contexts characterized by rapid growth and informal settlements. Urban form metrics can thus serve as predictive tools for assessing potential heat vulnerability across neighborhoods and inform planning to enhance resilience to extreme heat [ 78 ]. 6.5 Data harmonization and bias correction Data harmonization is crucial for ensuring consistency across the multiple data sources used to analyze heat-related health risks. Differences in measurement devices, spatial resolution, temporal coverage, health-outcome definitions, and reporting intervals can introduce bias into analytical models [ 79 ]. For example, satellite-derived land surface temperature may overrepresent surface heating and may not directly reflect near-surface air temperature experienced by residents. This can be partially addressed by calibrating satellite temperature products against ground-based weather-station or urban-sensor observations using regression-based correction, cross-sensor calibration, or spatial downscaling methods. Similarly, health datasets such as emergency department visits, ambulance call-outs, hospital admissions, and mortality records may differ in coding systems, reporting frequency, and spatial aggregation. These inconsistencies can be reduced through temporal alignment of exposure and outcome data, standardization of diagnostic categories, normalization of variables, and sensitivity testing across alternative heat thresholds or lag periods. Bias correction is also important when integrating environmental and socio-spatial vulnerability data. For instance, census-based vulnerability indicators may be available at different spatial scales than thermal imagery or health records, requiring areal interpolation, aggregation to common spatial units, or uncertainty checks to avoid ecological bias. Missing observations in sensor networks or health datasets may require imputation procedures, while class imbalance in rare severe outcomes may require careful weighting or resampling during model development. Multiple trigger points for quantifying heat-health impacts can also improve interpretation by accounting for variability in health outcomes across different temperature thresholds [ 80 ]. This need for integrated environmental risk assessment is consistent with statistical approaches that combine heterogeneous environmental, spatial, and public health data streams to support risk characterization [ 81 ]. By improving data integration, correcting measurement bias, and testing sensitivity across assumptions, researchers can produce more reliable and actionable heat-health risk estimates. Health outcome variables or proxies should include heat-related emergency department visits, hospital admissions, ambulance calls, mortality, syndromic surveillance records, or other locally available health-service indicators; these outcomes have been recommended for evaluating heat-health burden and validating heat vulnerability indices and early warning systems [ 20 , 58 , 91 ]. Model outputs should be evaluated using transparent performance metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, root mean square error, calibration error, and spatial transferability across cities, because predictive models intended for public health decision support require not only accuracy but also validation, reproducibility, and interpretability [ 58 , 75 , 92 ]. This operational structure clarifies how the conceptual UHEH axis can guide future empirical testing, model validation, and decision-support implementation in rapidly urbanizing cities. Because this paper does not implement a new empirical model, validation is presented here as a necessary future step rather than as an accomplished outcome. Future applications of the UHEH framework should test predictive performance using retrospective and prospective urban datasets that link thermal exposure with observed health outcomes, including emergency department visits, hospital admissions, ambulance call-outs, mortality records, and syndromic surveillance indicators. Previous heat-health studies have shown that hospitalization, ambulance, and emergency service data can be used to evaluate heat-related morbidity burden and inform early warning systems [ 58 , 91 ]. Validation should compare predicted risk classifications against observed health events across spatial units and heat-event periods, using metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, calibration error, and spatial transferability. This approach would allow the UHEH framework to be tested for robustness, reliability, and applicability in real-world urban systems while maintaining the present paper’s role as a conceptual and methodological foundation. Such dashboards not only visually represent current weather conditions but also provide actionable data for the public health sector. For example, dashboards implemented in urban settings use satellite imagery and health data to assess vulnerability and facilitate targeted interventions [ 75 ]. The use of historical data in these dashboards can enhance predictions of future health risks and, when combined with environmental data, identify patterns associated with increased morbidity during extreme heat events [ 45 , 94 ]. 8.2 Predictive alerts for hospitals and communities Predictive alert systems are vital for preemptively notifying hospitals and communities about impending heat waves. Studies have shown that such alerts can effectively reduce heat-related hospital admissions when well-integrated into local health intervention systems (Anderson et al., 2013; [ 95 ]). For instance, countries like Australia have implemented systems that alert healthcare providers and at-risk populations, enabling timely responses that mitigate the health burdens associated with extreme heat [ 96 , 97 ]. The challenge, however, is that studies indicate that many individuals remain unresponsive to heat alerts, underscoring the need for more targeted communication strategies to facilitate behavioral change among at-risk populations [ 98 ]. 8.3 Integration with disaster risk systems Integrating heat-related early warning systems with broader disaster risk management frameworks ensures a comprehensive approach to handling heat emergencies. This interdisciplinary strategy encompasses urban health, emergency response, and environmental safety, thereby strengthening community resilience against heat waves [ 99 , 100 ]. Research has documented positive outcomes when such integration occurs, particularly in urban environments where the urban heat island effect exacerbates the impacts of extreme temperatures [ 10 ]. An example is the EuroHEAT project, which coordinated efforts across Europe to develop unified early warning system protocols that have been associated with reduced heat-related mortality during heat crises [ 94 ]. 8.4 Community-based warning strategies Community-based warning strategies empower residents and local organizations to actively engage in heat preparedness and response, which is crucial for vulnerable populations—particularly in dense urban centers where access to relief resources may be limited [ 45 , 101 ]. Engaging community members in creating localized response plans increases the relevance of warnings and ensures that communication strategies are culturally and contextually appropriate [ 97 ]. Cities like Ahmedabad have already adopted community-driven health action plans that prioritize outreach and education, significantly reducing heat-related mortality [ 93 , 101 ]. This grassroots approach can enhance the effectiveness of early warning systems by ensuring that information reaches those most vulnerable. 8.5 Policy triggers The effectiveness of heat risk management strategies depends significantly on establishing clear policy triggers that activate public health interventions in response to real-time data. For instance, certain regions have established temperature thresholds that, when exceeded, automatically trigger protocols for resource distribution, such as air-conditioning units or access to shaded cooling centers [ 96 , 102 ]. Aligning policies with operational early warning systems is crucial for fostering an agile and responsive public health apparatus, as demonstrated by global studies showing declines in heat-related morbidity following the enactment of such preemptive measures [ 95 , 103 ]. Moreover, creating adaptability in response protocols allows areas to fine-tune their strategies in alignment with evolving climate conditions, ensuring that health systems remain resilient to the compounded stresses from urbanization and climate change (Guo et al., 2024; [ 104 ]). Urban design that incorporates elements such as green roofs, vertical gardens, and reflective materials not only addresses UHI but also enhances urban resilience against extreme heat events [ 106 , 107 ]. Descriptive studies emphasize the importance of parks and urban forests in reducing local temperatures, thereby improving thermal comfort for city dwellers [ 109 , 110 ]. Furthermore, green infrastructure contributes to enhanced physical activity and social cohesion, both of which are vital for overall health and well-being [ 112 ]. This multifaceted approach effectively improves quality of life in urban settings while reducing the burden of heat-related health issues. Broader sustainability research also shows that resilience depends on integrated resource efficiency, ecological protection, and locally adaptable management systems [ 81 ]. 9.4 Climate justice Climate justice is crucial to urban health, as marginalized populations often bear the brunt of heat exposure due to inadequate access to resources and infrastructure. Vulnerable communities, including low-income families and older adults, are disproportionately affected by extreme heat, underscoring the urgent need for equitable urban planning initiatives [ 113 ]. Addressing these disparities through policy frameworks that prioritize environmental justice not only enhances resilience but also ensures that all communities have access to cooling resources, thereby aligning with climate equity objectives [ 114 , 115 ]. Addressing these challenges requires a multifaceted research approach across several domains. 10.1 Longitudinal heat–health datasets Longitudinal datasets that explore the correlation between heat exposure and health outcomes remain inadequate. Existing predictive models for heat health risks can benefit from structured time-series data, which facilitates the investigation of delayed effects. Gasparrini, [ 121 ] emphasizes the use of distributed lag models to quantify health impacts of environmental factors over time, demonstrating their practical value in public health research. Furthermore, Nori-Sarma et al., [ 122 ] argue that systematically collecting longitudinal datasets is necessary to improve heat alert systems and protect health amid increasing heat events. In such contexts, the effectiveness of health initiatives rises significantly when trends and patterns can be observed over extended periods. 10.2 Cross-city model transferability Enhancing the transferability of models across cities is paramount. Recent studies indicate that local demographics, infrastructure, and health service provision vary widely, impacting heat-related health outcomes. For instance, Zhang et al., [ 123 ] highlight the potential of transfer learning approaches to address data scarcity challenges, which can be similarly applied to health model predictions across different urban settings. Additionally, research in Nairobi suggests that environmental and health vulnerabilities intersect in ways that can inform broader climate justice strategies applicable across various urban environments [ 124 ]. Thus, developing frameworks for cross-city model transferability could be vital to enhancing predictive capacities for urban heat-related health risks. 10.3 Informal settlement data scarcity Informal settlements pose unique challenges for health research, complicating data collection due to socio-economic barriers and inconsistent infrastructure. Evidence from studies conducted in slums indicates that poor living conditions significantly increase health risks, underscoring the urgent need to prioritize data collection in these areas [ 125 ]. For example, Corburn and Hildebrand advocate integrating citizen science approaches to improve the collection of environmental health data in informal settlements, thereby empowering communities and contributing to local climate justice initiatives [ 124 ]. Such strategies can facilitate local data generation and ultimately inform policies tailored to the vulnerabilities these populations face. 10.4 Ethical governance of health data The ethical governance of health data in rapidly urbanizing contexts remains insufficiently addressed. The intricate balance between data utility and privacy, particularly for vulnerable populations in urban slums, necessitates rigorous frameworks. Raqib et al., note that populations living in urban slums are more susceptible to various health threats due to inadequate infrastructure [ 126 ], underscoring the need for ethically sound data practices that protect individual confidentiality while allowing health professionals to access crucial information for effective interventions. Responsible innovation frameworks are also needed when emerging technologies are linked to public health, environmental safety, and governance decisions [ 16 ]. Ethical frameworks must guide the use of data, respect community voices, and ensure that health interventions are just and equitable. 10.5 Limitations of the framework A key limitation of this manuscript is that the UHEH framework is not empirically implemented or validated in the present study. The paper does not estimate model parameters, train machine-learning algorithms, test predictive performance, or compare predicted risks against observed health outcomes. The framework should therefore be interpreted as a conceptual and translational structure rather than a validated predictive system. This limitation is important because heat vulnerability assessments should be tested against observed morbidity, mortality, hospital admissions, emergency department visits, and ambulance calls before being used for planning or public health decision-making [ 20 , 21 ]. Future research should validate the UHEH framework using longitudinal heat exposure data, socio-spatial vulnerability indicators, urban morphology metrics, and observed health outcomes. Evaluation should assess calibration, sensitivity, specificity, false-negative rates, observed-versus-predicted agreement, spatial transferability, and performance across vulnerable groups. This is especially important because social vulnerability indicators have been linked to heat-related emergency department visits and mortality, and heat-warning systems should be grounded in local epidemiological evidence and emergency management capacity [ 36 , 58 ]. The equity component of the framework also requires empirical testing. Although the UHEH framework identifies fairness indicators and governance mechanisms, this paper does not implement subgroup performance testing, fairness constraints, or community-based accountability procedures. Future applications should evaluate whether models produce unequal false-negative rates, calibration errors, or intervention gaps across elderly residents, children, low-income communities, racial/ethnic minority populations, informal-settlement residents, outdoor workers, and people with disabilities. Without this evaluation, heat-health prediction systems may reproduce existing inequities even when designed for climate adaptation. 11 Conclusion This framework-oriented integrative synthesis reframes urban heat as a dynamic, governable health-risk system rather than a temperature hazard alone. The central argument advanced through the UHEH axis is that heat-related morbidity and mortality emerge from the interaction of environmental, biological, social, infrastructural, and institutional conditions. In rapidly urbanizing cities, these interactions are especially consequential because heat exposure is intensified by urban form, unevenly distributed across neighborhoods, and amplified among populations with limited cooling access, poor housing conditions, pre-existing health risks, and reduced institutional protection. The UHEH framework contributes to heat-health scholarship by organizing thermal exposure, socio-spatial vulnerability, urban morphology, mechanistic health pathways, health-system demand, and governance response into a single analytical structure. Its value lies in clarifying what future heat-health intelligence systems must connect: where heat accumulates, who is exposed, which groups are biologically and socially vulnerable, what health outcomes should be monitored, and which policy actions should be triggered. This structure provides a foundation for future empirical modeling, local calibration, validation against observed health outcomes, and decision-support development. The framework also makes equity central to heat-health governance. It shows that effective adaptation cannot rely only on citywide temperature thresholds or generalized heat maps. Cities need locally validated systems that link early warnings, real-time potential heat-risk dashboards, community communication, access to cooling, health-sector preparedness, nature-based cooling, and targeted policy interventions to the populations most likely to experience harm. In this sense, predicting heat-related health risks is not only a technical modeling challenge but also a governance and climate justice challenge. The UHEH framework should, however, be interpreted as a conceptual and translational structure rather than a validated predictive model. Its next step is empirical testing using city-specific thermal exposure data, socio-spatial vulnerability indicators, urban morphology metrics, and observed health outcomes such as emergency department visits, hospital admissions, ambulance calls, and mortality records. If implemented and validated, the framework can support a shift from reactive heat emergency response toward anticipatory, equity-centered, and climate-resilient urban health governance.
