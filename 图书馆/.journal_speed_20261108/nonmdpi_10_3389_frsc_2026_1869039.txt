Faculty of Engineering, Autonomous University of San Luis Potosí, San Luis Potosí, Mexico See more Article metrics View details Abstract Green infrastructure, such as urban tree canopy cover and green spaces, plays a fundamental role in improving environmental comfort and quality of life in semi-arid cities. However, its distribution is often uneven across the urban systems and associated with socioeconomic status, a phenomenon known as the luxury effect. Despite the growing adoption of the 3-30-300 rule as a comprehensive indicator, its application in Mexican cities is virtually nonexistent and remains limited in semi-arid contexts. This study evaluates this rule in the Metropolitan Area of San Luis Potosí by integrating public green spaces with socioeconomic data at the Basic Geostatistical Area level. Green spaces were classified into urban parks, linear parks, neighborhood parks, and local gardens according to their recreational and social functions. The results indicate an availability of 5.6 m2 of green space per capita, below the World Health Organization’s recommended minimum of 9 m2. Public green spaces account for only 0.4% of the urban area and are primarily concentrated in large-sized parks. Furthermore, the indicators of the 3-30-300 rule are predominantly complied with in areas with lower levels of social deprivation, which exhibit greater vegetation cover, better accessibility, and stronger integration with residential areas, confirming the presence of the luxury effect. It was observed that in most cases, semi-arid cities have low per capita levels of green space. These findings highlight the need to generate evidence on the provision of green infrastructure in semi-arid cities, as well as to reconsider conventional indicators and incorporate socio-spatial dynamics into their evaluation. Introduction Green infrastructure, primarily composed of urban tree cover and green spaces, has become a key indicator for environmental assessment within urban systems and across cities. Due to their public use, these areas must be accessible and equitably distributed throughout the city (Hassan and Jombach, 2025; Almeida and Fonseca, 2025). The WHO (2017) has proposed that the minimum public green space provision should be at least 9 m2 per capita to ensure that the environmental benefits are accessible and equitably distributed among the population. Moreover, in the context of global climate change, green spaces, as part of green infrastructure, are regarded as a strategy for mitigating and adapting to extreme urban climate conditions (Wong et al., 2021). Among the main environmental benefits of green spaces are carbon sequestration, atmospheric oxygen production, and the regulation of temperature and humidity, among others (Semeraro et al., 2021). Additionally, from a social perspective, green spaces promote social cohesion, recreation, and both physical and mental health (Jennings and Bamkole, 2019). Therefore, the planning of urban green spaces should integrate environmental, sociodemographic, and territorial dimensions to ensure equitable benefits in terms of surface area, quality, and accessibility (Reyes-Riveros et al., 2021; Graça et al., 2022). However, the adequacy of these spaces varies significantly across regions and climatic conditions. Depending on the latitude, notable contrasts exist between cities in the Global North and South. For instance, Ljubljana, Slovenia, provides up to 560 m2 of green space per capita (Cömertler, 2017), whereas Rome, Italy, and Madrid, Spain, offer 39 and 21 m2 per capita, respectively (Guerini, 2021). In contrast, the average availability in Latin American cities is only 3.5 m2 per capita (UN-Habitat, 2019). Although climatic conditions influence the development of green infrastructure, cities in arid and semi-arid regions remain understudied, despite facing greater challenges in the provision and maintenance of public green spaces (Arku and Marais, 2021; Garau et al., 2025). In Mexico, green space availability is similarly limited, particularly in semi-arid cities. Monterrey and Chihuahua report 4.8 and 6.2 m2 per capita, respectively (Jiménez et al., 2013; Herrera-Correa and Romo-Aguilar, 2021), whereas the Metropolitan Area of San Luis Potosí reaches only 6.7 m2 per capita (Ramos-Palacios et al., 2024). These figures indicate a consistently low provision of public green spaces and highlight a widespread deficit across semi-arid Mexican cities. In arid and semi-arid cities, insufficient and uneven distribution of green spaces tends to intensify due to climatic conditions characterized by low precipitation, high temperatures, and limited water availability (Nathawat et al., 2025; Garau et al., 2025). Additionally, in cities of the Global South, including those in Latin America, urban planning often fails to prioritize the creation of green spaces, resulting in segregated and inequitable distributions, as reported by Arku and Marais, 2021. Consequently, environmental justice has become a widely used framework for studying green space provision and distribution. It refers to equitable access to environmental benefits and the differentiated impacts of climate change across diverse social groups (UNDP, 2023). In urban contexts, environmental justice allows for the analysis of relationships between population distribution, environmental conditions, and demographic characteristics (Casillas Zapata, 2023). From this perspective, Hope et al. (2003) introduced the concept of the “luxury effect” to describe the positive relationship between income levels and green space availability. This phenomenon is interpreted as a form of distributive environmental injustice, as urban greenery tends to concentrate in higher-income areas. However, the luxury effect is also linked to environmental factors, particularly climate and constraints on vegetation maintenance. The luxury effect framework contributes an urban ecology perspective by emphasizing climatic, geographic, and biotic factors while recognizing that social and economic drivers are fundamental determinants of urban green cover patterns (Aznarez et al., 2023). This phenomenon has also been documented across countries with varying levels of socioeconomic development (Uribe and Villaseñor, 2024), suggesting that urban planning processes often favor wealthier populations in the distribution of green spaces rather than ensuring equitable access for all residents, regardless of national or local socioeconomic conditions. According to Chamberlain et al. (2020), the luxury effect is more pronounced in arid and semi-arid cities, whereas in temperate cities with annual precipitation above 500 mm, its impact is less evident. This difference is largely associated with challenges in implementing and maintaining urban green infrastructure, including water scarcity, increased maintenance costs, and limited research in semi-arid contexts (Okour and Shaweesh, 2024). Additionally, Leong et al. (2018) highlight the role of green space privatization—often referred to as urban green grabbing (Fairhead et al., 2012) —in reinforcing the luxury effect. Privatization of green spaces incentivates the luxury effect due to its concentration in wealthier areas in order to increase property values (García-Lamarca et al., 2021). Green space provision is most effective when it is equitably distributed in terms of surface area, publicly accessible, and spatially integrated across the territory, with the state playing a central role (Bush, 2020). For this reason, studies on green infrastructure primarily focus on public green spaces, as these reflect governmental efforts to deliver environmental benefits to the population. The WHO (2017) emphasizes the importance of green spaces as a key component of public space due to their environmental and social benefits, advocating for equitable access and distribution in cities. This underscores their collective nature and public function within urban environments. Nevertheless, the insufficiency and unequal distribution of green spaces remain persistent challenges worldwide. In Latin America, uneven distribution patterns have been identified in cities such as Buenos Aires, Argentina (Spescha et al., 2020), Medellín, Colombia (Paniagua-Villada et al., 2024), and Santiago de Chile, Chile (Riquelme and Vázquez-Fuentes, 2021; Navarro Martínez, 2018). In Mexico, similar patterns have been reported in Mexico City (Fernández-Álvarez, 2017), Guadalajara (Cruz-Sandoval et al., 2020), and Monterrey (Casillas Zapata, 2023). In San Luis Potosí, multiple studies confirm inequitable green space distribution (González Lugo and Pérez Barragán, 2022; Ramos-Palacios et al., 2024). Furthermore, Rodríguez-Parra et al. (2024) found that land surface temperature—closely associated with green spaces—varies according to socioeconomic status in the same study area. Thus, unequal green space distribution may reflect underlying socioeconomic patterns, consistent with the luxury effect. On the other hand, the use and benefits of public green spaces are strongly linked to their accessibility (Kemec and Abdalkarim, 2023). In this regard, several indicators have been developed to assess whether green space provision is both sufficient and accessible in spatial and environmental terms. One of these indicators is the 3-30-300 rule proposed by Konijnendijk (2021), which states that every person should have at least three visible trees from their home, a minimum of 30% tree canopy cover in their neighborhood, and access to a public park within 300 meters. The novelty of this approach, compared with the 10-20-30rule and other biodiversity metrics, lies in its integration of social and urban dimensions into urban tree planning. This perspective recognizes urban green infrastructure not only as an ecological asset but also as a resource that supports social use and promotes equitable access to ecosystem services. While this rule has been primarily applied in temperate cities, evidence of its performance in arid and semi-arid contexts remains limited. Therefore, this study analyzes the distribution, proximity, and sufficiency of public green spaces in the semi-arid metropolitan area of San Luis Potosí. The study adopts an environmental justice framework, applying spatial indicators and the 3-30-300 rule. A multiscale analysis was conducted to compare indicator performance across socioeconomic zones throughout the metropolitan area. This study contributes to understanding the implications of the luxury effect on the distribution of green spaces in semi-arid cities, highlighting the particular challenges associated with planning, implementation, and management of urban vegetation in these contexts. Moreover, semi-arid urban systems are especially vulnerable to climate change, while urban green spaces are among the most effective nature-based solutions for mitigating and adapting to its effects. However, the development and management of green infrastructure in low-precipitation cities remain poorly understood due to the limited number of studies conducted in semi-arid environments. Therefore, expanding knowledge of green-space distribution and sufficiency in these urban systems is essential to support evidence-based urban planning and the respective green infrastructure to adapt to climate change. Methods Study area The Metropolitan Area of San Luis Potosí (MASLP) comprises the municipalities of San Luis Potosí, Soledad de Graciano Sánchez, and Villa de Pozos, with a total population of 1,243,980 inhabitants and an area of 1,787.7 km2 (SEDATU, 2024). The MASLP exhibits variations of arid temperate climate with dry winters (BWkw), semi-arid temperate climate with dry winters (BS0kw), and semi-dry temperate climate (BS1kw). Under these conditions, precipitation occurs mainly during the summer, with an average annual rainfall below 400 mm. The study area consists of 488 Urban Basic Geostatistical Areas (AGEBs, by its acronym in Spanish) classified by Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía. (INEGI) (2024). These are defined as municipal subdivision polygons comprising between 1 and 50 urban blocks and are delineated by recognizable urban features such as streets and avenues (INEGI, 2014). AGEBs are used to collect demographic and socioeconomic information at the sub-municipal level. Within the MASLP, AGEBs are distributed across the municipalities of San Luis Potosí, Soledad de Graciano Sánchez, and Villa de Pozos (Figure 1). This area corresponds to the zone with the most significant urban development in recent years, with a population density of 107.4 inhabitants per square kilometer, classifying it as a medium-density urban area (SEDATU, 2024). Selection criteria and classification of green spaces The geospatial analysis was conducted using QGIS version 3.40.14, based on Google Earth 2022 imagery, within the EPSG:32614 coordinate reference system corresponding to UTM Zone 14 N. This reference system was used to calculate polygon areas of green spaces and other features within the study area in square meters (m2) or hectares (ha). Basic geostatistical framework maps from Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía. (INEGI) (2024) were used at the urban block and AGEB levels, at a scale of 1:200,000. In all cases, polygons of public green spaces (PGS) were overlaid with GIS layers corresponding to different scales of analysis: metropolitan, municipal, urban AGEB, neighborhood, and urban block. The green-space classification adopted in this study was based on the criteria proposed by Ramos-Palacios et al. (2024), which consider the presence of a clearly defined boundary, vegetation cover, urban infrastructure, and recreational equipment. Additionally, only green spaces with a social and recreational function were included in the analysis. Areas lacking vegetation cover, presenting very small surface areas, or providing limited environmental and social benefits were excluded from the study. It is also important to note that residual green areas were not included in the analysis because they contain little to no tree cover and contribute only 0.003 m2 of green space per capita in the MASLP. Finally, given the 3-30-300 rule’s focus on access to urban green infrastructure, spaces without public access or lacking urban equipment were not considered in the analysis. For the green spaces, four typologies were considered: urban parks (UP), linear parks (LP), neighborhood parks (NP), and local gardens (LG). Under this classification, an urban park is defined as a space with diverse social uses and a high level of urban infrastructure—such as lighting, benches, playgrounds, among others—designed to accommodate a large number of users. Further, linear parks are characterized by elongated spatial configurations and the presence of urban infrastructure and amenities. Similarly, neighborhood parks refer to green areas with infrastructure and equipment that function as recreational spaces at the neighborhood scale. In contrast, local gardens are defined as spaces with limited urban equipment but some infrastructure, whose maintenance largely depends on the surrounding community. Based on this classification, a total of 14 urban parks, 4 linear parks, 73 neighborhood parks, and 96 local gardens were included, resulting in a sample of 187 public green spaces across the city under study (Figure 2). Figure 2 Classification of green spaces, where: (A) Urban park (B) Lineal park (C) Neighborhood park and (D) Local garden. To analyze the distribution of green spaces in relation to socioeconomic and demographic indicators, polygons were grouped by urban AGEB according to Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía. (INEGI) (2024). These polygons were overlaid onto AGEB units to obtain the total green infrastructure area for the study site, as well as aggregated values by green space category. Subsequently, total PGS surface area was calculated per AGEB and by category. For green spaces spanning more than one AGEB, proportional surface area was calculated using the “Intersect” tool in QGIS software by overlaying the respective layers. This approach was supported by a document review of public space and green space planning in each municipality, based on the Municipal Development Plans 2024–2027 (Ayuntamiento de San Luis Potosí City, 2024; Ayuntamiento Soledad de Graciano Sánchez, 2024; Concejo Municipal de Villa de Pozos, 2025). To assess socioeconomic conditions, the marginalization index (MI) developed by CONEVAL (2020) was used at a scale compatible with AGEB-level data. Thus, the following MI categories were defined: very low (VL), low (L), medium (M), high (H), and very high (VH). For this classification, AGEB codes were reviewed and updated according to the 2024 geostatistical framework, as some codes and polygons were modified following the incorporation of the Municipality of Villa de Pozos in 2024. It is important to note that the AGEB corresponding to the urban park ‘Tangamanga I’—one of the main green spaces in the metropolitan area—was excluded from MI-related analyses; however, this did not affect its inclusion in the remaining indicators. Finally, the total number and surface area of public green spaces were calculated for each MI category to determine their distribution in relation to social marginalization (Figure 3). (2024) and Browning et al. (2024). Although this measure is typically applied to urban parks, additional green space categories were included to provide a broader assessment of their area of influence on the population and housing (Figure 4). The indicator primarily focuses on the category of urban parks; therefore, for this category, the AGEBs located within the areas of influence of these polygons were identified, along with their corresponding marginalization index (MI). 30 Indicator No comprehensive information on urban tree canopy cover was available for the entire MASLP. Thus, based on the premise that each neighborhood should have at least 30% tree canopy cover, the percentage of public green space (PGS) cover was analyzed and used as a proxy to estimate tree canopy cover distribution, at the urban neighborhood scale (CONAPO, 2020). This calculation considered the contribution of tree canopy cover at the neighborhood level. For this analysis, the total surface area of urban green spaces was aggregated by neighborhood, and the percentage that it represents relative to the total area of each neighborhood polygon was calculated. Subsequently, descriptive statistics were generated for different ranges of vegetation cover percentages. Neighborhoods with values equal to or greater than 30% cover were considered to meet the indicator, and the marginalization index (MI) category of those neighborhoods was recorded. 3 Indicator For this parameter, it is established that at least three trees from public space should be visible from inside each dwelling. For its assessment, street tree data for the same study area collected by Ramírez-Castillo et al. (2024) were used, consisting of georeferenced tree data from 2022. These tree points were overlaid on the marginalization index (MI) map by AGEB from CONAPO (2020) in order to evaluate the “3” indicator across different MI levels. AGEBs with high and very high MI were excluded from this analysis due to the absence of street trees within these polygons. Therefore, the analysis focused on medium (M), low (L), and very low (VL) MI categories. Based on the number of trees within each MI category, the sample size was calculated for a finite population with a 95% confidence interval. Subsequently, 1,200 trees were selected, distributed across five different sampling sites for each MI category. Using Google Maps, dwellings from which at least three trees were visible were identified in order to assess compliance with the indicator. According to Browning et al. (2024), the use of street-view imagery provides a valid approach to assessing indicator 3 by manually counting visible trees. However, this method is subject to potential errors of omission and commission and is limited to observations made from ground level. Consequently, visibility conditions from upper-floor apartments or multi-story dwellings could not be directly evaluated. Likewise, Owen et al. (2024) highlighted that measuring tree visibility remains a methodological challenge, and standardized approaches for its assessment are still under development and discussion. Therefore, this study considered only ground-level observations and evaluated tree visibility within a 30 m radius of each dwelling, following the recommendations proposed by Browning et al. (2024) and Konijnendijk (2021). In this context, the Index of Houses with Tree Compliance (IHTC) is proposed as an alternative method for calculating the number of trees per dwelling, using prior knowledge of the number of dwellings and trees per polygon. The selection of sampling sites considered residential land use (IMPLAN, 2021), distance from the selected green spaces, and the presence of tree distributions in nodal configurations. This approach was intended to minimize differences associated with green space management and to focus specifically on the distribution of street trees. Afterwards, the sampled dwellings were recorded for each MI category, and those that reached the “3” indicator were counted. For each MI level, the IHTC was calculated, as shown in Equation 3.where SDI represents the number of sampled dwellings that met the indicator and TDR the total number of dwellings recorded per sampling site. The data were presented using box-and-whisker plots to illustrate the distribution of values according to levels of social marginalization and the number of dwellings with visibility of at least three trees. All statistical analyses were performed using the Real Statistics Using Excel resource pack Realease 9.8 by Zaiontz (2026) running on Microsoft Excel. Subsequently, the Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to evaluate the normality of the data for each study site. Then, a Kruskal-Wallis test was conducted to determine significant differences in IHTC values across MI categories, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test to identify differences between groups. The 3-30-300 rule and the luxury effect in the MASLP To assess the luxury effect of public green spaces in the MASLP, compliance with the 3-30-300 rule was compared across marginalization levels. For this analysis, only zones exhibiting some degree of compliance with the rule were included. The results of the ‘3 indicator’ were derived from the sample-based assessment. In the case of the ‘300 indicator’, only the proximity of urban parks was considered. Finally, the criteria were integrated to understand the compliance of the three indicators individually and collectively, regarding their marginalization index. A similar summary was implemented for Owen et al. (2024). Green space per capita in semi-arid cities at national and international scales Another indicator evaluated was per capita green space in semi-arid cities at both national (29 cities) and international (49 cities) scales. Data were compiled from scientific articles, government reports, and assessments by international organizations. Per capita green space values were compared with the value obtained in this study for the Metropolitan Area of San Luis Potosí, which reported 5.7 m2 per inhabitant. These values were presented in a bar chart by city. Additionally, cities were classified according to urban size: small (S) for cities with an area equal to or less than 100 km2, medium (M) for those between 100 and 300 km2, and large (L) for cities exceeding 300 km2. The Metropolitan Area of San Luis Potosí, with an area of 146.8 km2, was classified as a medium-sized city. The distribution of per capita green space values was also presented using box-and-whisker plots. Discussion Distribution and sufficiency of public green spaces The results reveal a highly uneven distribution of public green spaces across the Metropolitan Area of San Luis Potosí (MASLP), with a strong concentration in the municipality of San Luis Potosí and particularly around the historical urban core. Meanwhile, peripheral municipalities have not been provided with equivalent infrastructure during urban expansion processes. Similar spatial patterns have been reported in other metropolitan areas, where green infrastructure tends to be concentrated in central municipalities as a consequence of unequal urban planning processes (Ojeda-Revah, 2021; Cruz-Sandoval et al., 2020). The MASLP reached 5.6 m2 of public green space per inhabitant, a value slightly lower than that reported by Ramos-Palacios et al. (2024) due to methodological differences, but still below the minimum threshold recommended by the WHO (2015). This value is nevertheless comparable to those reported for other semi-arid Mexican cities such as Chihuahua, with 5.6 m2 per capita, and Monterrey, with 4.8 m2 per capita (Herrera-Correa and Romo-Aguilar, 2021; Jiménez et al., 2013). A defining characteristic of the metropolitan green infrastructure is its strong dependence on the Tangamanga I and II parks, which account for 92.7% of the total public green spaces. Although these large parks provide important ecosystem services and recreational opportunities, previous studies suggest that isolated large parks cannot substitute for a well-distributed and interconnected network of accessible green spaces (Rudd et al., 2002). In contrast, neighborhood parks and local gardens are more numerous but remain small, fragmented, and poorly connected, limiting ecological functionality (Kong et al., 2009), thermal regulation, and climate adaptation benefits (Aram et al., 2019). At the urban AGEB scale, only 24.2% of the area includes public green spaces -excluding private ones-, highlighting a lack of sufficient green infrastructure. Distribution patterns are heterogeneous, with some AGEBs lacking any vegetation cover across the metropolitan area. However, the polygons containing green areas mostly exhibited values below 10% of public green space within their total surface area. Once again, at this scale, the influence of the ‘Tangamanga Parks’ can be observed, where public green space cover reaches at least 70% within their corresponding AGEBs. In contrast, southeastern sectors show no public green space cover, largely due to industrial land use. Such urban development patterns reflect inequalities in access to urban greenery, as access depends on purchasing power rather than universal public provision—a phenomenon referred to as urban green grabbing (Fairhead et al., 2012). Furthermore, Mayen-Huerta (2022) notes that green space inequality is influenced by deliberate processes of privatization of urban green spaces. Performance of the 3-30-300 rule The evaluation of the 3-30-300 rule revealed substantial deficits across all three indicators. Regarding the proximity criterion, more than half of the metropolitan population lacks access to an urban park within 300 m of their residence. Areas meeting this requirement were concentrated in AGEBs characterized by low or very low social marginalization, whereas medium- and high-marginalization sectors generally lacked adequate access. These findings indicate significant socio-spatial inequalities in accessibility and support previous studies describing unequal green-space distribution as a form of environmental injustice (Leong et al., 2018; Almeida and Fonseca, 2025). Compliance with the 30% of tree canopy cover indicator was limited almost exclusively to areas influenced by the Tangamanga parks. This pattern highlights the concentration of urban vegetation within a small number of large public green spaces rather than throughout the urban fabric. This indicates a dependence on a few large green spaces and a lack of integrated green infrastructure at the neighborhood level. Wu et al. (2022) warn that such dependence may overestimate actual green space sufficiency when distribution and accessibility criteria are not considered. Once again, compliance was concentrated in areas with low or very low marginalization. Additionally, these results also showed that although the number of small green spaces is high, they are insufficient to achieve the minimum 30% cover established by the indicator. The ‘3 indicator’ also showed low compliance levels across the metropolitan area. The average of 0.63 trees per dwelling indicates that the polygons analyzed in the MASLP only reach the 21% of compliance with this indicator. It is important to mention, that this data considers only polygons with very low, low, and medium marginalization. Thus, polygons with high and very high marginalization levels are in inferior conditions regarding this indicator. Regarding the IHTC, fewer than half of the sampled dwellings met the minimum threshold, even within the least marginalized sectors. In contrast, in the medium social marginalization category, the indicator was met by only 20% of dwellings, with lower variation, likely associated with the generally lower tree abundance in this sector. Comparable limitations have been documented in cities with very different climatic conditions, including tropical environments such as Surakarta, Indonesia (Iqbal et al., 2025). These findings suggest that achieving this indicator may be challenging across diverse urban contexts and particularly difficult in arid and semi-arid environments characterized by low precipitation and high temperatures (Nathawat et al., 2025; Garau et al., 2025). Overall, the low levels of compliance observed for all three indicators indicate that achieving the 3-30-300 rule remains a considerable challenge in the MASLP. Even neighborhoods with favorable socioeconomic conditions failed to reach substantial compliance levels. This outcome appears largely associated with the metropolitan area’s dependence on a limited number of large parks whose benefits remain spatially restricted and insufficient to guarantee equitable green infrastructure provision across the urban territory. Socio-spatial inequalities and the luxury effect Across all indicators and spatial scales, compliance was consistently higher in areas with lower social marginalization. This pattern demonstrates that access to urban green infrastructure in the MASLP is strongly influenced by socioeconomic status and reflects the presence of the luxury effect. From the perspective of distributive environmental justice, urban settlements should be accompanied by a sufficient and equitable provision of public green spaces, especially in cities with low precipitation where access to urban greenery is limited (Chamberlain et al., 2020). The literature has described this phenomenon as the luxury effect in relation to green infrastructure, whereby environmental benefits are concentrated in sectors with greater economic capacity (Hope et al., 2003). In rapidly urbanizing municipalities of the metropolitan area, these disparities may become increasingly pronounced if green infrastructure provision does not keep pace with urban growth. This pattern is consistent with studies conducted in other arid and semi-arid cities (Chamberlain et al., 2020), where socioeconomic differences frequently shape the spatial distribution of urban vegetation (Herrera-Correa and Romo-Aguilar, 2021). Consequently, reducing environmental inequalities should be considered a central objective of future urban greening strategies within the metropolitan area. Planning and governance implications The findings have important implications for metropolitan planning and governance. The unequal distribution of green infrastructure, combined with the administrative fragmentation of the metropolitan area, suggests that improving public access to green space will require coordinated action among local governments. Although current planning instruments recognize the importance of green spaces, they generally lack mechanisms for intergovernmental cooperation and citizen participation in metropolitan-scale green infrastructure planning. In MASLP, it is necessary to increase the number of parks in the areas with current urban growth. Such new green spaces should be diverse, accessible and public. Other strategies should prioritize increasing tree canopy cover within existing public spaces and expanding street-tree networks throughout underserved neighborhoods. Such interventions may represent a cost-effective approach to reducing environmental inequalities while enhancing ecosystem services in areas with limited green spaces. Proactive planning measures may be critical to preventing the further consolidation of socio-spatial inequalities in access to urban green infrastructure. However, ensuring an adequate provision of public green spaces in semi-arid cities involves multiple sociopolitical challenges (Okour and Shaweesh, 2024). Previous studies have highlighted that the lack of urban green infrastructure planning mechanisms within local policy frameworks constrains the equitable distribution and sufficient availability of green spaces in semi-arid urban environments (Hyder et al., 2023). In this context, green infrastructure planning should be incorporated as a strategic component of urban development policies, particularly in rapidly expanding semi-arid cities. Accordingly, the integration of green infrastructure into urban planning instruments should be prioritized by local governments seeking to enhance urban sustainability and climate resilience (Graça et al., 2022). This is particularly important because, in addition to their environmental functions, green spaces contribute to urban cooling, reduce stormwater runoff and pollution, and improve water management (Wong et al., 2021). Comparable approaches have been proposed in Tehran, Iran, where priority areas for green infrastructure development were identified based on green-space deficits and climate vulnerability (Jamali et al., 2021). These experiences demonstrate that strategically targeted planning interventions can enhance green-space provision while contributing to reduce environmental inequalities in semi-arid cities. Challenges of the 3-30-300 rule in semi-arid cities According to the documentary review on the sufficiency of per capita green space in different semi-arid cities, none of the Mexican cities analyzed approached the minimum threshold of 9 m2 recommended by the United Nations. At the international level, only 36.7% recorded values above the recommended minimum. Differences among cities may be associated with multiple factors; however, the most influential are likely population density, available public space, socioeconomic conditions, and climatic and geographic variables. In this regard, the value proposed by the World Health Organization could be questioned, since cities in semi-arid contexts generally did not meet this threshold. However, from the perspective of the luxury effect, it is noteworthy that United States had the largest number of cities meeting the minimum required green-space standard, which may suggest an association with high GDP levels and urban planning capacity in that country. In contrast, cities located at lower latitudes tended to present lower per capita green-space values and lower GDP, indicating the luxury effect as a global phenomenon in semi-arid cities. Nevertheless, some cities in the Global South also exhibited high per capita green-space values, such as Gandhinagar, in India (Hassan and Jombach, 2025) and Al Baha, in Arabia Saudita (Mostafa and Alshahrani, 2024), where strong governmental efforts to provide sufficient green areas have been highlighted. This may indicate the substantial influence of the state in promoting urban greenery (Chaudhry et al., 2011). In particular, the two Indian cities mentioned above are recognized for implementing public green space provision projects. In other Indian cities, such as Kalaburagi and Bathinda, per capita values of 25 and 21.7 m2, respectively, have also been reported, again associated with governmental commitment to ensuring adequate green-space provision (Shekhar and Aryal, 2019). For Latin American cities with arid climates, Bakhtsiyarava et al. (2024) reported that per capita green space is up to 14% lower than in temperate and tropical cities within the same region. The same study also notes that arid cities are characterized by lower green-space sufficiency and greater fragmentation. Regarding the influence of city size, reported values were below 6.5 m2 per capita, while large cities showed a higher mean of 4.4 m2 compared with 2.6 m2 for smaller cities. This suggests that urban size may be related to per capita green-space values. Therefore, in the case of Mexico, the argument is reinforced that per capita green space varies primarily according to socioeconomic conditions, demographic factors, and governmental urban planning. In this analysis, San Luis Potosí ranks as a medium-sized city with one of the highest per capita green-space values in the country, second only to Apodaca, in Nuevo León. However, as indicated by the 3-30-300 rule, San Luis Potosí still exhibits a substantial deficit in green infrastructure and an unequal spatial distribution. Regarding international per capita values, they generally increased with urban land area, with large cities being the ones most likely to achieve the minimum threshold of 9 m2. Similarly, the main differences in green-space sufficiency appear to be associated with factors such as GDP per capita and demographic characteristics. In this context, San Luis Potosí again stood out for being above the mean value of 1.0 m2 for cities of similar size; however, its position well below the third quartile value of 8.5 suggests the need for the city to increase its per capita green space. Per capita green-space density is an emerging and widely used metric, and in the present study it made it possible to assess the 3-30-300 rule and the luxury effect in a semi-arid city. According to the specialized literature review, reports of these parameters are difficult to find in studies of semi-arid cities across different regions and latitudes. The luxury effect and the 3-30-300 rule are commonly examined in higher-latitude settings; however, green spaces in semi-arid cities are not directly comparable to those in temperate or tropical urban systems. Based on the values identified, semi-arid cities can reach and some even exceed the minimum of 9 m2 per inhabitant, but can the 3-30-300 rule be standardized for cities of any latitude and climate type? These questions deserve deeper investigation. Although public green spaces are anthropogenic in nature, the climate of semi-arid regions is both a natural condition and a limiting factor because these spaces require irrigation for several months of the year. Despite the economic investment this implies for maintenance, the establishment of native plant species adapted to semi-arid climates can help increase green-space density. Moreover, such increases can strengthen resilience to the impacts of climate change, especially in growing urban systems. The issue of green infrastructure density in semi-arid urban ecosystems remains a broad field for further research. The application of this knowledge can also provide strong support for decision-makers facing environmental emergencies and urban planning challenges in semi-arid cities. Conclusion The distribution of public green spaces in the Metropolitan Area of San Luis Potosí is uneven, being primarily concentrated in the southwestern sector of the city. In terms of density and spatial distribution of green infrastructure, ‘Tangamanga I and II’ urban parks dominate, even though neighborhood parks and local gardens are more numerous. Moreover, none of the indicators of the 3-30-300 rule were met at the metropolitan scale. Nonetheless, these measures showed better performance in areas surrounding the ‘Tangamanga’ parks and in zones with lower socioeconomic marginalization. This unequal distribution of green infrastructure is concentrated in areas with very low and low levels of marginalization, reflecting the presence of the luxury effect in the metropolitan area. According to the literature review, semi-arid cities in Mexico exhibit a general deficit in green space availability per capita, based on the indicator proposed by United Nations, and there is also a limited number of studies addressing this issue. At both international and national scales, larger cities tend to show higher average per capita green space values compared to medium and small cities, although with considerable variability. In this international context, some semi-arid cities with higher levels of economic development exceed the minimum threshold established by the United Nations. The Metropolitan Area of San Luis Potosí shows a per capita green space cover above the national and international averages. However, this result is largely driven by the presence of large urban parks, whose spatial distribution remains inequitable, indicating a clear luxury effect within the metropolitan area. The climatic conditions of semi-arid cities pose significant challenges for expanding green infrastructure, making it debatable to assess them using the same density standards applied to temperate or tropical cities. For the study area, there is a clear need to increase the density of public green spaces—particularly urban parks—and to ensure a more equitable spatial distribution across the urban territory. Additionally, further research is needed to expand accessibility assessments by incorporating factors beyond proximity. The application of this research line is crucial for informing urban planning decision-making and addressing the environmental challenges faced by semi-arid cities. This study represents a first effort to evaluate the 3-30-300 rule in a Mexican city and one of the few applications conducted in a semi-arid urban context. Although the assessment was limited by the lack of detailed information on urban tree canopy and by the challenges of evaluating tree visibility at the metropolitan scale, the results highlight the difficulties of implementing and monitoring this rule in cities where geospatial and green-space data remain scarce. The absence of comparable studies in Mexican and semi-arid cities further emphasizes an important research gap. In this context, per capita green-space indicators provided a useful benchmark for assessing green infrastructure sufficiency and for contextualizing the results obtained through the 3-30-300 framework. The findings also underscore the need for updated urban vegetation inventories, improved geospatial datasets, and standardized methodologies to facilitate the application and monitoring of this rule in semi-arid cities of the Global South. Future research should incorporate both public and private green spaces to provide a more comprehensive assessment of urban greenery and to better understand how inequalities in green infrastructure provision shape the social and environmental dimensions of the luxury effect in semi-arid cities.
