School of Architecture, Planning and Landscape, Newcastle University, Newcastle upon Tyne, United Kingdom Article metrics View details Abstract Vertical farming is commonly framed as an urban food-production or environmental technology, yet its role as a building-integrated socio-spatial design strategy in culturally specific residential contexts remains underexplored. In the UAE, the transition from detached Emirati public housing to vertical housing has improved land-use efficiency but reduced access to private family-oriented outdoor spaces associated with the traditional hosh. This study investigates how building-integrated vertical farming can be configured in Emirati vertical public housing to reinterpret hosh-like outdoor living, and how prospective users evaluate alternative configurations through immersive virtual reality. Using the Dibba Al Hosn public housing project in Sharjah, UAE, the research combines precedent review, technical integration assessment of water-reuse and irrigation strategies, case-study analysis, Building Information Modeling (BIM)-based scenario development, and immersive user evaluation. Three scenarios were developed: Integrated External Vertical Farming, Attached Vertical Farming, and Mixed Vertical Farming. They were visualized in the Enscape VR application and evaluated through semi-structured interviews with 30 Emirati prospective users aged 20–23. Findings show that acceptance was shaped by socio-spatial, cultural, and practical considerations, including privacy, usable outdoor space, greenery, family gathering, cultural compatibility, and maintenance expectations. The Mixed Vertical Farming scenario received the strongest support because it combined the protected planted edge of the integrated terrace with the more generous hosh-like qualities of the attached terrace. The study contributes to sustainable housing research by reframing vertical farming as a culturally responsive residential design strategy. Water reuse and irrigation are treated as enabling technical systems that support the design scenarios, while environmental benefits related to shading, cooling, and water efficiency are discussed as literature-informed potentials requiring future validation. 1 Introduction The rapid urbanization and increasing population growth in the UAE have intensified the demand for housing models that are both sustainable and socially responsive. Historically, Emirati public housing has been dominated by single-family detached dwellings with generous private outdoor spaces and ahwash (singular: hosh), which supported privacy, family life, social interaction, and environmental comfort. However, land scarcity and the need for more compact and sustainable urban development have accelerated a shift toward vertical housing (Kordi et al., 2025; Al-Kodmany, 2018). A prominent example of this transition is the Dibba Al Hosn vertical public housing project in Sharjah, one of the pioneering vertical public housing initiatives in the UAE. Despite its land-use efficiency and environmental potential, this new housing typology provides limited open and communal spaces of the kind that Emiratis traditionally associate with family well-being and social cohesion (UAESDGs, 2024). In this study, UAE affordable/public housing refers to government-supported Emirati public/citizen housing delivered through federal and emirate-level housing programs for eligible individuals and families. Official UAE housing support may include land allocation, free housing, housing loans, residential facilities, and maintenance services for deserving Emiratis, while the Sharjah Department of Housing provides housing support for citizen families in Sharjah through grants, loans, and government residential complexes (Sharjah Department of Housing, 2025). Recent Sharjah housing-support data also show that eligibility is not limited to one income category: housing grants are directed to applicants with monthly income below AED 25,000, while housing loans apply to eligible applicants with income equal to or above AED 25,000 (WAM, 2024). Accordingly, the term affordable/public housing in this paper describes a government-supported housing pathway for eligible Emirati residents. Recently, multi-storey apartment-based citizen housing has become part of public-housing provision alongside the traditional single-family housing typology. In Dubai, for example, the Mohammed Bin Rashid Housing Establishment’s Muhaisnah Fourth project includes 20 residential buildings with 464 apartments, while the Al Quoz Second residential building project includes 96 apartments (MBRHE, 2021; MBRHE, 2018). Dubai’s National Housing Policy under Dubai 2040 allocated AED 65 billion to support Emirati housing over 20 years and emphasizes integrated communities, green areas, public services, recreational facilities, and diverse residential options for citizens (Dubai 2040, 2021). In Sharjah, the Al Hosn Island/Dibba Al Hosn residential project includes 30 residential buildings and 424 apartments and was described as supporting family stability, privacy, and efficient land use (WAM, 2024). These examples confirm the importance of studying the emerging vertical public/citizen housing as an emerging UAE housing model (Shareef and Galal Ahmed, 2024). On the other hand, vertical farming (VF) has emerged as a promising response to land scarcity, food security concerns, and environmental sustainability through the integration of productive planting systems into urban buildings and residential settings. Using hydroponic, aeroponic, and aquaponic systems, VF can support year-round food production with substantially lower land and water demand than conventional agriculture (Blom et al., 2023). Within this housing context in this study, VF refers specifically to building-integrated cultivation systems embedded within Emirati vertical public housing, including planted terraces, façade-based farming elements, shared intermediate-floor farming spaces, and rooftop farming areas, rather than standalone commercial vertical farming facilities. Beyond production, however, existing literature suggests that building-integrated vertical farming may contribute to residential environmental quality through shading, visual greenery, thermal buffering, air-quality improvement, water reuse, and access to planted outdoor spaces. In the present study, these environmental benefits are treated as literature-informed potentials, while the direct empirical focus is on spatial integration and user acceptance (Zhang et al., 2024). In the Emirati context, the relevance of vertical farming extends beyond food production, particularly in relation to the loss of the traditional hosh in vertical housing. This shift raises questions about how privacy, greenery, outdoor family space, and social interaction can be reinterpreted within high-rise environments (Baroro, 2024). At the same time, large-scale VF integration in residential buildings remains constrained by technological, economic, and regulatory challenges, including initial investment, operational energy demand, irrigation requirements, and structural limitations (Zhang et al., 2024). Public perception and user acceptance are also critical in the UAE, where vertical housing is still associated with concerns about privacy, limited outdoor space, and social isolation (UAESDGs, 2024). Although recent studies have examined vertical farming as an urban food-production strategy and vertical housing as a response to urban densification, limited research has connected these areas within the socio-cultural context of Emirati public housing. Much of the existing vertical farming literature emphasizes technological systems, crop production, commercial applications, or environmental performance, while less attention has been given to how building-integrated planting systems may reshape residential outdoor space, cultural continuity, and user acceptance. Similarly, studies on vertical housing in the Gulf region have highlighted issues of privacy, family life, and the loss of traditional outdoor spaces, but have rarely examined vertical farming as a possible spatial response to these challenges. Even fewer studies have combined scenario-based design development with immersive VR evaluation to test how prospective users perceive future vertical housing alternatives before implementation. This study is positioned across four related bodies of research. First, vertical farming studies have increasingly examined building integration, façade-based farming, resource efficiency, energy-water-nutrient synergies, and environmental benefits, but they remain largely focused on technical performance and food-production potential rather than culturally specific residential outdoor space (Shi et al., 2025; Blom et al., 2023). Second, recent biophilic design research has emphasized the contribution of vegetation and nature-integrated architecture to human comfort, well-being, user satisfaction, and environmental quality, but has less often examined productive planting as part of culturally specific housing layouts (Tekin et al., 2025; Tabassum and Park, 2024). Third, recent productive landscape and urban agriculture research has highlighted the social, ecological, and governance value of food-producing urban spaces, but rarely in relation to Emirati public housing or the spatial loss of the hosh in vertical housing (Senthamizh and Anbarasan, 2025; Qiu et al., 2024). Fourth, recent participatory design research has shown that VR can improve user understanding, communication, and engagement in residential and architectural design processes, but few studies have combined BIM-based scenario development, immersive VR visualization, and Emirati user evaluation to assess future vertical housing alternatives before implementation (Lo et al., 2025; Shareef and Galal Ahmed, 2024). The novelty of this study, therefore, lies in connecting these strands by examining vertical farming as a culturally responsive socio-spatial design strategy for Emirati vertical public housing. The research problem addressed in this study is therefore not whether vertical farming can produce food in buildings, but how it can be spatially and technically adapted to Emirati vertical public housing as a culturally responsive residential design strategy. In particular, the study examines whether vertical farming terraces, planted edges, rooftop farming areas, and shared growing spaces can reinterpret hosh-like outdoor living within a vertical housing typology. This problem matters because the acceptance of vertical public housing in the UAE depends not only on land-use efficiency and sustainability goals, but also on the ability of new housing models to respond to Emirati expectations regarding privacy, family-oriented outdoor space, cultural familiarity, and long-term usability. This study makes three specific contributions. First, it reframes vertical farming in residential buildings as a socio-spatial design strategy rather than only as a food-production system. Second, it develops and compares three context-specific design scenarios for integrating vertical farming into the Dibba Al Hosn Emirati vertical public housing case. Third, it evaluates these scenarios through immersive VR-based participation with Emirati users, identifying the socio-spatial, cultural, and practical factors that shape acceptance. The technical discussion of water reuse and irrigation is presented as a technical integration framework that supports the design scenarios, while the Dibba Al Hosn case-specific technical application examines condensate recovery, irrigation distribution, nutrient conditioning, regulatory alignment, and an indicative capital-cost envelope. Full engineering validation, environmental simulation, and detailed cost assessment remain future research requirements. Beyond the UAE case, the proposed framework offers positive transferable implications for other GCC and hot-arid cities facing similar affordable/public-housing pressures, land scarcity, high cooling demand, limited residential outdoor space, and culturally specific privacy requirements, provided that its spatial and technical details are adapted to local housing policies, regulations, maintenance systems, and social norms. Building on this problem statement and contribution, the main aim of the study is to investigate how building-integrated vertical farming can be adapted to Emirati vertical public housing as a culturally responsive socio-spatial design strategy that supports hosh-like outdoor living and prospective user acceptance. The main research question is: “How can building-integrated vertical farming be configured as a socio-spatial design strategy in Emirati vertical public housing, and how do prospective Emirati users evaluate alternative configurations through immersive VR?” To address this question, the study follows five connected objectives. First, it identifies spatial approaches for integrating vertical farming into buildings through a review of relevant international precedents. Second, it develops a technical integration framework for residential vertical farming in hot-arid UAE conditions by assessing suitable vertical farming systems, water-reuse sources, irrigation strategies, nutrient-conditioning requirements, and practical constraints. Third, it examines the case-specific spatial opportunities and practical constraints of the Dibba Al Hosn vertical public housing case for vertical farming integration. Fourth, it develops three alternative design scenarios for integrating vertical farming into Emirati vertical public housing. Fifth, it evaluates prospective Emirati users’ perceptions and preferences regarding these scenarios through immersive VR, questionnaires, and semi-structured interviews. The study is guided by five subsidiary research questions: What spatial approaches to building-integrated vertical farming can be identified from international precedents? What technical integration requirements are needed to support selected vertical farming systems, water-reuse sources, and irrigation strategies in hot-arid Emirati residential buildings? What case-specific spatial opportunities and practical constraints does the Dibba Al Hosn vertical public housing project present for integrating vertical farming? How can alternative vertical farming scenarios be developed for Emirati vertical public housing? How do prospective Emirati users perceive and compare the proposed vertical farming scenarios through immersive VR? 2 Research method This study adopts a qualitative case-study approach to investigate the integration of vertical farming into Emirati vertical public housing as a socio-spatial design strategy. As shown in Figure 1, the methodology follows a sequential process linking the research objectives with corresponding methods and outcomes: literature review and precedent analysis, technical integration assessment, case-study analysis, BIM-based scenario development, case-specific technical application, and VR-based user evaluation. Source: Authors. 2.1 Literature review The first stage of the research involved a literature review, precedent analysis, and technical integration assessment. Selected international precedents in residential, commercial, and mixed-use buildings were reviewed to identify spatial integration typologies, architectural configurations, environmental strategies, and building-scale vertical farming approaches relevant to the scenario-development stage. In parallel, hydroponic, aeroponic, and aquaponic systems were reviewed in relation to residential integration in hot-arid contexts. Water-reuse and irrigation strategies, including rainwater harvesting, greywater recycling, and air-conditioning condensate recovery, were also assessed as enabling technical systems. The review focused on system suitability, water demand, maintenance complexity, residential integration, and relevance to the UAE context. This stage informed Section 3, where the literature review and technical integration framework are developed as the basis for the Dibba Al Hosn scenarios. 2.2 Case study: Dibba Al Hosn vertical public housing The second stage of the research focused on the Dibba Al Hosn vertical public housing project in Sharjah, UAE, as the principal case study. The project was selected because it represents the transition from traditional horizontal Emirati housing to vertical public housing and reflects the spatial and social challenges associated with this shift for Emirati families. The case-study analysis drew on archival review, architectural drawings, satellite imagery, Google Earth analysis, and site observations to examine the spatial organization of the buildings, communal spaces, privacy conditions, circulation areas, rooftops, balconies, façades, and intermediate floors. This analysis helped identify potential locations and constraints for vertical farming integration, including terrace, rooftop, shared-space, and service-infrastructure areas relevant to condensate collection, storage, irrigation distribution, maintenance access, and long-term management. Located in Dibba Al Hosn, Sharjah, the project was initiated by the Sharjah Housing Program in 2014 to provide suitable and environmentally efficient affordable/public housing for eligible Emirati citizen families. Official descriptions of the Island Al Hosn Residential Buildings project state that it provides housing services, uses available spaces efficiently, and offers facilities that support family stability in residential neighbourhoods (WAM, 2024). The first phase includes eight double residential buildings, each consisting of seven floors. Figure 2 presents the front and rear elevations of the case-study building, showing the multi-storey massing, façade organization, and external surfaces that informed the identification of potential façade and terrace VF integration zones. Each building accommodates fourteen apartments, with two apartments per floor, and each apartment covers approximately 315 m2. The ground floor includes shared amenities such as a reception lobby and a children’s play area. When completed, the wider project is expected to include 102 apartment buildings across reclaimed sea land and to house around 10,000 Emiratis, reflecting the use of vertical public housing as a response to land scarcity in Dibba Al Hosn (Shareef and Galal Ahmed, 2024). Figure 2 Front and rear elevations of Dibba Al Hosn Emirati public housing project in Sharjah. Source: Authors, based on Google Earth. The design addresses several socio-cultural requirements of Emirati family life. To support privacy, each residential building includes two separate entrances: one for men and another for women and families. Two of the four elevators are also allocated for women and families. Figure 3 presents the ground-floor and roof plans, showing the shared amenities, circulation areas, service zones, and rooftop areas that informed the analysis of shared-space and infrastructure opportunities. Each apartment is divided into three main zones: a men’s guest zone near the entrance, including the majlis, dining area, handwashing basins, and guest toilet; a family zone, including the living area, balcony, and bedrooms; and a service zone, including the kitchen and maid’s room. Figure 4 illustrates the typical apartment plan and clarifies the internal zoning, balcony locations, privacy arrangement, and possible private or façade-based VF locations. Despite these cultural considerations, the project still follows a conventional apartment model. Figure 5 shows the existing built condition and wider site context, illustrating the limited provision of shared open spaces and communal gathering areas, which may reduce social interaction and weaken residents’ sense of belonging. The model also does not fully compensate for the absence of traditional courtyards, or ahwash, which historically supported privacy, family life, and outdoor domestic activities in Emirati houses (Shareef and Galal Ahmed, 2023). These observations identified the main opportunities for vertical farming integration, including façade edges, balconies, private terraces, rooftops, and intermediate-floor shared spaces, as well as key constraints related to privacy exposure, circulation, structural loading, waterproofing, drainage, maintenance access, child safety, climate usability, and long-term management. These findings informed the scenario-development process presented in the following subsection. Source: Google Earth. 2.3 Development of vertical farming integration scenarios Based on the literature review, technical integration framework, and case-study analysis, three alternative vertical farming integration scenarios were developed for the Dibba Al Hosn case. These scenarios explored different strategies for integrating vertical farming into Emirati vertical public housing, including façade-integrated planting, attached terraces, rooftop farming, and shared intermediate-floor farming spaces. The scenarios were designed to represent different degrees of integration between private residential farming spaces and shared community-oriented farming areas. They were developed in BIM and rendered in the Enscape VR application to enable immersive visualization and comparative evaluation. A case-specific technical application was then developed to examine how water reuse, irrigation, nutrient conditioning, pilot-scale sizing, cost implications, and regulatory considerations could support the proposed scenarios. Rather than serving as fixed architectural or engineering solutions, the scenarios functioned as comparative design models through which spatial suitability, socio-cultural relevance, user acceptance, and practical implementation considerations could be assessed. To avoid the total farming area becoming a confounding factor in the user evaluation, the three scenarios were developed with an equivalent total productive VF area; therefore, participant preferences reflected differences in spatial configuration, privacy, outdoor usability, and hosh-like qualities rather than differences in the amount of farming space. The VF total area on each floor was calculated to assimilate the average corresponding area of the hosh in conventional single-family public housing models, as detailed later in Section 4. Figure 6 summarizes the scenario-development logic by showing how the existing baseline condition was translated into the three alternative design scenarios of Integrated VF, Attached VF, and Mixed Integrated/Attached VF, while retaining the full intermediate-floor and rooftop farming spaces as shared VF areas among all residents across the three scenarios. Source: Authors. 2.4 User evaluation: semi-structured interviews in VR The user-evaluation instrument consisted of a semi-structured interview guide administered in two stages. The first stage took place during the VR exploration, where participants were asked scenario-specific open-ended questions covering comfort, openness, privacy, greenery, similarity to the Emirati hosh, and the perceived role of shared spaces. The second stage, conducted after participants had viewed all scenarios, focused on overall preference, the reasons underpinning that preference, and reflections on the usefulness of VR for understanding the proposed housing environments. A short post-VR evaluation form was also used to record immediate participant assessments of each scenario. Participants were selected using purposive, criterion-based sampling (Etikan et al., 2015). This approach was appropriate because the selected case study is an Emirati public housing project, and public housing in the UAE is directed toward Emirati citizens. The study therefore required participants to be Emirati nationals. Participants aged 20–23 years were selected because this age range falls within the developmental period of emerging adulthood, during which marriage intentions, career planning, residential aspirations, and future family formation become increasingly salient. In the UAE, although the average age at first marriage among Emiratis is generally higher (approximately 26 years for women and 29 years for men), individuals aged 20–23 years are commonly engaged in higher education or early employment transitions and are therefore at a stage where preferences regarding future housing choices can be meaningfully assessed (Arnett, 2000). This age group was also relevant because vertical public housing remains a relatively new and unfamiliar housing model for Emirati families, while many older Emirati households are already established in detached villas or have accessed conventional public housing pathways, such as ready-built villas or residential land with government-supported loans for villa construction. All participants were living with their families in villas or detached Emirati family houses at the time of the study, reflecting the dominant Emirati housing pattern in which the hosh forms an important part of family life, privacy, and outdoor use. None of the participants had previous residential experience in vertical housing or apartment living. The final sample included 30 Emirati participants, consisting of 15 female and 15 male participants. This gender-balanced composition was intentional to reduce gender-related sampling imbalance and to ensure that both female and male perspectives on privacy, family use, outdoor space, and vertical housing acceptance were represented. The sample was therefore intentionally controlled in terms of nationality, age range, educational/current-status background, current housing background, and previous vertical-housing experience, while maintaining gender balance. This profile supports the study’s focus on prospective acceptance of future Emirati vertical public housing alternatives rather than post-occupancy evaluation by current apartment residents. The sample size was determined according to qualitative principles of data saturation rather than statistical representativeness. Interviews were conducted until additional sessions no longer yielded substantially new themes regarding preferred scenarios or the reasons behind those preferences. Recent guidance on qualitative sampling emphasizes that, for relatively homogeneous groups and focused research questions, core thematic saturation can often be achieved within approximately 12–18 interviews (Ahmed, 2025; Guest et al., 2006). In this context, 30 in-depth VR-supported interviews were considered adequate for the qualitative objectives of this case study. Each evaluation session followed the same structured sequence. Sessions began with an introduction explaining the shift from traditional horizontal to vertical housing, the purpose of the research, and the concept of integrating vertical farming with the Emirati hosh as a form of private outdoor family space. Participants were then shown rendered images of each scenario to clarify layout and key features before proceeding to the immersive phase. Participants subsequently explored the baseline model and three proposed scenarios through Enscape 360° visualizations viewed via Meta Quest VR headsets, enabling them to experience interior and exterior spaces and to assess spatial proportions, terraces, shared areas, and façade farming systems. Following the VR exploration, participants completed a structured questionnaire evaluating each scenario in terms of comfort, privacy, openness, similarity to traditional Emirati housing, outdoor-space usability, greenery integration, social interaction opportunities, and overall acceptability. Semi-structured interviews were then conducted to explore in greater depth the reasoning behind those responses. All participants were introduced to the same scenarios in the same order and received identical explanations. Interviews were subsequently transcribed and translated into English for thematic analysis. The interview guide was organized around three main areas: overall scenario preference, key factors influencing housing preferences, and participants’ perceptions of VR as a tool for understanding and comparing design alternatives. 2.5 Data analysis The data were analyzed using a combination of descriptive questionnaire analysis and thematic interview analysis. Questionnaire responses were reviewed descriptively to identify broad patterns of scenario evaluation and overall scenario preference. These questionnaire responses were not used for inferential statistical testing; rather, they provided descriptive support for interpreting the qualitative interview findings. Interview transcripts were analyzed through iterative thematic analysis. The process began with repeated reading of the translated transcripts, followed by open coding of statements related to participants’ perceptions of the baseline model and the three vertical farming scenarios. Initial codes were then grouped into broader themes through comparison across participants and scenarios. The final themes were developed by comparing repeated patterns in the interview data with questionnaire responses and scenario-preference results. These themes were not treated as predetermined outcomes; rather, they emerged through iterative comparison of participant responses while being interpreted in relation to the broader research focus on socio-spatial suitability, cultural relevance, and user acceptance. Initial coding was conducted by one researcher using a consistent coding framework. The lead researcher then reviewed the coding structure, checked the grouping of codes into broader themes, verified the selected representative statements, and refined the interpretation where necessary. Coding notes were organized to link participant statements with initial codes, broader themes, related scenarios, and design implications. To reduce researcher bias and improve credibility, all participants experienced the same baseline model and the same three scenarios through a common VR presentation sequence, the same semi-structured interview guide was used across all sessions, and questionnaire responses were used to triangulate the interpretation of interview themes. A formal intercoder-reliability test was not applied because the study did not involve multiple independent coders; however, consistency was strengthened through lead-researcher review, repeated transcript checking, comparison across cases, and systematic documentation of the coding process. Before the main data collection, the questionnaire, interview guide, and VR interaction flow were piloted with three participants to assess clarity, sequence, and comprehensibility. Minor refinements were then made to improve procedural consistency and content validity. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the United Arab Emirates University Social Sciences Ethics Committee – Research/Course, Al Ain, United Arab Emirates (Application No. ERSC_2025_8227; approved 16 November 2025). All participants were informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality, and data use, and provided informed consent prior to participation. Consent for publication was also obtained for the identifiable author shown in the VR demonstration image; no identifiable participant images are included in the manuscript. 3 Literature review for initiating a framework for the integration scenario development 3.1 Global case studies of vertical farming integration in residential buildings Building-integrated vertical farming has been explored through different architectural configurations, including planted façades, productive terraces, rooftop farms, attached farming modules, and shared growing spaces. Previous studies suggest that such systems may support food production, shading, water reuse, biodiversity, and social or recreational functions in dense urban environments, although these benefits depend on building type, climate, technology, and management strategy (Alam et al., 2023; Beacham et al., 2019; Al-Kodmany, 2018). To identify spatial approaches relevant to the Dibba Al Hosn case, selected global precedents were categorized into three integration typologies: Integrated External VF, Attached VF, and Mixed VF. These typologies form the conceptual basis for the three design scenarios developed in this study. 3.1.1 Integrated external VF typology Integrated external vertical farming refers to systems where vegetation and farming elements are embedded within the building envelope, including façades, terraces, and rooftops. In this approach, greenery becomes an architectural layer that contributes to shading, environmental performance, and visual quality, while reshaping the relationship between indoor and outdoor spaces. Three representative precedents illustrate this typology. The Oxygen Eco Tower represents a conceptual mixed-use high-rise that integrates vertical farming directly into the building envelope through green façades, rooftop gardens, and distributed private planting areas. The project combines vegetation with renewable energy systems, including photovoltaic panels and geothermal technologies, alongside rainwater harvesting and greywater recycling to support automated irrigation (Arketipo, 2015). While it demonstrates strong environmental integration and self-sufficiency, its spatial configuration emphasizes distributed greenery rather than clearly defined communal or socially active outdoor spaces. Thus, it highlights the environmental potential of façade-integrated systems but offers limited insight into residential outdoor living and social interaction. Similarly, Bosco Verticale is a widely cited example of façade-integrated vegetation in residential towers, where extensive planting is incorporated into balconies and terraces to create a vertical forest (Stefano Boeri Architetti, 2024; Greenroofs.com, 2019). The vegetation improves air quality, reduces urban heat island effects, and enhances thermal insulation through shading. Maintained through a centralized irrigation system using filtered rainwater, the project demonstrates the environmental and aesthetic benefits of integrating greenery into the building envelope. However, the spatial strategy primarily supports private outdoor use, with limited provision for shared or communal spaces. Therefore, although façade-integrated systems can enhance environmental performance and visual comfort, their contribution to collective outdoor living remains constrained. In a comparable approach, Tao Zhu Yin Yuan extends the concept of integrated external vegetation through a rotating tower form that accommodates extensive planting across private terraces (Vincent Callebaut Architectures, 2024). The building integrates automated irrigation systems using recycled rainwater and environmental sensors to optimize water use, while vegetation contributes to carbon sequestration, shading, and air purification. Similar to Bosco Verticale, the project emphasizes environmental performance and private access to greenery. However, the spatial organization remains primarily oriented toward individual units, offering limited opportunities for shared outdoor experiences. This reinforces a broader pattern in integrated external systems, where environmental benefits and private greenery are prioritized over socio-spatial integration. 3.1.2 Attached VF typology Attached vertical farming refers to systems that are physically connected to buildings but remain structurally and spatially distinct from the primary building envelope. These systems often take the form of external modules, greenhouses, or vertical farming units, allowing for flexibility in implementation without major modifications to the building structure. To examine this approach, two representative case studies are discussed. The Live Share Grow project proposes a conceptual residential tower incorporating attached hydroponic farming systems supported by greywater recycling and aquaponics (Martella, 2012). The project integrates waste-to-energy strategies, including blackwater combustion systems, to enhance resource efficiency and support agricultural production. While the project demonstrates advanced technological integration and resource circularity, its spatial configuration treats vertical farming primarily as a productive system rather than an extension of residential living space. As a result, the link between farming systems and everyday residential use remains limited, with less emphasis on outdoor usability, comfort, or social interaction. In contrast, the Home Farm project presents a more socially integrated approach, combining residential living with vertical aquaponic farming and rooftop planting areas (SPARK Architects, 2016). The design encourages resident participation in food production and incorporates shared farming spaces that promote social interaction and community engagement. The use of inclined farming walls enhances solar exposure while providing shading benefits to the building envelope. Supported by rainwater harvesting and greywater recycling, the project demonstrates how attached vertical farming can extend beyond production to support social sustainability. Compared with other attached systems, it more effectively links farming with residential life through shared spaces and participatory activities. 3.1.3 Mixed VF typology The mixed vertical farming scenario represents the highest level of integration, combining multiple approaches within a single development. This includes façade-integrated systems, rooftop farming, shared communal spaces, and interior agricultural areas. Such configurations allow buildings to function simultaneously as residential, productive, and social environments. To explore this approach, four representative case studies are analyzed. The Agro-Housing project integrates a high-rise residential tower with a multi-level vertical greenhouse, combining private housing with large-scale agricultural production (Knafo Klimor Architects, 2015). The system employs drip irrigation supported by greywater recycling and rainwater harvesting, alongside renewable energy systems. Importantly, the project incorporates shared farming areas and rooftop gardens, enabling social interaction alongside food production. This dual integration of private and communal spaces reflects a shift toward vertical farming as both an environmental and socio-spatial system. Similarly, the Vertical Farm / Living Hybrid project in London adopts a circular economy approach, integrating residential, commercial, educational, and agricultural functions within a single system (Hēta Architects, 2023). Farming spaces are combined with educational facilities and community markets, supported by closed-loop irrigation and nutrient systems. This project extends the role of vertical farming beyond residential use to include public engagement and knowledge exchange. Its multi-functional spatial configuration highlights the potential of vertical farming to act as an urban infrastructure that connects social, economic, and environmental systems. In a comparable large-scale development, the South Beach project integrates green roofs, vertical gardens, and landscaped terraces throughout a mixed-use complex (BluPrint, 2020; BCA Singapore, 2016). Supported by automated irrigation systems using rainwater harvesting and greywater recycling, the development enhances environmental performance while providing accessible green spaces for social and recreational use. Unlike purely residential examples, it shows how multi-level greenery can support both environmental regulation and public life. Finally, the Charleston Vertical Farm project combines rooftop farming, interior vertical farming floors, and community-oriented spaces within an urban building (Clemson University, 2011). The integration of hydroponic, aeroponic, and aquaponic systems is supported by automated irrigation, water recycling, and environmental monitoring technologies. In addition to food production, the project includes shared facilities that support community interaction and engagement. This multi-layered integration shows how vertical farming can operate as a productive, environmental, and social system within urban buildings. Contemporary systems rely on artificial lighting, environmental control, and automated nutrient delivery to reduce water use and chemical inputs while improving plant growth. For residential integration, however, system selection must also consider spatial compatibility, maintenance requirements, and user acceptance, not productivity alone. Hydroponics is the most widely used vertical farming system and involves growing plants in nutrient-rich water without soil (Alam et al., 2023). It enables precise control of environmental conditions and is highly adaptable to indoor and vertical configurations (Sathyanarayan et al., 2023). Common systems include Nutrient Film Technique (NFT), drip systems, Deep Water Culture (DWC), and wick systems (Velazquez-Gonzalez et al., 2022). Among these, NFT and drip systems are particularly suitable for vertical farming buildings due to their efficiency, modularity, and controlled nutrient delivery (Nelson et al., 2025). Hydroponics is water-efficient, compatible with automation, and suitable for a wide range of crops, particularly leafy greens and herbs (Garzón et al., 2023; Rani et al., 2022). However, it requires continuous monitoring of nutrient balance and depends on reliable technical management. Overall, hydroponics offers the best balance between efficiency, technical complexity, and operational feasibility for residential vertical farming integration. Aeroponics is a soilless system in which plant roots are suspended in air and sprayed with a nutrient-rich mist (Garzón et al., 2023). It is one of the most water-efficient systems and enhances plant growth through increased oxygen exposure and efficient nutrient delivery (Regmi et al., 2024). Aeroponic systems typically rely on pumps, misting nozzles, sensors, and controlled environments, making them suitable for compact and technologically advanced vertical farming applications (Roshitha, 2024). Despite these advantages, aeroponics requires high technical precision, continuous operation, and stable environmental conditions. Its dependence on equipment performance and maintenance increases system complexity and cost (Regmi et al., 2024). Although aeroponics offers strong water efficiency, its use in residential buildings may be limited where low-maintenance systems are preferred. Aquaponics combines hydroponics and aquaculture in a closed-loop system where fish waste provides nutrients for plant growth, while plants filter and recycle the water (Sowmya et al., 2024; Ramchiary et al., 2022). This system reduces chemical input and improves water efficiency through nutrient recycling (Monsees et al., 2022). However, it requires careful management of water quality, oxygen levels, fish health, and biological balance (Goddek et al., 2015). As a result, aquaponics is more complex than hydroponics and demands continuous monitoring. In residential contexts, it offers environmental and educational value, but is more suitable for shared or community-based systems than individual units. Hydroponics, aeroponics, and aquaponics differ in water use, technical complexity, and suitability for residential integration (Garzón et al., 2023; Goddek et al., 2015). Hydroponics provides the most balanced option because it is efficient, scalable, and comparatively manageable. Aeroponics offers greater water efficiency and fast growth, but requires advanced technology and continuous monitoring. Aquaponics provides a sustainable closed-loop system, but involves higher biological and maintenance complexity. For vertical residential buildings, the key challenge is not only integrating farming systems into buildings, but adapting them to socio-spatial expectations, maintenance capacity, and hot-arid climatic conditions. In this regard, hydroponic systems supported by efficient irrigation and water-reuse strategies represent the most feasible option for residential integration. Table 2 summarizes the comparison. It identifies water-reuse, irrigation, nutrient-conditioning, plant-selection, and implementation considerations that may support the design scenarios developed later in the paper. The framework is literature-informed and is not intended as a full engineering validation. Instead, it provides the general technical logic for assessing which water sources and irrigation strategies are most relevant for residential vertical farming in the UAE context. The case-specific application of this framework to Dibba Al Hosn, including conceptual condensate sizing, cost implications, and regulatory alignment, is presented in Section 4. 3.3.1 Water supply systems for residential vertical farming In hot-arid regions such as the UAE, water availability is one of the main technical challenges for vertical farming integration. This study considers three alternative water-supply approaches: rainwater harvesting, greywater recycling, and air-conditioning condensate recovery. These systems differ in reliability, treatment requirements, cost implications, and suitability for irrigation in arid residential environments (Kumar et al., 2023). Rainwater harvesting collects rooftop runoff for non-potable uses such as irrigation and can reduce potable water demand where sufficient rainfall and storage capacity are available (Maha Abdel, 2023; Campisano et al., 2017; Carter and Fowler, 2008). However, its effectiveness in the UAE is limited by low rainfall and strong seasonal variability. Greywater recycling provides a more consistent water source by reusing domestic wastewater from showers and sinks, and studies show that it can reduce freshwater demand by up to 45.5% when used for irrigation (Al Khoury et al., 2023). However, treatment is required to remove contaminants such as surfactants and pathogens, and its use for edible crops may be limited unless advanced treatment is applied (Kumar et al., 2023). For this reason, greywater is retained in this study as a comparative non-potable reuse strategy rather than the selected supply source for the edible hydroponic model. Air-conditioning condensate recovery captures water produced by cooling systems, with typical production rates ranging from approximately 10–70 L per unit per day depending on climate, system operation, and humidity (Ahmed et al., 2026; Alom et al., 2021). In the UAE, where mechanical cooling is widespread, condensate recovery can provide a useful supplementary irrigation source, although it depends on cooling demand and requires treatment before use. Overall, rainwater harvesting is low-energy but rainfall-dependent, greywater recycling is relatively reliable but treatment-intensive, and condensate recovery is a feasible supplementary source in mechanically cooled buildings. Accordingly, the case-specific application in Section 4.2 uses air-conditioning condensate recovery as the selected supplementary water source for the proposed hydroponic irrigation model, while treated greywater remains a potential supplementary option subject to treatment and regulatory approval. In vertical residential buildings, irrigation systems should ensure efficient water distribution, nutrient balance, and operational control while minimizing water loss and maintenance. For hydroponic and building-integrated vertical farming, drip irrigation and closed-loop fertigation are among the most suitable approaches because they allow controlled water delivery, pH adjustment, and electrical conductivity management. Fertigation systems inject water-soluble fertilizers into the irrigation line and may include sensors to monitor EC, pH, and water flow, helping maintain stable nutrient delivery (Imbernón-Mulero et al., 2023). In smaller systems, manual nutrient mixing may also be used, but regular EC and pH monitoring remains necessary to avoid nutrient imbalance and maintain plant growth. 3.3.3 Literature-informed environmental impact considerations Beyond water provision, the environmental implications of each water-reuse strategy should be understood as literature-informed considerations rather than measured outcomes of this study. Previous life-cycle assessment studies indicate that energy use, infrastructure, and system operation can strongly influence the environmental performance of water-reuse systems. Systems based on waste recovery, renewable energy, and water conservation may provide broader environmental benefits when properly designed and managed (Al Khoury et al., 2023). In hot-arid residential contexts, greywater recycling may reduce freshwater demand by reusing wastewater generated within the building, while condensate recovery can make use of water that would otherwise be discarded from cooling systems. For the proposed case-study model, condensate recovery appears more directly compatible than rainwater harvesting because it captures a relatively clean water stream already generated by mechanically cooled buildings. Greywater remains environmentally relevant as a broader non-potable reuse strategy, but was not adopted in the present edible hydroponic model because of its stricter treatment and regulatory implications. By contrast, rainwater harvesting in the UAE is constrained by low rainfall and storage limitations. Therefore, greywater reuse and condensate recovery appear more relevant than rainwater harvesting for residential vertical farming in the UAE, although their environmental performance requires future validation through water-balance modeling, life-cycle assessment, and post-occupancy monitoring. 3.3.4 Implementation and integration challenges The integration of vertical farming systems in residential buildings involves technical, environmental, regulatory, and managerial challenges. Rainwater harvesting systems are limited by seasonality and storage requirements, while greywater recycling requires treatment, source separation, monitoring, and public acceptance (Al Khoury et al., 2023; Carter and Fowler, 2008). Condensate recovery systems also require storage, conditioning, and continuous monitoring, as water production depends on environmental conditions and air-conditioning operation (Mammana, 2023). Successful implementation, therefore, depends on coordinating water storage, treatment, conditioning, and distribution systems within building infrastructure while also ensuring compliance with water-quality standards, maintenance responsibilities, and long-term operational feasibility. These issues are especially important in residential settings, where system simplicity, safety, and ease of management strongly influence practical adoption. 3.3.5 Nutrient enrichment of recycled condensate water Condensate water contains very low levels of dissolved minerals and nutrients and therefore cannot normally function as a complete irrigation or hydroponic solution without conditioning (Matarneh et al., 2024). Its slightly acidic pH may also require adjustment depending on crop requirements (Ayers and Westcot, 1985). Hydroponic nutrient-solution guidelines typically focus on nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. Reported concentrations vary by crop type, growth stage, and irrigation method, but approximate indicative ranges such as 20–100 mg·L−1 N, 5–20 mg·L−1 P, and 20–80 mg·L−1 K may be used as starting points for many vegetables and ornamental plants (Savvas and Gruda, 2018). Nutrient supplementation may be provided through automated fertigation or manual dosing, while regular EC and pH monitoring is necessary to avoid nutrient imbalance. Basic management measures should therefore include filtration, covered storage, periodic cleaning, EC and pH monitoring, and controlled low-dose nutrient supplementation before irrigation. 3.3.6 Suitable plants for UAE climatic conditions The UAE’s hot-arid climate requires plant selection to consider heat tolerance, water efficiency, salinity, maintenance, and compatibility with hydroponic or controlled-environment systems. Not all crops are equally suitable for vertical farming because of root depth, structural support, and space limitations (Kumar et al., 2023; FAO, 2018). Native and drought-tolerant species such as Ghaf (Prosopis cineraria), date palm, neem (Azadirachta indica), Acacia species, and bougainvillea are suitable for façade greening, landscape integration, and ornamental vertical systems in arid environments (FAO, 2018). By contrast, productive hydroponic systems commonly rely on herbs and leafy greens such as rosemary, thyme, basil, mint, lettuce, spinach, and kale because of their fast growth and adaptability (Savvas and Gruda, 2018). Fruiting crops, including tomatoes, eggplant, strawberries, cucumbers, peppers, and beans, can also be cultivated hydroponically, but require greater structural support and more intensive nutrient management. Overall, plant selection for UAE residential vertical farming should prioritize climate resilience, water efficiency, hydroponic compatibility, manageable maintenance, and the spatial and structural constraints of vertical residential buildings. These technical considerations inform the Dibba Al Hosn case-specific application in Section 4.2, including the condensate-recovery model, conceptual pilot-scale sizing, preliminary cost envelope, implementation constraints, and regulatory alignment. It does not provide a full engineering validation; rather, it translates the technical framework into a case-specific application. The aim is to connect the spatial proposals with practical requirements for condensate collection, storage, filtration, nutrient conditioning, irrigation distribution, maintenance access, cost, safety, and regulatory alignment. 4.2.1 AC condensate recovery and irrigation model The proposed model uses air-conditioning condensate as a supplementary irrigation source for the selected case study. Condensate produced by the air-handling units is collected and conveyed to an underground storage tank, from which it is redistributed to each floor’s terrace and, where applicable, to the attached farm building. Two pipe networks are required: one for condensate collection and transfer to storage, and another for irrigation distribution to the farming areas. A conditioning point may be integrated before final distribution to enable filtration, nutrient supplementation, and pH adjustment where required. In this framework, condensate is not treated as a directly ready-to-use irrigation source, but as a relatively clean source of water that must be conditioned into a controlled hydroponic nutrient solution. The model limits the use of greywater for edible hydroponic systems because greywater requires advanced treatment, strict safety controls, and regulatory approval for food-related applications. Therefore, the proposed model prioritizes condensate collection, storage, conditioning, and irrigation distribution, while retaining treated greywater as a possible supplementary option subject to further validation (Figure 14). Source: authors. 4.2.2 Conceptual block-level sizing and system components A conceptual block-level sizing was conducted for the proposed condensate-based irrigation model. A Dibba Al Hosn residential block accommodates 14 apartments. Under a conservative assumption of one captured condensate-producing source per apartment and using the condensate yield range cited in this study of 10–70 L per unit per day, one block could recover approximately 120–840 L/day, or 0.12–0.84 m3/day, of condensate. As an indicative crop-water benchmark, peer-reviewed hydroponic lettuce data report yields of 41 ± 6.1 kg/m2/year and water demand of 20 ± 3.8 L/kg, equivalent to approximately 2.25 L/m2/day. For conceptual design, a conservative allowance of 3.0 L/m2/day was adopted. On this basis, 100 m2 of productive area requires approximately 0.225–0.30 m3/day, 150 m2 requires 0.337–0.45 m3/day, 200 m2 requires 0.449–0.60 m3/day, and 300 m2 requires 0.674–0.90 m3/day. These values suggest that condensate can support a pilot of around 100–200 m2 under medium-to-high recovery conditions. In contrast, larger productive areas would require either near-peak condensate generation or a supplementary source such as treated greywater. The proposed pilot is therefore sized conceptually around five elements: (1) condensate collection and transfer from AC units, (2) 2–3 m3 of covered equalization/storage capacity, (3) mechanical filtration and UV disinfection, (4) nutrient conditioning with EC/pH monitoring, and (5) a dedicated non-potable drip/fertigation network serving private terraces and shared farming spaces. The system should be treated as a non-potable condensate-based network in which water quality is verified before use and lines are clearly identified and protected against backflow. The productive VF area was extracted from the BIM model and converted into gross irrigation demand. Because the three scenarios were intentionally designed to have an equivalent total productive VF area, the water-demand values are identical across the Integrated External VF, Attached VF, and Mixed VF scenarios. The baseline model has no VF productive area and no VF-related irrigation demand. In the retrofit condition, the standardized productive VF area includes 410 m2 on each of the seven residential levels, 1,067 m2 on the shared intermediate farming floor, and 1,067 m2 on the rooftop farming area, giving a total productive VF area of 5,004 m2/block. Using the literature-derived hydroponic irrigation benchmark of approximately 2.25 L/m2/day and the conservative design allowance of 3.0 L/m2/day adopted in this study, the gross irrigation demand is estimated at 11,259–15,012 L/day/block, or approximately 11.26–15.01 m3/day/block. This calculation clarifies the order of magnitude of the proposed intervention while confirming that detailed water-balance modeling and engineering validation remain future research requirements. 4.2.3 Preliminary capital-cost envelope Capital and operating costs for residential VF water systems vary according to system scale, tank size, collection layout, filtration components, pumping needs, treatment requirements, and maintenance strategy. Rainwater harvesting costs depend mainly on storage and rainfall patterns, while greywater recycling requires treatment, filtration, and water-quality monitoring. AC condensate recovery may involve moderate operating costs depending on collection layout, pumping demand, treatment requirements, and the building’s cooling profile (Matarneh et al., 2024; Campisano et al., 2017). Therefore, the cost discussion in this study should be read as preliminary contextual framing rather than as a contractor-level feasibility assessment. The present study introduces a rough-order-of-magnitude (ROM) capital-cost envelope for the condensate-recovery and irrigation infrastructure. As a Gulf-region benchmark, a condensate-recovery feasibility study at KAUST reported an indicative capital cost of approximately USD 210,000 for a four-building pilot, including collection sumps and pumps, storage tanks, UV/filtration, and irrigation tie-in, while noting that costs should be verified by local contractors. A simple one-quarter normalization gives approximately USD 52,500 per building; applying a ± 25% early-stage uncertainty band yields a practical ROM envelope of approximately USD 39,375–65,625 per Dibba Al Hosn residential block, or approximately AED 145,000–241,000. This envelope should be interpreted as a pre-tender pilot estimate for condensate-recovery and irrigation infrastructure. It excludes structural reinforcement, façade-support modifications, crop-lighting systems, detailed energy modeling, and long-term operating expenditure. In addition to this preliminary capital-cost envelope, economic feasibility would depend on recurring operating costs, including maintenance labor, filter replacement, pump and sensor servicing, nutrient supply, plant replacement, water-quality testing, pest control, and cleaning of shared farming areas. These recurring costs are especially relevant because participants repeatedly identified maintenance responsibility and long-term management as concerns during the VR-based evaluation. The Mixed Vertical Farming scenario may require more coordinated implementation and management because it combines private terrace planting with shared intermediate-floor and rooftop farming areas; however, the present study does not provide a scenario-by-scenario cost comparison. Its economic implications would depend on total planted area, planter type, structural support, screening and shading systems, irrigation layout, maintenance model, and the extent to which shared infrastructure can be centralized. Future economic feasibility studies should therefore evaluate both financial cost and socio-spatial value, including cultural acceptance, privacy, outdoor-space usability, and resident satisfaction. 4.2.4 Regulatory alignment and implementation considerations In the UAE regulatory context, the use of non-potable water for irrigation is supported in principle but is subject to water-quality control and plumbing separation requirements. At the federal level, Annex No. (1) to Cabinet Resolution No. (39) of 2021 establishes treated-wastewater standards for reuse and limits irrigation uses to non-potable applications, while prohibiting reuse for drinking, kitchen, bathing, swimming, and the irrigation of vegetables and fruits consumed by humans (UAE Government, 2021). Although this study focuses on AC condensate recovery and conditioned non-potable irrigation rather than municipal treated wastewater, these standards provide a benchmark for water-quality control and end-use restrictions. Abu Dhabi’s Recycled Water and Biosolids Regulations 2021 require recycled water supplied for reuse to meet prescribed quality criteria and to be distributed with zero risk of contact with potable water (Department of Energy Abu Dhabi, 2021). Abu Dhabi’s Water Supply Guide 2024 further requires that rainwater, recycled water, greywater, and other non-potable water to be clearly protected against backflow (Department of Energy Abu Dhabi, 2024). In Dubai, Al Sa’fat 2023 requires some additional practices for ensuring the safety and protection of the recycled wastewater and AC condensate, such as dual plumbing, non-potable labeling, air-gap separation, and treatment where greywater systems are installed (Dubai Municipality, 2023). Publicly accessible Sharjah municipal information also indicates the presence of treated irrigation water networks relevant to infrastructure planning (Sharjah City Municipality, 2025). Accordingly, the proposed irrigation model should be understood as a code-aligned condensate-based non-potable irrigation framework, subject to project-specific approval, water-quality verification, and utility/municipal coordination in Sharjah, particularly if intended for edible crops rather than ornamental planting or non-direct-contact irrigation. Practical implementation would also require assessment of structural loading from saturated planters, growing media, water tanks, occupants, shading elements, and safety barriers, particularly on private terraces, shared intermediate floors, and rooftop farming areas. Waterproofing, drainage, overflow control, leakage prevention, and maintenance access would need to be coordinated with the architectural and building-services design. Operationally, the proposed system would require regular inspection of pumps, filters, pipes, sensors, pH and electrical conductivity levels, nutrient dosing, plant replacement, pest control, and cleaning of shared farming areas. Long-term management responsibility would also need to be clearly defined. While private terraces may involve partial resident responsibility, shared intermediate-floor and rooftop farming spaces would require building-level management, a caretaker, or a specialist maintenance provider. These requirements indicate that the proposed system should be understood as a technical integration framework that requires future structural, MEP, regulatory, and operational validation before construction. The baseline model generated widespread dissatisfaction across both groups. Female participants described the existing balcony as spatially inadequate for family use and lacking meaningful privacy: “I feel there is no privacy because the place is open and tight.” Male participants framed deficiencies in relation to traditional housing standards, noting that Emirati families expect large outdoor yards. Scenario 1, Integrated External VF, was valued mainly for privacy, greenery, and room coverage because the long terrace extended along the apartment rooms and created a planted protective edge. Several participants felt that the trees improved privacy by closing off views; one female participant explained, “The trees increase privacy because they close off views, so I can relax more.” However, this scenario was less preferred overall because the long terrace could feel narrow and less suitable for family gatherings. Participants also criticized the hydroponic units when placed in the middle of the circulation zone, with one male participant stating, “The greenery is soothing to the eye and gives privacy, but I prefer to reduce the hydroponics in the middle.” Scenario 1 was therefore perceived as effective for privacy and greenery, but weaker for gathering and flexible outdoor use. Scenario 2, Attached VF, was valued because it provided one larger attached outdoor area that felt closer to the traditional Emirati hosh. Female participants described it as “spacious, not tight,” while male participants emphasized the value of one larger area by stating, “One wide space is better than many small areas around the apartment.” However, despite its hosh-like character and stronger gathering potential, Scenario 2 was not the majority preference because some participants felt it was less fully private than Scenario 1, since it did not cover all rooms in the same way. Participants therefore recommended additional trees, side screening, or operable mashrabiya-style elements to improve privacy. Scenario 3, Mixed VF, was the clear majority preference among both female and male participants. It was preferred because it combined the strengths of the first two scenarios: the privacy and protective planted edge of Scenario 1, and the larger hosh-like outdoor space of Scenario 2. Participants described it as more balanced and more culturally compatible because it supported privacy, family gathering, greenery, shared use, and similarity to the traditional hosh. One female participant stated, “I feel like I am living in a regular house with a hosh.” Male participants also supported the scenario because it could accommodate gender-segregated gatherings. However, participants recommended improving the shared intermediate floor by moving hydroponic elements to the perimeter and adding seating, coffee corners, and children’s zones. Source: Authors. 6 Discussion This research suggests that integrating VF into residential buildings in hot-arid regions may help reintroduce outdoor living spaces, privacy gradients, and semi-private family areas into vertical housing environments. In the Emirati housing context, the traditional courtyard historically served as a climatic moderator, social space, and private outdoor area. Vertical farming terraces, rooftop gardens, and shared farming floors can therefore be interpreted as a vertical reinterpretation of selected courtyard functions, allowing vertical housing developments to translate some spatial and social characteristics of traditional Emirati housing while responding to land scarcity and urban densification. The use of immersive VR also represents a methodological contribution. In contexts where future housing typologies do not yet exist as built references, as is the case with vertical farming integration in Emirati housing, VR-based participatory evaluation can elicit informed user feedback that conventional drawings, photographs, or physical models may not provide. Participants reported that VR helped them assess spatial proportions, privacy conditions, and outdoor usability more clearly than images alone. This aligns with emerging literature on VR as a participatory design instrument (Shareef and Galal Ahmed, 2024) and suggests that immersive evaluation methods can support pre-design community consultation in the UAE and similar Gulf contexts. The literature review demonstrated that global vertical farming integration projects such as Bosco Verticale, Tao Zhu Yin Yuan, and Agro-Housing are often discussed in relation to food production, environmental performance, thermal comfort, biodiversity, and social interaction within buildings. Previous studies suggest that integrating vegetation into buildings may improve thermal insulation, reduce cooling loads, enhance air quality, and contribute to urban biodiversity and environmental sustainability (Zhang et al., 2024; Blom et al., 2023; Al-Kodmany, 2018). However, the present study did not directly measure these environmental effects through simulation, monitoring, or performance assessment. Instead, it extends the literature by showing that, in a socio-culturally specific context such as Emirati housing, the perceived value of vertical farming integration is evaluated primarily through social and spatial functions rather than agricultural productivity or measured environmental performance. Participants assessed the proposed scenarios as outdoor living environments, reinforcing the argument that residential vertical farming should first be designed as a socio-spatial strategy, while environmental performance should be validated through future technical studies. The preference distribution clarifies how participants evaluated the three design strategies. Scenario 3 was selected by 21 out of 30 participants, showing a clear majority preference for the mixed approach, while Scenarios 1 and 2 received lower and relatively similar levels of support. This pattern suggests that neither privacy alone nor hosh-like spatial generosity alone was sufficient. Scenario 1 offered stronger privacy through planted room coverage but was limited by its narrow linear form, while Scenario 2 offered a larger hosh-like terrace but required additional privacy treatment. Scenario 3 was preferred because it combined both qualities within one spatial strategy. The case study analysis of Dibba Al Hosn revealed that one of the major challenges of vertical housing in the UAE is the loss of traditional outdoor spaces such as courtyards, which historically supported family gatherings, planting, and daily activities. Previous research on Emirati public housing has shown that social sustainability is strongly linked to privacy, family interaction, and outdoor space availability (Shareef and Galal Ahmed, 2024). The e-participation findings strongly support and deepen this conclusion. Privacy emerged as the single most influential factor shaping scenario acceptance across both male and female participants, with vegetation alone considered insufficient, particularly at night, and architectural screening elements such as operable mashrabiya-style screens consistently recommended as necessary complements to planting. The gendered patterns of preference identified in this study carry direct implications for the spatial programming of vertical farming terraces in Emirati housing. Female participants consistently prioritized the cultural legibility of outdoor space as a hosh analogue and its capacity to support family gathering, while male participants foregrounded spatial efficiency, climate-specific usability, and the technical adequacy of privacy screening beyond vegetation alone. These differences suggest that a single undifferentiated terrace typology is unlikely to satisfy both groups simultaneously, and that future design frameworks for Emirati vertical housing should consider gender-responsive spatial differentiation, including the option of gender-segregated gathering zones within shared spaces, as an important design parameter rather than a secondary consideration. This finding extends existing literature on gender and space in Gulf housing contexts by showing that gendered spatial preferences remain important when traditional housing expectations are translated into vertical environments. The technical framework identifies hydroponic systems supported by drip irrigation or closed-loop fertigation as the most adaptable option for residential integration, while greywater recycling and air-conditioning condensate recovery emerge as more relevant water-reuse strategies than rainwater harvesting in the UAE because rainfall is limited and seasonal. The selected case-study model proposes air-conditioning condensate recovery as a supplementary irrigation source, while treated greywater remains a possible supplementary option subject to treatment and regulatory approval. The Mixed Vertical Farming scenario, which received the strongest overall support, also presents the most complex irrigation demand because it combines private terrace planting, shared intermediate farming floors, and rooftop growing areas. This suggests that automated drip irrigation, centralized water conditioning, and clear maintenance access are important for supporting the social acceptance of the preferred scenario, particularly by reducing the maintenance burden on residents. Without reliable, low-maintenance irrigation infrastructure, the maintenance concerns identified by participants could undermine the long-term viability of the most socially preferred design. This finding reinforces the importance of integrating technical and socio-spatial design considerations from the earliest stages of vertical housing development rather than treating water management as a secondary engineering concern (Matarneh et al., 2024; Al Khoury et al., 2023). The research also identified practical implementation challenges related to capital cost, irrigation maintenance, nutrient management, water treatment, building infrastructure integration, and long-term management responsibility. Participants expressed concerns regarding maintenance responsibility, insects, cost, and shared-space management, indicating that vertical farming integration should be supported by clear maintenance strategies and dedicated operational frameworks rather than relying on residents alone. The preference expressed by multiple participants for a building-level specialist to oversee shared farming areas reflects a broader pattern in community-based urban agriculture, where collective maintenance is more sustainable when supported by institutional structures rather than voluntary resident participation (Martin et al., 2016). These findings should be interpreted in relation to the methodological, technical, and environmental-performance limitations of the study. The findings also have policy and spatial-equity implications for UAE affordable/public housing. The proposed VF extensions should be understood as government-supported design alternatives for public housing, not as retrofits that individual residents are expected to fund. Since these projects involve structural additions, rooftop and intermediate-floor farming spaces, waterproofing, safety systems, condensate recovery, filtration, pumps, irrigation networks, and long-term maintenance, the main infrastructure should be planned and supported by housing authorities. The three proposed scenarios can help government decision-makers understand which spatial configurations future users may prefer before implementation. Residents would mainly use and cultivate the private and shared farming spaces provided, while government agencies, municipalities, NGOs, or community organizations could support participation through incentives, starter kits, seedlings, training, farming guidance, and maintenance support. This is important to prevent retrofit and maintenance costs from being transferred unfairly to residents, especially limited-income beneficiaries. Planning guidelines should also define privacy screens, shaded terraces, safe rooftop and intermediate-floor farming areas, drainage, waterproofing, non-potable irrigation systems, maintenance access, and management responsibilities. Shared farming spaces should have clear usage rules to ensure fair access for women, men, children, elderly residents, and different household types. These rules should clarify access schedules, booking systems, gender-sensitive use, children’s safety, crop ownership, and maintenance responsibility. In addition to improving greenery and outdoor use, VF may also support small-scale food production through herbs and vegetables, but actual crop yield, household savings, and food-security benefits should be tested through future pilot projects. The findings of this study may be analytically transferable to other GCC and hot-arid contexts where vertical housing, land scarcity, high cooling demand, limited outdoor space, and culturally specific privacy expectations shape residential design. However, transferability should not be understood as direct generalization. The Emirati hosh has specific cultural meanings related to family life, privacy, gendered use, hospitality, and outdoor domestic activities, and comparable courtyard or outdoor-space traditions may differ across the Gulf region. Therefore, applying the proposed vertical farming scenarios in other GCC or hot-arid contexts would require adaptation to local family structures, privacy norms, housing policies, building regulations, water-reuse standards, maintenance systems, and climatic conditions. The study’s contribution is therefore most relevant as a transferable design approach: using building-integrated vertical farming to reinterpret culturally valued outdoor living spaces in vertical housing, while adjusting spatial, technical, and regulatory details to each local context. This study has three main limitations. First, the research was based on a single case study, the Dibba Al Hosn vertical public housing project in Sharjah, and on an intentionally controlled participant sample of Emirati participants aged 20–23. Although this sample was appropriate for exploring the perceptions of a young generation likely to encounter future public housing options during household formation, it does not represent all Emirati age groups, household types, marital statuses, or socio-economic profiles. Future research should therefore examine additional housing typologies, emirates, age groups, and family structures to further validate the proposed vertical farming integration strategies. Second, the user evaluation was conducted through immersive VR-based scenarios rather than through built or occupied vertical farming environments. Although VR enabled participants to compare spatial proportions, privacy relationships, terrace size, greenery, and outdoor-space usability before implementation, it could not reproduce the full sensory and operational conditions of everyday residential use. In particular, the VR evaluation could not simulate thermal comfort under hot-arid outdoor conditions, wind movement, humidity, solar glare, acoustic conditions, smells from planting or irrigation systems, insects, dust, irrigation noise, or the day-to-day effort required to maintain plants, pumps, filters, nutrients, and shared farming areas. It also could not represent temporal changes such as night-time privacy, seasonal heat variation, plant growth, plant decay, or long-term shared-space management. Therefore, the VR findings should be interpreted as perception-based evidence of spatial acceptance rather than proof of long-term residential performance. Future studies should combine VR evaluation with pilot installations, living-lab experiments, environmental monitoring, and post-occupancy assessment. Third, the technical framework requires future validation. The study identifies water-reuse options, irrigation concepts, plant-selection considerations, conceptual sizing, cost implications, and practical implementation constraints, but it does not provide full engineering validation. Detailed structural load calculations, system sizing, crop-yield estimation, energy analysis, code-compliance review, water-quality testing, cost–benefit analysis, operational maintenance planning, and long-term environmental performance simulation remain necessary before implementation. Future research should therefore integrate structural analysis, building-services design, local regulatory review, lifecycle assessment, economic evaluation, environmental simulation, pilot testing, and post-occupancy assessment to evaluate the technical and financial viability of vertical farming systems in hot-arid residential buildings. From a design perspective, the findings of this research suggest that vertical housing developments in the UAE should incorporate multi-level outdoor spaces rather than relying solely on internal corridors and enclosed apartments. Vertical farming terraces, rooftop gardens, and shared sky courts may function as spatial and environmental buffers, social spaces, and semi-private outdoor areas that support perceived residential quality. The results also indicate that privacy should remain a primary design consideration, and vertical farming elements should be combined with shading devices, screens, and planting buffers to maintain visual privacy. Furthermore, integrating vertical farming systems with building water-reuse systems, particularly greywater reuse and air-conditioning condensate recovery, may support water efficiency in hot-arid climates, although detailed water-balance, environmental-performance, and cost assessments remain necessary before implementation. 7 Conclusion This study investigated the potential integration of vertical farming systems into vertical residential buildings in hot-arid regions, with a particular focus on the UAE context. The research aimed to develop alternative vertical farming design scenarios suitable for Emirati vertical housing and to evaluate their architectural, socio-spatial, and technical water-reuse implications through literature-informed technical review and e-participation using immersive virtual reality environments. To achieve this aim, the research first reviewed global vertical farming projects and technologies to identify suitable systems for hot-arid climates. Based on this and the technical framework, three alternative scenarios were developed for the Dibba Al Hosn case study: Integrated External, Attached, and Mixed Vertical Farming. These scenarios were modeled using BIM and visualized through Enscape 360° and VR simulations, allowing participants to experience the spaces at a human scale. The results from the e-participation sessions indicated that vertical farming integration is socially acceptable when it supports outdoor living, privacy, greenery, and cultural housing patterns. Privacy, outdoor-space usability, family gathering, and similarity to the traditional hosh were the most important factors influencing participant preferences. Scenario 1, the Integrated External Vertical Farming scenario, was valued for privacy because its long terrace covered the main rooms and created a protective planted edge, but it was considered narrow for family gatherings. Scenario 2, the Attached Vertical Farming scenario, was appreciated for its resemblance to the traditional Emirati hosh because it provided one larger attached outdoor area for seating, planting, and family use, although participants felt that additional privacy treatment would be required. Scenario 3, the Mixed Vertical Farming scenario, received the strongest overall support because it combined the privacy benefits of the integrated terrace with the hosh-like qualities of the attached terrace. From a technical perspective, the study provides a literature-informed and case-specific assessment of water-reuse and irrigation requirements for the proposed VF scenarios. The analysis suggests that air-conditioning condensate recovery, supported by filtration, storage, nutrient conditioning, and drip/fertigation systems, could help support pilot-scale VF irrigation in hot-arid housing. Rainwater harvesting and greywater recycling remain relevant comparative or supplementary options, but their use depends on rainfall availability, treatment requirements, water quality, regulatory approval, and long-term maintenance. Therefore, the technical findings should be understood as a conceptual integration assessment rather than full engineering validation. Future technical work should test detailed system sizing, structural loading, waterproofing, drainage, MEP coordination, water-quality performance, maintenance requirements, and cost feasibility. While the VR-based evaluation empirically validated prospective resident preferences, particularly the stronger acceptance of the Mixed VF scenario, environmental outcomes such as façade shading, microclimatic improvement, cooling-load reduction, energy reduction, and life-cycle benefits remain potential benefits that should be tested through future simulation, field monitoring, and post-occupancy evaluation. Future research should follow a multi-dimensional agenda. First, environmental studies should quantify cooling performance, façade-shading effects, energy reduction, evapotranspiration, microclimatic comfort, and seasonal usability under UAE hot-arid conditions. Second, technical studies should test structural loading, waterproofing, drainage, MEP integration, non-potable water separation, nutrient conditioning, irrigation reliability, and system performance across seasons. Third, economic studies should compare capital costs, operation and maintenance costs, affordability impacts, and possible subsidy models for public housing. Fourth, social research should expand the participant profile beyond the current 20–23 prospective-user cohort to include broader age groups, married couples, families with children, elderly residents, current public-housing applicants, and residents from different emirates. Fifth, policy research should examine planning regulations, maintenance governance, usage rights for shared spaces, and incentive mechanisms that can support equitable implementation in UAE affordable/public housing. Overall, the study suggests that vertical farming can add value to Emirati vertical housing when designed as a culturally responsive outdoor living system rather than as a purely technological add-on. The mixed scenario performed best as it combines private and shared spaces and can be linked to building-scale water reuse strategies. The main implication is that future vertical housing in hot-arid regions should integrate farming, shading, water reuse, and socio-cultural spatial patterns within a unified design framework.
