We sampled bees and hoverflies along a semi-natural–suburban–urban gradient in the Poznań city to examine how urbanization influences taxonomic and functional community structure and patterns of beta diversity differ between pollinator taxa. Semi-natural habitats supported the highest bee and hoverfly abundances and species richness and, for bees, the greatest alpha diversity, indicating strong negative impacts on diversity of local assemblages. Nonmetric multidimensional scaling revealed significant shifts in community composition for both groups, with hoverfly communities showing stronger separation among urbanization levels than bee communities, suggesting greater sensitivity of hoverflies to urban environmental change. Functional responses differed between taxa: in bees, functional dispersion declined in urban sites, indicating trait clustering and environmental filtering, whereas in hoverflies, functional richness was markedly reduced in suburban and urban habitats, pointing to the loss of larval and adult trait diversity. Partitioning of beta diversity revealed taxon-specific patterns in both taxonomic and functional dimensions. Overall, our findings show that urbanization restructures pollinator communities through taxonomic and functional reorganization, with responses differing between bees and hoverflies. The observed beta diversity patterns suggest that urbanization is associated with taxon-specific patterns of differentiation. Because our study is based on one city in Poland, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to other urban systems. Maintaining a mosaic of semi-natural, suburban, and urban green spaces that provides diverse floral resources, nesting substrates, and larval microhabitats will be essential to conserve pollinator diversity in increasingly urban landscapes. Biodiversity Entomology Urban Ecology Ecology Urban Sociology Ecosystems Urban Pollinator Ecology and Conservation Introduction Human-induced changes to landscapes play a pivotal role in the substantial loss and fragmentation of natural habitats (McKinney 2002 ; Grimm et al. 2008 ). This transformation has far-reaching implications for both biodiversity and the functioning of ecosystems (Fischer and Lindenmayer 2007 ; Cardinale et al. 2012 ). The global-scale conversion of natural habitats into urban or agricultural landscapes by human activities has been widespread (Seto et al. 2011 ), leading to shifts in patterns of species diversity and the provision of ecosystem services (Dirzo et al. 2014 ). Pollinating insects have emerged as a flagship group for raising public awareness of the impacts of human activities on biodiversity (Fortel et al. 2014 ). Pollinators play a vital role in maintaining the balance of ecosystems by facilitating the reproduction of flowering plants through the transfer of pollen. Urbanization exerts complex effects on pollinator communities and their traits, leading to a filtering process shaped by various ecological and anthropogenic factors. Habitat fragmentation and loss in urban areas reduce the availability and diversity of floral resources, favouring disturbance-tolerant plant species. These altered plant communities, often dominated by non-native or invasive species or shaped by inappropriate management practices, influence the availability of resources for pollinators and consequently the traits favoured within pollinator assemblages. The physical barriers posed by urban structures, such as buildings and roads, along with the artificial nature of the environment, collectively contribute to the selective pressures on pollinator species, ultimately shaping the composition and traits of pollinator communities in urban ecosystems (Baldock et al. 2015 ). Understanding these processes is essential for developing effective conservation strategies and promoting biodiversity in urban ecosystems (Aronson et al. 2017 ). As cities become increasingly urbanized, many organisms are deprived of natural habitats, which may result in biodiversity loss (Czech et al. 2000 ; Grimm et al. 2008 ; Evans et al. 2009 ), leading to biotic homogenization, which causes the extinction of a large portion of the species pool and the spread of only a limited number of species (McKinney 2002 ). However, many both plants’ and animals’ species can also benefit from using the city as an alternative habitat (Sattler et al. 2011 ; Dyderski et al. 2017 ), including species that are endangered on a regional scale (Aronson et al. 2014 ; Ives et al. 2016 ). Pollinator diversity and their communities within the city border are influenced by urban matrix variables such as urbanization level or connectivity (Von Der Lippe et al. 2020 ) and local landscape variables such as patch size, a cover of bare soil, a cover of plant species, plant species richness, and vegetation height (Dylewski et al. 2019 ; Felderhoff et al. 2023 ). Pollinator assemblages are filtered by urbanization, favoring species with traits adapted to life in the city while others are still avoiding it (Blair 1999 ; Banaszak-Cibicka and Żmihorski 2012 ; Persson et al. 2020 ; Gathof et al. 2022 ). For example, it was found that polylectic, eusocial bee species with a long flight period benefit in urban areas (Banaszak-Cibicka and Dylewski 2021 ). In contrast, hoverflies showed the opposite effect, where more species were associated with habitats with a low degree of urbanization due to traits related to larval development (Gathof et al. 2022 ). Additionally, different parts of the city have varying levels of impervious surface area, which affects pollinator functional diversity (Banaszak-Cibicka and Dylewski 2021 ). The assessment of anthropogenic landscapes’ impact on pollinators community assembly can be effectively conducted by examining measures of beta diversity (Verboven et al. 2014 ; Banaszak-Cibicka and Żmihorski 2020 ; Tsang et al. 2025 ). Beta diversity describes variation in species composition among sites and provides insight into how biological communities change across environmental gradients (Whittaker 1960 ). Beta diversity plays a pivotal role in connecting spatial diversity patterns at local scales (alpha-diversity) with the overall regional species pool (gamma-diversity) (Tsang et al. 2025 ). This metric is instrumental in examining how community composition changes along environmental gradients and can provide insight into patterns potentially associated with biotic homogenization, as highlighted by Anderson et al. ( 2011 ) and Cornell and Harrison ( 2014 ). Within communities, changes in beta diversity can manifest through two distinct processes, turnover and nestedness (Baselga 2010 ). Spatial turnover involves the replacement of certain species by others, while spatial nestedness indicates the non-random loss or gain of species, resulting in some communities being subsets of others. In the classical sense, biotic homogenization refers to a cross-level pattern in which urban communities become nested subsets of more species-rich rural or semi-natural assemblages, reflecting directional species loss rather than replacement (Olden et al. 2004 ; Olden and Rooney 2006 ; Persson et al. 2020 ) A complementary within-level question is whether sites sharing the same urbanization class converge in community composition and whether this convergence is driven by species replacement (turnover) or by ordered species loss (nestedness). The aim of this study was to assess how urbanization influences taxonomical and functional alpha and beta diversity patterns of bee and hoverfly communities across semi-natural, suburban, and urban habitats. Specifically, we aimed to characterize how the relative contributions of turnover and nestedness to taxonomical and functional beta diversity shift along the urbanization gradient, and whether these within-level compositional patterns differ between bee and hoverfly communities. Unlike cross-level approaches that test whether urban assemblages are nested subsets of semi-natural ones (Persson et al. 2020 ), we focus on within-level variation to reveal how sites of similar urbanization class differ from one another. By comparing two key pollinator taxa with contrasting ecological strategies, we sought to identify taxon-specific responses to urban land-use change and to disentangle whether community reassembly along the urbanization gradient is driven primarily by species loss (nestedness) or species replacement (turnover). We hypothesized that (I) pollinator abundance, species richness, and taxonomical and functional alpha diversity would be highest in semi-natural habitats and decline with increasing urbanization, reflecting environmental filtering and the dominance of disturbance-tolerant species in urban areas, (II) urbanization significantly modifies taxonomical and functional beta diversity of pollinator communities, with suburban and urban habitats compared to semi-natural habitats, indicating that community reassembly along the gradient is driven primarily by species and trait replacement rather than ordered species loss, and (III) bees and hoverflies exhibit contrasting taxonomical and functional responses to urbanization due to differences in ecological traits and life-history strategies. Materials and methods The research was conducted within the city of Poznań, Poland (52º17′34″–52º30′27″ N, 16º44′08″–17º04′28″ E), which covers a total area of 261.9 km² and has 540,146 residents. Additional sampling was carried out in adjacent semi-natural areas (Fig. 1 ). Fig. 1 Full size image Maps show the location of urban, suburban and semi-natural study sites within Poznań, Poland, surrounded by different levels of impervious surface area Fieldwork was carried out over two seasons, from June to August in 2022 and 2023, focusing on the collection of pollinating insects using entomological nets. To capture the urbanization gradient, the sampling sites were selected across three habitat categories: urban (high degree of urbanization), suburban (medium degree), and semi-natural (low degree). The level of urbanization for each site was quantified using the Impervious Surface Area (ISA) index, applying the following thresholds: low (0–15%), medium (16–50%), and high (51–100%). A total of 30 sampling sites were established: 10 in urban green areas, 10 in suburban zones, and 10 in semi-natural habitats (Fig. 1 ). Within each habitat category, the specific locations were randomly generated from a pool of 30 preselected sites. The 10 urban sites consisted of intensively managed lawns and small greenery patches located in front of apartment blocks, characterized by frequent mowing, ornamental plantings, and a high proportion of impervious surfaces. The 10 suburban sites were situated in other urban green areas (i.e. backyard gardens in districts with detached houses and green wedges), typically subject to moderate management and containing a mixture of maintained and semi-natural vegetation. The 10 semi-natural sites were located on the outskirts of the town and consisted mainly of meadows, characterized by low management intensity, a high proportion of native flora, and minimal impervious surface cover. Pollinators, including bees (Apiformes) and hoverflies (Syrphidae), were collected at regular four-week intervals from June to August in each of the two study years (2022–2023). Specimens were captured using sweep-netting, performed between 10:00 and 16:00 under suitable weather conditions: air temperature above 20 °C, light wind (less than 20 km/h) or calm air, and cloud cover not exceeding 50%. To standardize sampling effort and sampled area among sites, all surveys were conducted along a fixed 200 × 1 m transect (200 m²) at each sampling site, and exactly 300 sweep-net strokes were performed during each sampling session (Dylewski et al. 2019 ). The same sampling protocol was applied at all sites and in both study years. Sweep-netting was conducted systematically along the transect under standardized weather conditions, thereby keeping the sampled area and sampling effort constant among habitat categories and sampling occasions Collected insects were pinned in the laboratory and subsequently sorted and identified to the species level. Bee and hoverfly traits Life history traits were compiled for each pollinator species based on a comprehensive literature review to enable functional diversity assessment. For bees, the following traits were extracted: mean body length, nesting site (cavity, soil, or hive), social behavior (solitary, eusocial, or cleptoparasitic), floral specificity [oligolectic (single plant species or genus) or polylectic (multiple plant species)], flight initiation period (March/April, May/June, or July/August), flight termination period (May/June, July/August, or September/October), and total flight period duration (months of activity) extracted from following literature: (Banaszak 1993 , 2000 ; Scheuchl 1995 ; Schmid-Egger and Scheuchl 1997 ; Banaszak et al. 2001 ; Pesenko et al. 2002 ; Pawlikowski and Celary 2003 ). For hoverflies (Syrphidae), trait data comprised mean body length, flight period (months of activity), migratory status (present or absent), and larval food type (zoophagous, aquatic saprophagous, or terrestrial saprophagous) extracted from Saure ( 2018 ) and Speight et al. ( 2013 ). Statistical analysis All statistical analyses and data visualizations were conducted in R version 4.5.2 (R Core Team 2025 ). All calculations were performed on the combined dataset of bees and hoverflies (Syrphidae) collected across the 2022 and 2023 seasons for each sampling site. To assess differences in the community structure of bees and hoverflies among study areas, nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) was performed using the vegan package (Oksanen et al. 2025 ). Diagnostic tests for normality of residuals, overdispersion, and homogeneity of variance were performed using the DHARMa package (Hartig 2022 ) with the simulateResiduals() function. To determine differences in specimen abundance, number of species (hereafter species richness), alpha diversity, functional alpha diversity parameters across three urbanization levels (semi-natural—low urbanization; suburban—medium urbanization; urban—high urbanization), generalized linear models (GLMs) were fitted using the glm() function and analyzed with ANOVA using the Anova() function to obtain Wald Chi² or F statistics. Multiple comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s post hoc test via the emmeans() function. A negative binomial distribution was applied to specimen abundance data for both bee and hoverfly assemblages, as well as to hoverfly species richness. A normal distribution was used for the remaining response variables, including bee species richness, alpha diversity indices, functional diversity parameters. Alpha diversity (α-diversity) for each pollinator group was quantified using the Shannon–Wiener index, calculated using the diversity() function in R using vegan package (Oksanen et al. 2025 ). Functional diversity was assessed using four complementary indices: functional richness (FRic), representing the amount of functional space occupied by the species assemblage; functional dispersion (FDis), describing the extent of species trait variation in functional space; functional divergence (FDiv), expressing the average distance of each species from the center of mass (centroid) of the functional trait space; and functional evenness (FEve), indicating the regularity of biomass distribution in niche space. These indices were calculated using the FD package (Laliberté and Legendre 2010 ), employing the dbFD() function. To determine differences in taxonomical and functional beta diversity across three urbanization levels, we used the same procedure as above. A normal distribution was used for the beta diversity metrics. Taxonomic and functional beta diversity (β-diversity) was assessed using the Jaccard dissimilarity index based on a presence–absence matrix, computed with the beta . pair() function. The components of β-diversity (total β-diversity, nestedness, and species turnover) were calculated using the betapart package (Baselga and Orme 2012 ). Functional beta diversity was quantified using species’ functional-trait information to account for differences in community trait composition among sites. A species-by-trait matrix was compiled from the functional traits described above, and pairwise functional distances among species were calculated using Gower’s distance, which accommodates mixed trait types. Functional beta diversity was then calculated using a trait-based dissimilarity approach and partitioned into total functional β-diversity and components associated with functional turnover and nestedness. Additional packages used for analysis and visualization included: ggplot2 (Wickham 2016 ), ggeffects (Lüdecke et al. 2021 ), car (Fox and Weisberg 2019 ), and emmeans (Lenth 2025 ). Overall, semi-natural areas supported the highest values of bee abundance, species richness, and alpha diversity, as well as hoverfly abundance and species richness, indicating that less disturbed habitats provide more suitable conditions for diverse and abundant pollinator assemblages. These findings align with our expectation that urbanization can negatively affects pollinator communities by reducing habitat quality and resource availability (Dylewski et al. 2024 ; Banaszak-Cibicka et al. 2025 ). However, our results show that within-level beta diversity patterns did not show a uniform response across urbanization levels or pollinator groups. These findings align with the growing recognition that the ecological consequences of urbanization cannot be reduced to a simple diversity-loss or homogenization narrative. Rather, urbanization may affect community assembly through multiple pathways, including environmental filtering, habitat heterogeneity, and species-specific ecological responses (Lokatis and Jeschke 2022 ; Sexton and Egerer 2026 ). Bee abundance was significantly higher in semi-natural habitats compared to suburban sites, whereas differences between semi-natural and urban areas, as well as between suburban and urban areas, were not statistically significant. Bee alpha diversity was also highest in semi-natural habitats, reinforcing the idea that semi-natural ecosystems support more diverse bee communities, whereas urban and suburban habitats may favor a subset of tolerant species (Banaszak-Cibicka and Żmihorski 2012 ; Banaszak-Cibicka and Dylewski 2021 ). Notably, suburban sites had the lowest mean bee abundance, richness, and diversity, indicating that they do not necessarily represent intermediate conditions along the urbanization gradient. Hoverflies showed a comparable but more consistent response to urbanization. Both their abundance and species richness were significantly higher in semi-natural habitats and lower in suburban and urban areas with no differences between the latter two. This suggests that, like bees, hoverflies may benefit from environmental conditions characteristics of semi-natural areas and may be sensitive to habitat transformation. However, unlike bees, hoverfly alpha diversity did not show differences among habitats, despite significant difference in abundance, richness, and community composition. One possible explanation is that hoverfly communities may be dominated by a few abundant generalist species across all urbanization levels, potentially masking underlying compositional shifts (Bates et al. 2011 ). Urbanization also significantly altered the functional diversity of pollinator communities, although its effects were restricted to a subset of functional metrics and differed between bees and hoverflies. Bee communities exhibited significantly reduced functional dispersion in urban and suburban habitats compared to semi-natural areas, indicating a reduced spread of co-occurring species in functional trait space and potentially greater functional clustering in these habitats. Such a pattern is consistent with the possibility of stronger environmental filtering in urban environments, whereby urban conditions may disproportionately exclude species possessing traits poorly suited to human-dominated environments. However, previous studies indicate that functional responses of bee communities to urbanization are context-dependent with both reductions and increases in functional dispersion (Buchholz and Egerer 2020 ; Banaszak-Cibicka and Dylewski 2021 ). Previous studies have also shown that functional traits such as sociality, dietary breadth, nesting strategy, and phenology can influence bee responses to urbanization (Geslin et al. 2013 ; De Palma et al. 2015 ; Banaszak-Cibicka and Dylewski 2021 ; Liang et al. 2023 ). Urban and suburban habitats may therefore favor assemblages with greater functional similarity among co-occurring species, as suggested by the lower functional dispersion observed in our study, This could potentially alter the range of functional roles represented within individual sites and consequently, the capacity of bee communities to provide diverse pollination functions (Oliver et al. 2015 ; Campbell et al. 2022 ). Importantly, the lack of significant differences in functional richness, divergence, and evenness suggests that urbanization did not reduce the overall range of functional traits present, nor did it substantially alter the distribution of abundances within the occupied trait space. This may indicate that although some species are lost in more urbanized habitats, some aspects of functional diversity are maintained despite taxonomic differences, potentially indicating partial functional compensation among species with similar traits. Hoverflies showed a stronger functional response to urbanization, with functional richness being significantly higher in semi-natural habitats than in suburban and urban areas. This indicates a pronounced reduction in functional trait diversity in suburban and urban habitats relative to semi-natural habitats, potentially reflecting the loss or reduced representation of species with more specialized ecological requirements and differences in larval and adult resource availability. Urban landscapes often lack the structural and compositional heterogeneity necessary to support this functional diversity, leading to assemblages dominated by widespread, eurytopic species with flexible larval requirements (Meyer et al. 2009 ; Bates et al. 2011 ). Previous studies have shown that hoverfly functional diversity is closely linked to habitat complexity, vegetation structure, and the availability of larval microhabitats, all of which tend to decline with urban development (Sommaggio 1999 ; Meyer et al. 2009 ; Bates et al. 2011 ). Our results highlight that urbanization affects not only pollinator abundance and diversity but also community composition. The stronger response observed in hoverflies compared to bees emphasizes taxon-specific responses to urbanization and underscores the importance of considering multiple pollinator groups when assessing the ecological impacts of urban land-use change. The NMDS analysis demonstrated that urbanization significantly influenced the composition of both bee and hoverfly communities. Bee communities showed significant but comparatively weaker separation among urbanization levels, suggesting that while urbanization alters bee species composition, some species persist across multiple habitat types. In contrast, hoverfly communities exhibited stronger differentiation among urbanization levels, indicating that hoverflies are more sensitive to changes associated with urban development (Verboven et al. 2014 ; Persson et al. 2020 ; Gathof et al. 2022 ; Dylewski et al. 2024 ). Across the urbanization gradient, both taxonomical and functional beta diversity revealed taxon-specific patterns in variation among sites within the same urbanization level (Fisogni et al. 2025 ). Classical studies of biotic homogenization have examined whether urban communities become increasingly similar to one another or form nested subsets of more species-rich rural assemblages (Olden et al. 2004 ; Grimm et al. 2008 ). In contrast, our study addresses the complementary within-level question of how compositional variation among sites of the same urbanization class is partitioned between turnover and nestedness. This approach provide insight into how urbanization shapes the spatial structure of both taxonomic and functional community variation within urbanization levels. Consequently, our results should be interpreted as evidence of urbanization-related changes in community structure and in the relative contributions of turnover and nestedness to beta diversity, which may be consistent with homogenizing process, rather than as a direct test of biotic homogenization itself. This distinction is particularly relevant because changes in taxonomic dissimilarity do not necessarily imply corresponding changes in functional dissimilarity, yet the functional dimensions of beta diversity in urban environments remain comparatively understudied (Lokatis et al. 2023 ). Urbanization had contrasting effects on the taxonomic structure of bee and hoverfly communities. In bee communities, overall beta diversity was lowest in semi-natural habitats, whereas suburban and urban habitats showed higher values, indicating greater compositional differentiation among sites in these habitat types. Urban areas were characterized by the highest nestedness and the lowest turnover, suggesting that urban bee communities in species-poorer urban sites tended to form subsets of those in richer urban sites. In contrast, suburban habitats exhibited the highest turnover and overall beta diversity, reflecting greater species replacement among sites, likely due to the more heterogeneous nature of suburban landscapes. For hoverflies, the pattern differed. Semi-natural habitats exhibited higher nestedness than suburban areas, suggesting that species-poor hoverfly communities in semi-natural habitats tended to contain subsets of the species present in richer communities, rather than differing primarily through species replacement. Taxonomic turnover was highest in suburban habitats, reflecting strong species replacement among sites, which may be related to greater environmental heterogeneity among suburban sites, while urban habitats showed intermediate turnover values. Unlike bees, overall beta diversity did not differ significantly among habitat types. Thus, although hoverfly turnover and nestedness varied among urbanization levels, these differences did not translate into significant differences in overall taxonomic beta diversity. Consequently, our results reinforce the view that urban landscapes should be regarded as heterogeneous mosaics where local environmental conditions, habitat history and management practices jointly determine community composition rather than as ecologically uniform systems. This interpretation aligns closely with the conceptual framework proposed by (Sexton and Egerer 2026 ), who emphasize that fragmentation, habitat heterogeneity and varying disturbance regimes can maintain or even enhance beta diversity within cities. Functional beta diversity patterns partly mirrored taxonomical trends but revealed additional insights into ecosystem functioning. In bees, functional nestedness remained stable across habitats, suggesting that urbanization did not primary operate through the systematic loss of particular functional traits. Instead, the significantly higher of functional turnover and overall functional beta diversity in suburban and urban habitats indicates greater functional differentiation among sites in more urbanized landscapes. The lower overall functional beta diversity in semi-natural habitats suggests more consistent trait composition among sites, likely reflecting relatively stable environmental conditions and similar resource availability across sites. Hoverflies showed a contrasting pattern. Functional nestedness was highest in semi-natural habitats, indicating stronger subset relationships among communities in this habitat. In contrast, suburban habitats exhibited highest functional turnover and overall functional beta diversity, suggesting a breakdown in functional similarity and greater functional differentiation and functional replacement among sites. This suggests that suburban environments favor different functional strategies at different locations, likely driven by variable resource availability and disturbance regimes. This aligns with findings that urban landscape heterogeneity can drive beta diversity through complex environmental filtering (Sidemo-Holm et al. 2022 ). Urban habitats showed intermediate values, suggesting that highly urbanized environments may impose some environmental constraints but do not generate as strong functional differentiation as suburban areas. Comparing taxonomical and functional dimensions revealed taxon-specific responses to urbanization. Bees displayed strong concordance between species-level and trait-level responses, with higher turnover and overall beta diversity in suburban and urban habitats than in semi-natural areas, indicating restructuring of species and functional composition along the gradient. This supports research by (Normandin et al. 2017 ), which highlights that while cities can host diverse wild bee communities, the specific urban setting and the resulting functional trait composition significantly dictates community dynamics. Hoverflies, however, exhibited a more complex pattern. While taxonomic overall beta diversity remained relatively stable, functional turnover and overall functional beta diversity varied significantly, peaking in suburban habitats. Overall, our results show that urbanization reorganizes pollinator communities through taxonomic and functional restructuring rather than simple loss of diversity. Because bees and hoverflies respond uniquely to urban gradients, conservation strategies must move beyond treating pollinators as a single group. Thus, community differences across sites were generally more strongly associated with turnover than nestedness, but the relative contribution of taxonomic and functional turnover differed between bees and hoverflies. This contrast underscores the differing ecological strategies and sensitivities of bees and hoverflies, reinforcing recent evidence that pollinator taxa react uniquely to both localized garden features and broader urbanization gradients (Sexton et al. 2025 ). Several methodological limitations merit consideration. Fieldwork was restricted to June–August, which may have underrepresented early-spring bee specialists and potentially late-season taxa. However, this window captures peak summer pollinator activity and diversity in Central European urban landscapes, and the consistent multi-year, 30-site design ensures that comparisons across urbanization levels remain internally valid. The absence of vegetation surveys and flower cover estimation prevents direct attribution of community patterns to local floral resource availability. Nevertheless, the ISA-based urbanization classification provides a consistent framework for distinguishing the three habitat types, and the three contrasting habitat types (intensively managed urban lawns, suburban green areas, and semi-natural meadows) implicitly represent a wide spectrum of resource environments relevant to pollinator community assembly (Dylewski et al. 2023 ). Our results have significant implications for pollinator conservation in urban landscapes. The dominance of turnover over nestedness in beta diversity patterns indicates that effective conservation strategies must protect multiple sites across the urbanization gradient rather than focusing solely on species-rich “hotspots” (Socolar et al. 2016 ). High turnover implies that different sites harbor compositionaly distinct assemblages, meaning that conserving multiple sites may capture a broader range of species and functional composition within the regional species pool (Socolar et al. 2016 ). Conservation prioritization based solely on alpha diversity (e.g., protecting sites with the highest species richness) may miss compositionally distinct assemblages in suburban or moderately urbanized areas that harbor different species via turnover processes. Effective pollinator conservation in urban landscapes requires moving beyond aggregate biodiversity metrics toward strategies grounded in within-level beta diversity patterns. By maintaining diverse natural and semi-natural habitats, enhancing local resource availability, urban areas can support functionally diverse and resilient pollinator communities capable of providing essential ecosystem services. As urbanization continues to expand globally, integrating beta diversity-informed, pollinator-friendly design into urban planning and management will be crucial for identifying sites that contribute distinct taxonomic and functional assemblages and sustaining both biodiversity and the ecological functions that support human well-being (Bellamy et al. 2017 ; Liang et al. 2023 ; Süle et al. 2025 ). Finally, while functional redundancy may offer temporary insurance against environmental shifts, its erosion in urban areas signals a decline in ecosystem resilience (Winfree and Kremen 2009 ; Feit et al. 2019 ). Aligning with the EU Nature Restoration Regulation, we advocate for integrating multi-dimensional diversity assessments into urban planning. By utilizing beta diversity partitioning, practitioners can move beyond aggregate species counts to identify functionally irreplaceable sites, ensuring that urban green infrastructure effectively safeguards both biodiversity and essential pollination services.
