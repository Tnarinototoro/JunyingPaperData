College of Computing and Information Technology, University of Doha for Science and Technology, Doha, Qatar See more Article metrics View details Abstract Introduction: This study asks whether the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) describes the relationship between income and carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions in Saudi Arabia. Methods: A log-quadratic EKC specification, augmented with primary energy use and the renewable share of primary energy, is estimated on annual data for 1990–2022 (n = 33; 31 effective observations in the dynamic models). The sample ends in 2022 because the harmonised real-income series ends in that year; data for 2023–2024 are used descriptively only. The autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) bounds test provides the decisive evidence. For the specification selected by both the Akaike and Schwarz criteria, ARDL(2,0,0,0,1), the bounds F-statistic is 2.01, below the 10% lower (I(0)) bound of both the asymptotic (2.45) and finite-sample (2.75) critical values. It remains below the I(0) bound in all 162 lag configurations, in the best-fitting configuration free of residual serial correlation, after adding level shifts for 2014 and 2016, and after adding oil rents and trade openness. A Gregory–Hansen test allowing a regime shift also detects no cointegration. Unit-root tests indicate that per-capita emissions are stationary around a shifting mean, whereas income is integrated of order one. Long-run coefficients from the ARDL, nonlinear ARDL (NARDL) and dynamic ordinary least squares (DOLS) models are therefore reported only as exploratory quantities with measures of uncertainty. The income terms are jointly insignificant in every specification, and the Lind–Mehlum slope conditions for an inverted U are not met. The NARDL Wald test does not reject long-run income symmetry (p = 0.62), a result conditional on a long-run framework that the bounds test does not support. Results: The data provide no evidence of an inverted-U EKC, but they also do not establish a statistically significant U-shape. Total emissions show relative but not absolute decoupling from gross domestic product (GDP): the Tapio elasticity is 0.34 for 2016–2022, and total emissions rose by 7.9% between 2016 and 2024. The decline in carbon intensity slowed after 2016. Discussion: These patterns are described as associations and are not attributed causally to Vision 2030. Code and data that reproduce every table and figure are provided. 1 Introduction Whether economic growth can continue without a commensurate rise in greenhouse-gas emissions is a central question for hydrocarbon-exporting economies as the international community moves towards mid-century net-zero targets. The workhorse framework for this question is the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC), which hypothesises that pollution first rises with income and then declines once income passes a threshold, tracing an inverted U (Grossman and Krueger, 1995). The framework, and related STIRPAT-type (Stochastic Impacts by Regression on Population, Affluence and Technology) analyses, have been applied across sectors and regions, including transport emissions in South Africa (Oladunni and Olanrewaju, 2022; Oladunni et al., 2024). Its application to hydrocarbon exporters is problematic. Where hydrocarbon export revenue constitutes a large share of gross domestic product (GDP), income is mechanically tied to fossil-fuel extraction and, through subsidised domestic energy, to fossil-fuel combustion. Saudi Arabia exemplifies the case: it is among the world’s largest oil exporters and records some of the highest per capita carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions in the world (Friedlingstein et al., 2026). Its Vision 2030 programme aims to diversify the economy away from petroleum, and the credibility of that programme depends in part on whether growth can be decoupled from emissions (Al-Saidi, 2022; Amran et al., 2020). A sizeable literature has estimated the income–emissions relationship for Saudi Arabia (e.g., Alajlan and Alreshaidi, 2022). Table 1 summarises the principal single-country studies by sample period, dependent variable, regressors, estimator, treatment of cointegration and structural breaks, and principal EKC result. Three regularities emerge. First, the evidence on the EKC is mixed rather than uniform. Seven of the twelve studies find no inverted U (Alkhathlan and Javid, 2015; Alshehry and Belloumi, 2017; Samargandi, 2017; Raggad, 2018; 2020; Aldegheishem, 2024; Moustafa and Alomran, 2025); six of these rely mainly on autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) or nonlinear ARDL (NARDL) estimation, and three (Raggad, 2020; Aldegheishem, 2024; Moustafa and Alomran, 2025) do not include a quadratic income term. By contrast, studies using simultaneous-equation or Johansen/vector error-correction model (VECM) approaches report an inverted U, either for aggregate CO2 (Kahia et al., 2021b; Alsaedi et al., 2022) or only for liquid-fuel emissions (Kahia et al., 2021a). Second, every study in Table 1 that reports a cointegration test concludes, on at least one test, that a long-run relationship exists, and long-run elasticities are then interpreted as equilibrium parameters. To our knowledge, none of these studies tests for the degenerate case in which the dependent variable is itself stationary. Third, only four of the twelve studies model structural breaks: three at the unit-root stage (Alshehry and Belloumi, 2017; Raggad, 2018; 2020) and two in the long-run equation itself, through impulse-indicator saturation dummies (Raggad, 2018) or intervention dummies in a structural time-series model (Alkhathlan and Javid, 2015). Related sector-level research has already examined two aspects of the question elsewhere: the shape of the income–emissions curve for transport greenhouse-gas emissions in South Africa (Oladunni et al., 2024) and the fossil-energy, urbanisation and passenger-vehicle channels of emissions mitigation, analysed with system-econometric methods (Oladunni et al., 2025a; 2025b). What remains to be examined, particularly for a hydrocarbon exporter, is whether a stable levels relationship between income and emissions exists before the shape of that relationship is interpreted. This study addresses that question, which corresponds to the second and third regularities. Its contribution is methodological and empirical rather than a claim to a new theory. Methodologically, the paper treats cointegration as a hypothesis to be tested, with a decision rule fixed in advance: no estimate is interpreted as a long-run equilibrium parameter unless both the bounds F- and t-statistics lie beyond their I (1) critical bounds. The decision is assessed against asymptotic critical values and against finite-sample critical values simulated for the exact design (T = 33 with 31 effective observations, four regressors and the selected lag structure). It is also checked with an F-test on the lagged regressors that detects the degenerate case of a stationary dependent variable (McNown et al., 2018), across the full lag grid, after allowing for a regime shift, and after adding omitted structural variables. Empirically, the paper shows that the statistical properties of the data explain why a stable levels relationship is not found. Per-capita emissions are stationary around a shifting mean, whereas real income is integrated of order one, I (1), so a common stochastic trend is implausible. The paper also replaces the qualitative decoupling discussion common in this literature with explicit decoupling metrics and a formal test for a post-2016 change in the carbon-intensity trend. All data are public, and code that regenerates every table and figure accompanies the paper. The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature and sets out the conceptual framework and hypotheses. Section 3 describes the data and estimation strategy. Section 4 reports the results. Section 5 discusses them, and Section 6 concludes. 2 Literature review and conceptual framework 2.1 The environmental Kuznets curve and its discontents The EKC hypothesis posits an inverted-U relationship between per capita GDP and environmental damage: pollution rises with output at low levels of development, peaks at a turning point, and declines thereafter as structural change towards services, cleaner technology, and stronger regulation takes hold (Grossman and Krueger, 1995). Its empirical support varies with the country studied, the pollutant, the period, and the method. Cross-national studies show that the inverted U is far from universal (Shuai et al., 2017; Thio et al., 2022), and single-country evidence shows that it can disappear once energy consumption, trade, and structural variables are controlled for (Farhani and Ozturk, 2015). For CO2 in particular, a globally mixed pollutant with no local damage signal, turning points are often statistically insignificant or lie outside the observed income range (Farhani and Ozturk, 2015; Saidi and Ben Mbarek, 2017). A further statistical point is often overlooked: a positive linear and a negative quadratic coefficient are necessary but not sufficient for an inverted U. The slope of the fitted relationship must also be significantly positive at the lower end and significantly negative at the upper end of the observed income range (Lind and Mehlum, 2010). Sector-level applications show that even where the EKC is supported, economies may remain on its rising segment: for transport emissions in South Africa, all nine provinces are still below the turning point (Oladunni et al., 2024). 2.2 STIRPAT and the IPAT tradition A more structural approach to anthropogenic environmental impact is provided by IPAT (impact = population × affluence × technology) and its stochastic generalisation STIRPAT, which express impact as a function of population, affluence, and technology (Dietz and Rosa, 1997; York et al., 2003). The framework nests the EKC when a squared affluence term is added, so that the two income terms represent affluence and its square, and it disciplines the specification with demographic and technology channels (Fan et al., 2006). Applications typically find affluence and energy or carbon intensity to be the dominant drivers, with the roles of population and urbanisation varying across settings (Lv et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023; Lee and Zhao, 2023; Oladunni and Olanrewaju, 2022). The STIRPAT literature has increasingly adopted time-series cointegration estimators (Pattak et al., 2023), panel methods (Ghazali and Ali, 2019) and system-econometric approaches to fossil-energy use and emissions mitigation (Oladunni et al., 2025a). In this paper STIRPAT is used as an organising framework, not as a fully specified structural model. Two features of the specification depart from a complete STIRPAT model and are stated explicitly. First, population does not enter as a separate regressor. Expressing emissions and income in per capita terms imposes a unit elasticity of total emissions with respect to population, and Section 3.1 explains why a free population elasticity cannot be estimated reliably in this sample. Second, technology is represented by only two proxies: energy use per capita, which, conditional on income, captures energy intensity, and the renewable share, which captures the fuel mix. The model is therefore a parsimonious STIRPAT-type specification that conditions the EKC income terms on the two proximate technology channels. 2.3 Energy, oil rents, and the carbon curse in rentier economies In oil-exporting states the relationship between income and emissions is shaped by a distinctive political economy. The “carbon curse” hypothesis holds that resource-abundant countries develop production structures that favour energy- and carbon-intensive activities, keep domestic energy prices low, and provide weak incentives for efficiency. As a result, emissions intensity tends to rise with resource dependence (Nwani et al., 2024; Okere et al., 2026). Rents from oil and gas exports have been found to be positively related to emissions levels in panels of Middle East and North Africa and other oil-rich countries, while the magnitude of this relationship varies with institutional quality and the degree of dependence (Dabboussi, 2024; Mahmood et al., 2023; Abd El Nasser et al., 2025). Some studies identify thresholds of rent dependence beyond which the carbon curse becomes active (Nwani et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). Commodity-price volatility is a further channel: changes in world oil prices alter rent revenues and, through rent-financed spending and administered prices, domestic energy demand (Liu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). A complete model of Saudi emissions would therefore include rents. Section 4.6 adds oil rents and trade openness to the baseline specification as a robustness check. 2.4 Evidence for Saudi Arabia Table 1 shows that the Saudi evidence is less uniform than is often stated. ARDL-based studies of transport emissions (Alshehry and Belloumi, 2017), sectoral value added (Samargandi, 2017), urbanisation (Raggad, 2018) and air transport and trade (Aldegheishem, 2024) generally find a positive, monotonic long-run association between income and emissions and do not support an inverted U, although Aldegheishem (2024) includes no quadratic income term. By contrast, Kahia et al. (2021b) and Alsaedi et al. (2022) report an inverted U using simultaneous-equation and VECM frameworks, and Kahia et al. (2021a) find one only for liquid-fuel emissions. Energy consumption is the most consistently significant proximate driver, and renewable energy has so far been too small to offset the emissions associated with growth (Kahia et al., 2021a; 2021b). Studies of asymmetry differ in the direction of the asymmetry they report. Raggad (2020) and Toumi and Toumi (2019) report asymmetric responses, Moustafa and Alomran (2025) report reductions in emissions after positive growth shocks, and, outside the Adebayo et al., 2021 find long-run asymmetry in a NARDL model for Chile, with income decreases having a larger effect on consumption-based emissions than income increases. Every study in Table 1 that reports a cointegration test finds a long-run relationship on at least one test. This uniformity is itself notable, given the low power and size distortions of cointegration tests in samples of 25–55 annual observations. 2.5 Determinants beyond income: trade, finance, and urbanisation Other determinants of emissions include trade openness, whose effect depends on whether scale or composition effects dominate (Li and Haneklaus, 2022; Udeagha and Breitenbach, 2023; Zhou et al., 2025). Financial development may ease funding for clean investment or accelerate energy-intensive activity (Ali et al., 2019; Shoaib et al., 2020; Nurgazina et al., 2021). Urbanisation raises energy demand through agglomeration but can lower it through density-related efficiency (Sharif et al., 2026), and vehicle ownership is an important urban channel in transport-emissions studies (Oladunni and Olanrewaju, 2022). Oladunni et al. (2025b) examine the joint impacts of urbanisation and passenger vehicles on carbon emissions in Gauteng, South Africa, with system-econometric methods. Omitting these variables from a parsimonious model is a potential source of misspecification. This paper does not infer their role from residual diagnostics alone. Instead, it adds the two most closely linked to the rentier and trade channels discussed above, oil rents and trade openness, and tests their effect directly (Section 4.6). 2.6 Methodological lessons: cointegration as a question, not an assumption In much of the applied EKC literature, including the Saudi studies, the cointegration test functions as a procedural step. Once a significant bounds statistic is obtained, long-run elasticities are estimated without examining how fragile, sample-dependent or specification-dependent the levels relationship is. Three methodological points follow. First, the bounds test has low power in samples of about 30 annual observations, and tests based on asymptotic critical values can be over-sized; finite-sample critical values (Narayan, 2005) or simulated values should therefore be reported. Second, a significant negative error-correction coefficient does not establish cointegration. When the dependent variable is itself stationary, the coefficient on its lag will tend to be negative and significant even if the regressors are unrelated to it. This degenerate case is detected by also testing the lagged regressors (McNown et al., 2018). Third, single-equation long-run estimators such as dynamic ordinary least squares (DOLS; Stock and Watson, 1993) presuppose cointegration and cannot be used to confirm it. In this paper they serve only to check whether the sign pattern of the income terms depends on the estimator. 2.7 Conceptual framework and hypotheses Figure 1 summarises the framework. Affluence, represented by centred log income and its square, acts on per capita emissions through scale and composition effects. Energy use per capita represents the technique channel, and the renewable share represents fuel substitution. Population enters only through the per capita normalisation. The omitted structural drivers (oil rents, trade, financial development and subsidised energy prices) are expected to affect both income and emissions. Two of them, oil rents and trade openness, are added in robustness checks rather than in the baseline model because of the degrees-of-freedom constraint; financial development and energy prices are discussed but not modelled. The parsimonious specification is also defensible on theoretical grounds. The EKC is a reduced-form relationship in which scale, composition and technique effects operate through the proximate determinants of emissions, namely, income, the energy intensity of activity and the fuel mix (Grossman and Krueger, 1995; York et al., 2003). Rents, trade and financial development act on emissions largely through these proximate channels, so omitting them biases the income coefficients only to the extent that they affect emissions other than through energy use and the fuel mix. An inverted U is supported only if (i) β2 < 0 (because income is centred, the sign of β1 only locates the turning point relative to mean log income); (ii) the implied turning point lies inside the observed income range; and (iii) the Lind and Mehlum (2010) slope conditions hold, that is, the slope β1 + 2β2x is significantly positive at the minimum and significantly negative at the maximum of the observed (centred) income, x, at the 5% level. 2.7.2 H2 (levels relationship) In Equation 3, H0f: φ = θ1 = … = θk = 0 (bounds F-test) and H0f: φ = 0 (bounds t-test). A levels relationship is accepted only if, at the 5% level, the F-statistic exceeds its I (1) upper bound and the t-statistic is more negative than its I (1) bound, under both the asymptotic critical values (Pesaran et al., 2001) and the finite-sample critical values simulated for this design. If the F-statistic is below its I (0) bound, or the t-statistic is less negative than its I (0) bound, H2 is not supported; values between the bounds are inconclusive. The F-test on the lagged regressors alone, H0: θ1 = … = θk = 0 (McNown et al., 2018), is reported to identify the degenerate case. 2.7.3 H3 (asymmetry) In the NARDL model of Equation 7, H0: θ+ = θ− (long-run symmetry) and H0: δ+ = δ− (impact symmetry), each tested with a Wald F-test at the 5% level. Because the long-run symmetry test is meaningful only if a long-run relationship exists, the H3 decision is conditional on H2. 2.7.4 H4 (decoupling after 2016) Decoupling is measured with the Tapio (2005) elasticity ε = (%ΔCO2)/(%ΔGDP) for total emissions and total real GDP over 2016–2022 (Equation 9). Absolute (strong) decoupling requires ε < 0 with positive GDP growth; relative (weak) decoupling requires 0 ≤ ε < 0.8. In addition, the change in the carbon-intensity trend after 2016, κ2 in Equation 10, is tested with H0: κ2 = 0; an acceleration of the decline would require κ2 < 0. H4 is supported only if total emissions decoupled absolutely. Even then, the result would be described as consistent with, rather than caused by, Vision 2030, because the design contains no policy-evaluation identification strategy. 3 Data and methodology 3.1 Data, sample and variables The study uses annual data for Saudi Arabia from Our World in Data (OWID), which harmonises primary sources in two public repositories: the OWID CO2 dataset (github.com/owid/co2-data) and the OWID energy dataset (github.com/owid/energy-data). Both were accessed on 11 October 2026. Territorial fossil CO2 emissions come from the Global Carbon Budget (Friedlingstein et al., 2026). Primary energy use and the renewable share come from the Energy Institute Statistical Review of World Energy (Energy Institute, 2025). Total real GDP comes from the Maddison Project Database 2023 (Bolt and van Zanden, 2025), and population from the United Nations World Population Prospects 2024 (WPP; United Nations, 2024), which incorporates the downward revision of Saudi population estimates that followed the 2022 census. Per-capita GDP, emissions and energy use are all expressed relative to this population series. Oil rents and trade openness for the robustness models are taken from the World Bank World Development Indicators (WDI; World Bank, 2026). Every model that contains income is therefore estimated on 1990–2022 (33 annual observations; 31 effective observations after two initial observations are lost to the lag structure). The years 2023 and 2024 are used only descriptively: in the trajectories and renewable shares shown in Sections 4.1 and 4.7, and in the 2016–2024 growth rates of total and per capita emissions reported in Section 4.7. They cannot enter any econometric model because no harmonised income observation exists for them, and splicing income from a different source would introduce a level break at the junction. World Bank oil rents end in 2021, so models that include rents are estimated on 1990–2021. With 31 effective observations, the preferred ARDL leaves 23 residual degrees of freedom and the common-lag ARDL and NARDL leave 20, so the cointegration, asymmetry and break tests have low power, and null results should be read as non-detection rather than as evidence of absence. The dependent variable is the natural logarithm of CO2 emissions per capita (lnCO2). Income enters as centred log real GDP per capita, xt = lnGDPt − m̄, where m̄ = 10.544 is the 1990–2022 sample mean of ln real GDP per capita. The quadratic term is the square of the centred variable, lnGDP2t ≡ xt2 = (lnGDPt − m̄)2. Centring reduces collinearity between the two income terms and changes neither the fitted curve nor the significance of the quadratic coefficient; it does change the interpretation of β1, which is the income elasticity at the sample mean of log income (geometric-mean income of about 37,960 international dollars, Int$, at 2011 prices). Energy use enters as lnEN, the log of primary energy consumption per capita, so its coefficient is an elasticity. The renewable share, REN, enters in levels, in percentage points, because it equals zero in 1990–2010 and cannot be logged. Its coefficient is therefore a semi-elasticity: 100 × β4 is the approximate percentage change in per capita emissions associated with a one-percentage-point increase in the renewable share. Because REN never exceeds 0.157% of primary energy in the estimation sample, a one-percentage-point change lies far outside the data, and the magnitude of the coefficient should not be interpreted economically. Population is not included as a separate regressor, for three reasons. First, the per capita formulation already imposes a unit population elasticity on total emissions. Second, the integration order of log population is ambiguous (Supplementary Appendix Table A1). With a constant and up to three lags, the augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test rejects a unit root in the first difference (p = 0.004), but the Phillips–Perron (PP) test does not (p = 0.471) and the Kwiatkowski–Phillips–Schmidt–Shin (KPSS) test rejects stationarity of the first difference (p = 0.025). All three tests indicate that the second difference is stationary. Because the bounds procedure is invalid if any regressor is I (2), including a series that may be I (2) would compromise the central test. Third, the Saudi population series contains an absolute decline between 2017 and 2018 (30.78–30.37 million) that coincides with the departure of expatriate workers. A free population elasticity estimated from 33 observations would largely reflect this episode rather than a structural demographic effect. 3.2 Model specification The empirical model embeds the EKC income terms within a parsimonious STIRPAT-type specification:where β0 is the intercept, β1 is the income elasticity at mean income, β2 is the curvature parameter, β3 is the energy elasticity, β4 is the renewable-share semi-elasticity and ut is the error term. An inverted U requires β2 < 0, with the turning point inside the observed income range; because income is centred, β1 is the slope at mean log income, and its sign only indicates whether the turning point lies above or below that mean. The slope of the income–emissions relationship and its turning point are given in Equation 2.so the turning point is expressed in uncentred income units by adding back m̄. Standard errors for x* are obtained by the delta method. The Lind–Mehlum conditions are evaluated as s (xmᵢn) and s (xmax) at the observed extremes, xmᵢn = −0.562 and xmax = 0.401, which correspond to Int$21,637 and Int$56,686. 3.3 Estimation strategy The estimation strategy proceeds in six steps. Step 1: integration order. Each series is tested with the ADF (Dickey and Fuller, 1979), PP (Phillips and Perron, 1988) and KPSS (Kwiatkowski et al., 1992) tests, with a constant and, as a robustness check, a constant and trend. ADF lags are selected by the Akaike information criterion (AIC) up to a maximum of three, using the rule ⌊4 (T/100)^0.25⌋ = 3 for T = 33. The PP test uses a Newey–West (Bartlett) bandwidth of three, and the KPSS bandwidth is selected automatically. Zivot and Andrews (1992) tests with an endogenous intercept break (model A, which includes a trend) check whether apparent unit roots reflect unmodelled shifts. Step 2: levels relationship. The conditional error-correction form of an ARDL (p, q1, … , qk) model under Pesaran et al. (2001) Case III (unrestricted intercept, no trend) is where z t = (x t , x t2 , lnEN t , REN t )′, k = 4, α 0 is the intercept, θ j are the coefficients on the levels of the regressors, γᵢ and δ jl are short-run coefficients and u t is a serially uncorrelated error. The lag index ι j equals 1 when q j ≥ 1 and 0 when q j = 0; in the latter case the regressor enters only contemporaneously in levels. The coefficient φ on the lagged dependent variable is the error-correction coefficient if a levels relationship exists. Conditional long-run coefficients are where ∇λ j is the gradient of λ j with respect to (θ j , φ). Delta-method standard errors and 95% confidence intervals are based on the t distribution with the residual degrees of freedom. Lag orders are chosen by AIC over the full grid p ∈ {1, 2} and q j ∈ {0, 1, 2}, giving 2 × 3 4 = 162 models estimated on a common sample (1992–2022). Both AIC and the Schwarz Bayesian criterion (SBC) select ARDL (2,0,0,0,1), which is the preferred specification ( Supplementary Appendix Table A3 ). A common-lag ARDL (2,1,1,1,1) is also reported for comparison. Because the bounds test assumes serially uncorrelated errors, the test is also reported for the best-fitting configuration in the grid that passes the Breusch–Godfrey test at one and two lags. The bounds statistics are compared with the asymptotic critical values of Pesaran et al. (2001) , Tables CI(iii) and CII(iii), for k = 4, and with finite-sample critical values obtained by simulating 20,000 replications of each estimated design under the null of no levels relationship. In the simulations the dependent variable is a driftless random walk and the regressors are independent white noise (I (0) bound) or random walks (I (1) bound), with T = 33 and the same lag structure and sample trimming as the estimated model. This procedure follows the logic of Narayan (2005) but matches the actual sample size and lag orders. The decision rule for H2 is stated in Section 2.7 . Step 3: a specification that does not rely on a levels relationship. If H2 is not supported, a model in first differences avoids spurious levels regression whether or not the series are cointegrated. Because lnCO2 is itself stationary, differencing may over-difference the dependent variable, so this model is used to describe short-run associations rather than as a structural specification: where c is a constant, ρ is the coefficient on lagged emissions growth, π 1 –π 4 are the short-run coefficients on the differenced regressors (Δx t2 denotes Δ(x t2 )) and e t is the error term. The model is estimated by ordinary least squares (OLS) with Newey–West heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors (Bartlett kernel, finite-sample correction; Newey and West, 1987 ). Two lags are used, the integer part of the rule-of-thumb truncation lag 0.75T^(1/3) for T = 31; the same setting is used for all HAC inference in the paper. Step 4: asymmetry. Following Shin et al. (2014), centred log income is decomposed into partial sums of positive and negative changes, and the NARDL (2,1,1,1,1) model, whose lag orders mirror the common-lag ARDL so that the symmetric and asymmetric models are directly comparable, replaces (x t , x t2 ) in Equation 3 by (GDP + , GDP − ): where θ 3 , θ 4 , γ 1 , δ 3 and δ 4 are defined as in Equation 3 . Long-run symmetry is tested with the Wald restriction H 0 : θ + = θ − , which is equivalent to λ + = −θ + /φ = λ − = −θ − /φ. Impact symmetry is tested with H 0 : δ + = δ − . Cumulative dynamic multipliers are computed from the levels representation of Equation 7 . A 90% band for the asymmetry multiplier is obtained from 5,000 Monte Carlo draws of the coefficient vector. Step 5: robustness. (a) DOLS (Stock and Watson, 1993) regresses lnCO2 on the levels of the regressors and on one lead, the current value and one lag of their first differences, with Newey–West HAC inference as above: where w t is either (x t , x t2 , lnEN t )′ or (x t , x t2 , lnEN t , REN t )′, β is the vector of long-run coefficients, ψ l are the coefficients on the leads and lags of Δw t , and ν t is the error term. Because DOLS presupposes cointegration, it is used only to check whether the sign pattern of the income terms depends on the estimator. (b) Level-shift dummies D2014 (equal to 1 from 2014) and D2016 (equal to 1 from 2016) are added to Equation 3 . The break dates are imposed exogenously, chosen from the oil-price collapse and the launch of Vision 2030 rather than estimated. (c) Endogenous breaks are examined with Zivot–Andrews unit-root tests and with the Gregory and Hansen (1996) residual-based cointegration test, which allows a level shift in the cointegrating regression at an unknown date (trimming 15%). Its finite-sample critical values are simulated (3,000 replications, T = 33, four regressors). (d) Oil rents and trade openness are added to Equation 3 to test directly whether these omitted structural variables restore a levels relationship. Step 6: diagnostics and decoupling. Model adequacy is assessed with the Breusch–Godfrey Lagrange multiplier (LM) test for serial correlation at one and two lags (Breusch, 1978; Godfrey, 1978), reported in both χ2 and F forms; the LM test for autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity, ARCH(1) (Engle, 1982); the Jarque and Bera (1980) normality test; and Ramsey’s (1969) regression specification error test (RESET) with the squared fitted values, F (1, df). Parameter constancy is assessed with cumulative sum (CUSUM) and CUSUM-of-squares tests of recursive residuals (Brown et al., 1975), with 5% bounds. The CUSUM-of-squares bound is simulated for the actual number of recursive residuals. Decoupling is measured with the Tapio elasticity, for sub-periods [a, b], computed for totals and per capita , and with a segmented log-linear carbon-intensity trend, estimated with HAC standard errors, where η t is the error term and ☐ (·) is the indicator function. κ 1 is the pre-2016 annual trend in carbon intensity, κ 2 is the change in that trend after 2016, and κ 3 is the level shift in 2016. 3.4 Reproducibility Every estimated statistic, results table and figure is generated by code directly from the public source files; Tables 1 and 2, which summarise the literature and the variable definitions, and the summary of hypothesis decisions in Section 4.8 are compiled from these sources and outputs. The error-correction coefficient requires careful interpretation. In the preferred model φ = −0.414 (standard error, SE, 0.155; t = −2.68). The coefficient is negative, which is the sign implied by the stable error-correction representation in Equation 3, and it would be significant against a conventional t distribution (p = 0.013). The relevant benchmark, however, is the bounds t-test. The statistic lies between the I (0) and I (1) bounds at 10% (asymptotic −2.57 and −3.66; simulated −2.54 and −3.60) and above the I (0) bound at 5% (asymptotic −2.86; simulated −2.90). The t-test is therefore inconclusive at 10% and does not reject at 5%. Combined with the F-test, which falls below the I (0) bound, there is no statistical support for a levels relationship. Given the stationarity of lnCO2 (Section 4.2), the negative φ is best read as mean reversion of emissions. It does not imply that roughly 41% of a disequilibrium with income is corrected each year, and no such convergence claim is made. In the common-lag ARDL (2,1,1,1,1), φ = −0.390 (SE 0.164, t = −2.38) and F = 1.95, which lead to the same conclusion. φ is negative in every ARDL and NARDL model reported in this paper and in all 162 configurations of the lag grid (range −0.489 to −0.239). The conclusion holds across all the specifications examined. Across all 162 lag configurations in the AIC grid, the bounds F-statistic ranges from 0.81 to 2.08 and never reaches the asymptotic 10% I (0) bound. Of these configurations, 85 show no residual serial correlation at one or two lags (Breusch–Godfrey LM and F p > 0.05); the best of them by AIC, ARDL (1,0,0,0,0), gives F = 1.17 and t = −1.99, both inside the no-rejection region (Table 5, row (j)). The conclusion therefore does not depend on the residual autocorrelation found in the preferred model. The F-statistic of the preferred model also remains below the I (0) bound after adding level shifts for 2014 and 2016 (F = 1.64), after adding oil rents (F = 1.48), and after adding oil rents and trade openness (F = 1.24). The only configuration in which F enters the inconclusive band is an ARDL with three lags of the dependent variable (F = 3.49). That model lies outside the pre-specified lag grid and shows significant residual autocorrelation at two lags (Breusch–Godfrey F = 5.34, p = 0.017), and even there F is below the 5% I (1) bound under both the asymptotic (4.01) and simulated (5.05) critical values. H2 is therefore not supported: the bounds test provides no statistical evidence of a levels relationship among emissions, income, energy use and the renewable share. Per-capita CO2 emissions in Saudi Arabia are high and bounded. After a level shift in the late 1990s they are stationary around a mean of about 20 t (sample mean 20.33 t; Table 3), whereas real income per capita grew by about 160% and follows an I (1) process. A series of this kind cannot share a common stochastic trend with income. Accordingly, the bounds test provides no evidence of a levels relationship in any of the 162 lag configurations or in the preferred model augmented with break dummies or with oil rents and trade openness, and the Gregory–Hansen test finds none when a regime shift is allowed. Consistent with this result, neither the long-run nor the short-run income terms are significant, there is no evidence of an inverted U, the curvature is not robust in sign across estimators, and income effects show no detectable asymmetry. Total emissions decoupled from GDP only in relative terms, and the decline in carbon intensity slowed after 2016. These results agree with the majority of the Saudi ARDL literature in finding no support for an inverted U (Alshehry and Belloumi, 2017; Samargandi, 2017; Raggad, 2018; Aldegheishem, 2024; Moustafa and Alomran, 2025). They differ from that literature on whether a stable long-run relationship exists. Several factors may explain the difference: most of these studies use total rather than per capita emissions and longer samples that include the rapid emissions growth of the 1970s and 1980s (Table 1); regressor sets differ (Aldegheishem, 2024, whose per capita sample is close to ours, uses air passengers and trade openness rather than energy use); and deterministic terms and the use of asymptotic critical values may also differ. Our results do not show that earlier long-run estimates are wrong. They show that, for 1990–2022, per capita data and the present set of regressors, a levels relationship cannot be established once finite-sample critical values and the stationarity of emissions are taken into account. The same caution applies to the asymmetry literature. NARDL studies report that positive income shocks raise emissions more than negative shocks lower them (Raggad, 2020), that renewable and emissions shocks transmit differently (Toumi and Toumi, 2019), or that recent positive growth shocks reduced emissions (Moustafa and Alomran, 2025). In samples of 25–55 annual observations, asymmetries are estimated with wide confidence bands, as Figure 5 shows, and international NARDL evidence is similarly heterogeneous (Hammoudeh et al., 2015; Malik et al., 2020; Fatima et al., 2021; Mujtaba and Jena, 2021; Sreenu, 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). The present failure to detect asymmetry is not inconsistent with earlier findings of asymmetry; it illustrates how imprecisely asymmetry is estimated in samples of this size. The findings also connect to sector-level EKC evidence elsewhere. In South Africa’s transport sector, Oladunni et al. (2024) find support for the EKC hypothesis in a panel of the nine provinces, but all provinces remain on the rising segment and none has yet reached the turning point. Oladunni and Olanrewaju (2022) identify GDP and passenger vehicles as the main drivers of transport emissions in an extended STIRPAT panel. System-econometric studies of KwaZulu-Natal and Gauteng likewise place fossil-energy use, urbanisation and passenger vehicles at the centre of emissions-mitigation analysis (Oladunni et al., 2025a; 2025b), which parallels the role of energy use as the most consistent proximate driver in the Saudi literature (Section 2.4). In these settings, as in Saudi Arabia, rising income has not yet been accompanied by falling emissions. 5.2 Possible mechanisms: the carbon curse and energy lock-in The mechanisms discussed in this section are interpretations drawn from the wider literature. The model does not estimate them directly. The carbon-curse literature argues that rentier economies use resource rents to fund low domestic energy prices and public spending, which steers development towards energy-intensive sectors such as petrochemicals, desalination and cement (Dabboussi, 2024; Nwani et al., 2024; Okere et al., 2026). Threshold studies suggest that the curse operates once rents exceed a certain share of GDP (Nwani et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). On this reading, rising income expands an existing carbon-intensive structure rather than inducing the compositional and technological change that an EKC turning point requires. This is consistent with emissions per capita that are high but stable, and with income terms that have no detectable effect. However, the direct test in Section 4.6 finds no evidence that oil rents restore a levels relationship or explain the residual autocorrelation in this sample. The carbon-curse account is therefore one possible interpretation of the bounded emissions path. It is not a finding of this paper, and the residual autocorrelation should not be read as evidence of omitted rent effects. 5.3 Implications for decoupling and policy The policy implications are conditional on the evidence. The model shows that income growth over 1990–2022 was not accompanied by any detectable income-related change in per capita emissions, and that total emissions decoupled only in relative terms. It does not show what effect Vision 2030 has had, because it contains no policy-treatment variable, the period from 2016 to 2022 has only seven income-matched observations, and no counterfactual is identified. Claims that Vision 2030 has had either a large or a negligible effect on emissions would go beyond the evidence. Within these limits, three implications follow from combining the empirical results with the wider literature. First, because the data show no detectable tendency for income growth by itself to reduce emissions, decarbonisation is likely to require deliberate changes in the carbon intensity of energy and the energy intensity of output. These are the channels through which energy consumption links growth to emissions (Gershon et al., 2024), and the decoupling literature shows that absolute decoupling has been achieved only gradually and in a minority of economies (Haberl et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2021; Hubacek et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021; González-Álvarez and Montañés, 2023). Second, the renewable share, at 0.73% of primary energy in 2024, is still too small for its estimated coefficient to be interpreted economically (Section 4.5). Scaling renewable capacity and continuing energy-price reform (Al-Gahtani, 2024; Muneer et al., 2025; Selim and Alshareef, 2025) are therefore likely to be necessary for any future decoupling that the data could register. Third, the literature on finance and trade suggests that directing financial deepening and export strategy towards low-carbon activities could reduce the carbon intensity of growth (Ali et al., 2019; Shoaib et al., 2020; Udeagha and Breitenbach, 2023; Zhou et al., 2025). These are implications for policy design, not results estimated in this paper. 6 Conclusion This paper asked whether an inverted-U EKC describes the relationship between income and per capita CO2 emissions in Saudi Arabia over 1990–2022, and whether a stable long-run relationship links emissions, income, energy use and the renewable share. The strongest defensible results are as follows. First, there is no statistical evidence of an inverted-U EKC in any reported specification, and the evidence is also insufficient to establish a statistically significant U-shape. Second, the ARDL bounds test provides no evidence of a stable long-run levels relationship. The F-statistic of 2.01 is below the I (0) bound under both asymptotic and finite-sample critical values, the bounds t-test is at most inconclusive at the 10% level, and the same conclusion holds across all 162 configurations in the pre-specified lag grid, in the best-fitting configuration free of serial correlation, with structural-break dummies, with a Gregory–Hansen regime shift and with oil rents and trade openness added; only one specification outside the pre-specified grid enters the inconclusive band. Third, per capita emissions are stationary around a shifting mean while income is I (1). This is consistent with the absence of a common trend and implies that a negative error-correction coefficient should not be read as equilibrium adjustment. Fourth, no long-run income asymmetry is detected, a result conditional on a long-run framework that the bounds test does not support. Fifth, total emissions show relative but not absolute decoupling from GDP after 2016, and the decline in carbon intensity slowed rather than accelerated. These patterns are not attributed causally to Vision 2030. These conclusions are subject to several sources of uncertainty. The estimation sample has only 31 effective observations, so the bounds, asymmetry and trend-break tests have low power, and the null results should be read as “not detected in these data” rather than “shown not to exist”. The preferred levels ARDL and the NARDL retain first-order residual autocorrelation; HAC inference, the serial-correlation-free configuration in Table 5 and the first-difference model mitigate but do not remove this concern. The renewable share varies too little to support an economic interpretation of its coefficient. The analysis covers only seven observations with matched income data from 2016 to 2022. Future research can address these limitations in several ways. Harmonised income data for 2023 onwards would allow re-estimation, and longer annual spans and sector-level data would increase the information available. Bootstrap ARDL tests (McNown et al., 2018) and Fourier-type cointegration procedures suited to oil-cycle economies could be applied. A panel of Gulf Cooperation Council members would test whether bounded per capita emissions and the absence of a levels relationship are shared across hydrocarbon exporters. Assessing Vision 2030 itself would require an explicit policy-evaluation design, for example, a synthetic-control or interrupted time-series approach with a defined treatment.
