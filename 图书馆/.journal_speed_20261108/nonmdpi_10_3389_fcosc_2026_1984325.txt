You can register here Notify me Abstract Humans are ecologically embedded organisms, yet increasingly large portions of everyday attention are organized by screen-mediated environments. This article, written for the Research Topic "Putting People in Their Place: Restoring the Human Animal in Ecological Systems," develops the hypothesis that digital compulsion contributes to cosmological disassociation: the lived condition in which the person no longer experiences the self as materially and dynamically continuous with the ecosystems that sustain them. The central problem is not screen time, nor the mere substitution of screens for outdoor time, but the possibility that compulsive digital engagement repeatedly engages and modifies the neurocognitive processes that keep organisms situated in their environments: attention allocation, interoceptive regulation, reward prediction, circadian entrainment, and embodied spatial cognition. The argument is stronger than a nature-deficit account, but more cautious than a claim that screen use has been shown to damage the brain. Existing research offers converging evidence for altered attention and reward processing in problematic digital use, circadian disruption from evening display exposure, and the constitutive role of body-environment coupling in cognition and selfhood, while evidence for lasting neuroadaptation remains weak. Reassociation is therefore unlikely to occur simply by encouraging people to spend time outdoors; it would require interventions that retrain attention, restore sensitivity to bodily and environmental rhythms, reintroduce embodied navigation, and weaken the reward architectures that make ecological presence difficult to sustain. These interventions are proposed as testable directions at the interface of neuropsychology and conservation, not as evidence-based recommendations.
