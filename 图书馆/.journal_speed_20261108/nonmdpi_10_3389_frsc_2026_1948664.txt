Urban Planning Program and Indigenous Studies Program, The University of Kansas, Lawrence, KS, United States See more Article metrics View details Abstract The rapid expansion of artificial intelligence and digital infrastructure has outpaced existing policy frameworks governing data center development. This mismatch between surging demand for digital infrastructure and local planning guidelines is widely acknowledged, although understudied. This study examines intergovernmental policy frameworks that either enable or restrict data center development. Using an exploratory design, we analyze both state economic incentive programs and local land use regulations. Results suggest a broad spectrum of governance approaches, ranging from “incentivizing” states that leverage legislative preemption to limit local zoning control, to “disincentivizing” states that enact moratoria and environmental mandates to safeguard local resources. These state-level interventions frequently override local autonomy, leaving local communities to manage grid, water, and social equity impacts. The friction surrounding data center development mirrors historical land-use debates in energy and housing infrastructure, proving that measures like buffering and screening are no longer sufficient. Addressing this governance gap demands proactive, resource-centered planning. We provide recommendations to streamline zoning regulations from multi-level governance perspectives, as well as recommendations on utility performance metrics, water-use disclosures, and transparent Community Benefit Agreements and engagements from the literature. 1 Introduction Scholarly attention to artificial intelligence (AI) has surged with the deployment of tools like ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, and Gemini. This attention has ranged from the mathematics and programming that allow the AI to function to their wide array of applications to emergent and future impacts on myriad aspects of economic and social life (Cugurullo et al., 2023). At the same time, scholarly attention to the physical infrastructure enabling the AI transformation, especially data centers containing the immense computing infrastructure, has grown much more slowly with few studies documenting, characterizing, and analyzing the move to build data centers seemingly everywhere all at once (Kollar, 2026). If the recent explosion of media attention on controversies around the siting of data centers is any indication (Healy, 2026; Macur, 2026; Whang, 2026), planning researchers must increase our efforts in this area, both to inform scholarly understanding and to provide policy and practice guidance. Major AI companies report plans for more than $3 trillion in AI infrastructure in the United States alone by 2030 (American Edge Project, 2025), on top of more than $500 billion invested between 2019 and, 2025 in 4,000 data centers across the country (Fitzpatrick, 2025). Data centers have become the hottest topic in local planning practice, and scholarship and education need to catch up. As demand for these facilities accelerates, a critical tension has emerged in the United States because of an underdeveloped intergovernmental policy framework. In the absence of federal policy, clarity, cohesion, and leadership, state governments are developing a broad array of policy frameworks to fill the gap. Meanwhile localities often struggle to adapt land-use planning systems to foster economic benefits while mitigating social and environmental costs, all of which challenge fundamental understandings of local control (Kollar, 2026). Part of the reason this tension arises is because data center development presents scale mismatches on multiple levels. Before the AI boom, data centers served as the backbone of the internet, as data storage sought “plug-and-play” utility access for what are now comparatively small facilities on affordable industrial land (Covas et al., 2013). Data centers were one of many light industries that communities knew how to plan for and regulate. On top of the stress placed on local communities by the sheer number of data centers being proposed, modern hyperscale facilities already consume 4.4% of total U. S. electricity—a figure projected to soar to 27% by 2028 (U.S. Department of Energy, 2025). This massive energy consumption, coupled with demands for land area and water to cool data center operations, triggers significant externalities like intensified urban heat island effects, rising utility costs for residents, and stress on water supplies (Wang et al., 2025; Lam et al., 2021). Thus, when state-level incentives prioritize rapid investment over local site-specific review, data centers become canonical “locally unwanted land uses” (LULUS) that may benefit society at large but impose clear, negative costs on neighboring communities. Moreover, these facilities can disproportionately burden marginalized neighborhoods and strain the competitiveness of small local businesses, echoing long-standing patterns of environmental and economic injustice (Bullard, 2018; Schoenig, 2024). The early stages of planning for AI-data center expansions have been fast-moving and chaotic. The next few years promise to be even more dynamic and challenging to monitor and manage. Thus, scholars and policymakers desperately need to increase our understanding of how state-level policy and incentive actions and local land-use planning and regulation are moving together or apart. Our exploratory scan of the leading edge of state and local activities systematically explores the state-local policy nexus, drawing on a robust analysis of variations in planning in all 50 US States. We seek to answer three foundational research questions: What policy and incentive activities are state governments initiating that shape the local landscape for siting data centers? What planning and regulatory approaches are local governments using to influence the siting of data centers? How can the emergent state policies and local regulatory approaches be categorized to help practitioners understand their context and their range of options? Given that data center development touches on multi-level governance, we first present a conceptual framework that situates data centers at the intersection of state-level programs, local land use regulation, and place-based equity. Through the lens of multi-level governance, we detail our research methodology, which involves a systematic review of state-level incentives and an exploration of exemplary local government land-use approaches to data center regulation. We then synthesize the findings and develop four general categories of approaches available to local governments and outline future research pathways to add both depth and breadth to our exploratory analysis. We conclude with a discussion of the implications for planning practice, highlighting alignments and mismatches between state-level incentives and local planning needs for future AI-related development. 2 The movement empowering AI infrastructure The “fourth industrial revolution” centers on deeper integration of the tools of the first (water and steam), second (electricity), and especially the third (digital), into all spheres of life, blurring the lines between what is cyber and virtual and what is bio-physical and lived (Schaub et al., 2025; Velarde, 2020). Over the last 10 years, as the fourth industrial revolution has accelerated at speeds once thought unrealistic or aspirational, governments have failed to develop clear and consistent policy frameworks for artificial intelligence and the massive infrastructure investments that make AI development and deployment possible. Through summer 2026, the federal playbook for addressing AI and data centers can be summarized as “approve and incentivize first, study and regulate second,” largely aligning with the proverbial Silicon Valley mantra of “move fast and break things.” For example, in July 2025, the Trump Administration announced plans to accelerate federal permitting processes for data center infrastructure and incentivize AI-friendly infrastructure involving high-voltage transmission lines and other equipment (The White House, 2025). Only 11 months later in June 2026, an eternity on the timeline of AI evolution, did the White House under Trump’s administration announce an executive order making initial moves to gain insight, if not oversight, into the development of AI models before their release to the public (Kang, 2026). This approach extends well beyond White House policy pronouncements, with parallels in the federal agencies, financial markets, and society more broadly. For example, in 2025 the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) expressed their support to ensure reliable power through private energy providers like Duke Energy to empower data centers (EPA, 2025), even as the EPA’s ability to study the impacts of AI tools and AI infrastructure on its mission of protecting human and ecological health has diminished (Kolbert, 2026). AI companies increasingly make up a large portion of the US stock market (CNN, 2026), often before generating substantial revenue streams and in spite of their initial products appearing culpable in spreading misinformation on topics of vital civic importance (Cybernews, 2026), abetting harmful behaviors in vulnerable individuals (Chatterjee, 2025), and wasting untold resources generating what is already widely called “AI slop” (Whang, 2026). In sum, the national-level political economy of AI consists of governments and industry rushing to capture the near-term, tangible benefits associated with the boom in AI, while moving more slowly to address the longer-term, less understood risks that are only beginning to come into focus. One of the underlying motivations for this research is to understand whether similar dynamics appear at the state level and what opportunities and challenges are presented for local communities using land use planning to manage data center development. These dynamics and possibilities will be explored in our empirical findings. But first, we provide a baseline understanding of data centers, their growth to date, and the array of state and local considerations, and to some extend an intergovernmental framework at play in decision-making. 3 Data centers: basics, growth trajectories, and governance Data centers are warehouse-like facilities that contain massive, networked computer servers used by specific organizations for remote data storage, processing, and distribution (Dictionary, 2026; Bohler, 2024). These facilities provide substantial computational power and house technology-intensive equipment that supports a wide range of digital functions, including telecommunications, cloud computing, high-speed network connectivity, and other web-based services. Data centers have become prevalent and the backbone of smart city development and applications empowering efficiency and digitization (APA Virginia, 2023; Bibri, 2021; Yigitcanlar, 2015). Yet, data centers are expensive to build and operate. Their development involves land purchases or leases with access to adequate energy, water, and broadband infrastructure; massive physical structures to house the operational equipment; the hardware required to run algorithms and building systems to maintain desired interior conditions; and the labor needed to complete construction (Lipsey, 2026; CUDO Compute, 2026). Beyond these initial costs, there are ongoing operational expenses, not least of which are energy, water, system maintenance, and security. The clearest evidence of this phase transition in data centers is seen in estimates that they already consume 1.7% of global electricity and 2.5% of the UK’s national electricity (Takci et al., 2025). In 2023, US data centers consumed approximately 4.4% of total domestic electricity, with consumption projected to grow to 27% of total energy use by 2028 (U.S. Department of Energy, 2025). If these growth projections seem overblown, consider that a single new facility proposed in central Montana may require 7,000 megawatts, which is more than the largest power plant in the country, the Grand Coulee Dam (Macur, 2026). Data centers underpin one of the fastest economic growth sectors in human history, although they generate competition among states and localities to attract development in hopes of benefiting from that growth. The policy challenges are embedded in the mismatch between top-down growth goals and local management through land use and zoning, which bears untold environmental and social costs. Such misalignment creates opportunities for multi-level governance regarding how the influx of data centers is managed, alongside the oversight of natural resources like water, land, and electricity. Multi-level governance has been widely used to study environmental governance, such as climate change adaptation and the transition to clean energy (Hooghe et al., 2020; Juhola, 2016; Njuguna et al., 2025). Multi-level governance represents the dispersion of authority at the subnational level; in the case of data centers, it reflects state and local commitments to pursuing economic prosperity while bearing environmental governance concerns at different levels (Hooghe et al., 2020). On the one hand, decentralization often leads to internal variation within a country—for instance, states and regions produce varying self-rules that foster joint policies for economic growth. On the other hand, state and municipal governance may take a stringent turn when facing a limited pool of resources. As a result, state-level governance targets capital mobilization, tax incentives, and the preemption of land-use rulings (Table 1). The state-level governance of data centers is embedded in the dynamics of aiming to attract and retain industries. For generations, states have competed with each other for myriad industries, ranging from textiles to military hardware to film to biomedical research (Eisinger, 1988). To achieve this, land use preemption at the state level can accelerate approval processes by allowing states to override, restrict, or displace local municipal and county authority over zoning and property development (Kollar, 2026). State economic development policies incorporate incentives, ranging from broad, statewide tax programs to customized packages that provide targeted benefits for specific industries or individual firms (Leigh and Blakely, 2017). Tax incentive packages lower costs for developers and build local workforce capacity through education, skills development, and training programs (Austrian and Norton, 2002; Leigh and Blakely, 2017). In turn, states hope the subsequent job creation, spending, property value increases, and tax revenue will provide a net benefit for the state in the long run (Slattery and Zidar, 2020). Data centers present new opportunities for economic benefits, although no systematic analyses of state-level incentive packages have existed despite the prevalent news coverage on state competition (Digital Realty, 2026; Macur, 2026). 4 Local land use regulations and site design considerations The data center development, along with the new infrastructure around it, creates opportunities for upgrading existing infrastructure (Gomez and DeAngelis, 2022). However, the core challenge is whether data centers should be categorized as a new land use type equipped with new zoning ordinances (Berkley Group, 2023) or managed through existing regulations with modified terms. Standalone land use policies often involve a use-specific zoning code. For instance, a county-level wind farm development agreement can induce one-off agreements in which developers pay fees to support county infrastructure development, including road repairs and electric grid upgrades (Njuguna et al., 2025). When using existing land use regulations, data centers are largely considered industrial uses with additional requirements for conditional use (Bohler, 2024; Berkley Group, 2023; Fairfax County, 2024). These additional requirements include design-specific elements (e.g., noise-canceling facades, expanded setbacks, fencing, and resource plans for water and energy use). However, the design strictly follows mission-critical facilities building codes, meaning that the facility is designed to operate uninterrupted without pausing for routine maintenance (McKay, 2020). Such energy and water prioritization must be accounted for when evaluating regional supply and demand. The design of a data center site is highly size-sensitive. The land use scale can range from “small-site” configurations of approximately 10,000 square feet to hyperscale campuses exceeding 1,000,000 square feet (Fluet, 2021). Site design oversight extends to the repurposing of existing buildings, which is relevant when data center developments leverage brownfields redevelopment. Such development can benefit from reinforced transmission lines, emergency power supplies, and specialized acoustic facades or fencing for soundproofing. The scale of the facility must match the electricity transmission compatibility, as well as the setbacks and buffers where industrial parcels abut residential or metropolitan areas. These requirements must come from an inclusive planning process that identify residents’ concerns (Burwell-Naney et al., 2013; Maranville et al., 2009; Bullard, 2018). Inclusive planning processes typically exhibit early and frequent involvement in all aspects of the decision-making processes, including goal setting, regional impact analysis, and request for additional design elements on top of existing guidelines. However, planning processes typically are politically charged and contentious because siting processes involve social choices where the interests of multiple parties collide with each other (Banfield, 2017). Often cities and counties want tax revenue and employment opportunities, with states in the background competing for technological innovation and economic development, while residents often seek protection against negative impacts on their quality of life. A complicating factor emerging recently involves localities implementing time-constrained restrictive measures to allow communities to take a collective “planning breath” before deciding on data centers. Examples of such measures include data center development moratoriums, interim restrictions on essential infrastructure, urban growth boundaries, and public facility ordinances, each of which can be used to intentionally decelerate the development pipeline (Njuguna et al., 2025). Such local restrictive measures may complement state-level disincentives or simply represent state-local land use conflicts that drive data center development to neighboring states (Duke, 2004; Hooghe et al., 2020). 5 Environmental and social equity impacts Environmental and social equity considerations for data centers, common to virtually all forms of development, include demands on resource supplies. Data centers typically consume prodigious amounts of land to construct large buildings, electricity to run the computers, and water to control the temperature of the facility (Lipsey, 2026). Existing land, water, and electricity availability varies widely from state-to-state and within states, as well as over time, complicating any efforts to generalize about site suitability for data centers. To date, developers appear to have preferred large parcels of land at the urban-suburban interface for data center proposals because of the (1) availability of cheaper land than in urbanized areas; (2) access to energy transmission capacity and broadband access, often lacking in rural areas; (3) adjacency to existing data centers, also located for access to transmission compatibility and signal processing optimization (Shao et al., 2025); and (4) the potential that energy prices well be more affordable than in highly urbanized areas or remote rural areas, which can vary widely (Onodera et al., 2025). Recent proposals for massive data center projects in rural Montana and Utah, however, suggest that historic preferences for parcels at the urban–rural interface may be shifting as the scale of data centers increases (Macur, 2026; Healy, 2026). Further complicating a holistic understanding of the resource impacts of data centers are place-based context, data center design choices, and technological innovations. For example, shifting water supply from urban and suburban uses to data centers on the exurban fringe may create different trade-offs than shifting water supply from agricultural uses to data centers in rural areas (Healy, 2026). Likewise, energy resource impacts will vary based both on the type of energy source, for example utility-scale wind and solar versus legacy coal plants, as well as whether new energy generation comes online as part of the project or whether the data center competes with other users for existing energy supply. At the same time, technological innovations may offset some of the resource impacts, for instance, the indirect environmental benefits from optimizing work efficiency enabled by data infrastructures and large-scale computation (Lei et al., 2026; Ayyildiz et al., 2025). In addition to resource impacts, environmental impacts of data centers include various forms of pollution widely documented in news accounts, even as academic reviews of the types of pollution and their impacts have only begun to emerge recently. For example, lawsuits have recently been filed against data centers for noise pollution, both in terms of audible noises that can harm hearing and lower quality of life and inaudible vibrations that can damage property and reduce property values (Hassan, 2026). Some data centers are powered by on-site natural gas turbines, which in turn release harmful particulate matter into the local air, complicating efforts to reduce negative impacts of air pollution; when data centers are concentrated in poorer neighborhoods, environmental justice concerns arise as well. Beyond noise and air pollution, on-site power generation and core data center activities generate large amounts of heat, which contribute to urban heat islands, in some cases generating heat equivalent of 40,000 homes and raising local temperatures by 4 degrees F in nearby neighborhoods (Sailor et al., 2026). Data centers powered by fossil fuel sources contribute greenhouse gas emissions that exacerbate anthropogenic climate change, potentially erasing many of the gains in climate change mitigation made in recent years through shifts to low-emission technologies like wind and solar generation. Over time, however, adjustments to data center design and construction—for instance using quieter liquid cooling rather than fan cooling—may mitigate some of these pollution impacts, although whether data center companies will make these often costly changes without federal, state, or local regulation remains to be seen. While national or even regional-state comprehensive analyses of the negative economic impacts of concentrations of data centers remain to be completed, recent studies of individual data center projects raise alarm bells in the realm of public health. For example, on-site power generation using natural gas at a single Loudon (VA) data center may generate $53 to $99 million in annual health costs due to particulate matter that will be breathed by residents in this densely populated region (Piedmont Environmental Council, 2026). Similarly, xAI’s massive Colossus II data center on the border of Tennessee and Mississippi outside of Memphis, which will be powered by methane gas turbines could generate $30–$44 million in annual health costs due to particulate matter (Southern Environmental Law Center, 2026) These impact analyses build on a long tradition of scholarship showing that industrial land use and locally unwanted land use (LULU) disproportionately harm to low-income and minority neighborhoods (Maantay, 2001; Bullard, 2018). As data centers are increasingly located in poorer areas (whether urban, suburban, exurban, or rural), which tend to have less political capital to resist unwanted land uses and tend to be communities of color, historic patterns of environmental injustice will be exacerbated. Whether data centers will have cumulative positive or negative impacts on property values and local economic activity remains an area mainly of speculation and merits additional research (Gabriel, 2026). On the one hand, data centers can offer localized economic benefits because the facility owners pay property taxes (when incentives packages due to provide exemptions), but the data centers themselves generate comparatively few demands on local governments, such as school systems, parks and recreation, and social services, compared to residential land uses. On the other hand, proximity to a data center can make existing housing and commercial properties and vacant land less attractive for long-term investment because of the noise, air, and other forms of pollution. Data center construction can be disruptive to nearby small businesses and residents (Cartwright, 2026). Additionally, if data center development precipitates the need for expanded energy and water infrastructure, especially electric transmission capacity, then cumulative costs of data centers may rise for state and local governments, as well as residents and businesses facing rising utility bills (Cáceres et al., 2024). Moving forward, part of the challenge in discerning whether the economic impacts are fairly distributed will be in the realm of causality, as data centers will likely be placed in areas where land is cheaper and local resistance is lower, both of which tend to be associated with lower starting property values. Given the variation of the local resource context and the rapidly increasing scale of data centers being proposed, site-specific environmental and social impacts reviews and monitoring will be critical for understanding impacts on land, water, and energy resources and infrastructure moving forward. So, too, will clearer procedures for developing, applying, and monitoring land use regulations and site design requirements, as detailed above. As federal and state officials develop incentive packages that preempt local control (Kollar, 2026), planning process for data centers will be less visible in the public sphere (Sanchez et al., 2025). This review of the literature highlights how data center development as part of the fourth industrial revolution challenges state and local governments, including creating internal tensions as a result of mismatches over time, space, levels of government, and planning values and goals. The specific review of state incentives, local land use regulations and site design considerations, as well as environmental and social equity concerns, leads us to these empirical research questions: What policy and incentive activities are state governments initiating that shape the local landscape for siting data centers? What planning and regulatory approaches are local governments using to influence the siting of data centers? How can the emergent state policies and local regulatory approaches be categorized to help practitioners understand their context and their range of options? 6 Methods 6.1 Research design We use an exploratory research design to examine the policy and regulatory frameworks that guide data center development in the United States. To answer our three research questions, we did an online search to identify state preemption efforts for large-scale digital and industrial development, with particular attention to mechanisms that expedite data center development. Then, we did a detailed read-through and analyzed all 50 states’ economic incentive packages aimed at attracting data center development and the preemption strategies of the predominant states. To couple with the state incentive analysis, we strategically selected one jurisdiction (either a county or a city) for each state to capture the zoning and design forefront of current planning practices. Specifically, our selection focuses on jurisdictions with the top numbers of data centers in a state (Data Center Map, 2025) to make our sample representative for jurisdictions with relatively mature data center development (Yigitcanlar, 2015; APA Virginia, 2023), although zoning codes were available at either the county or city levels. Such selection involves a combination of counties to account for zoning ordinances established either at a county or a city level. Such selection allows for an initial scan of proper zoning regulations tailored to accommodate state preemption and economic incentives. We listed our sample jurisdictions in Supplementary Table 1. Finally, we collected and analyzed staff reports that are available as case studies to showcase how current local governments harness the rapid development of data centers. 6.2 Data collection Our primary data collection centers on activities by state-level legislatures. We began with a search of websites and professional reports by real estate tax advisory firms, data center developers, data center forums, and states’ legislative websites (Hughett, 2025; H5 Data Center, 2025; SDIA Alliance, 2025) to identify the mandates and incentives along with the requirements set forth by each state. Using this information, we read through a range of websites, from senate bill, house bill, to passed legislature documented by agencies like the state department of Commerce (see Supplementary Table 3 for details with links). Then, we extracted the key incentive policy items regarding the tax incentives and categorized states along a spectrum from pro-investment to anti-investment in digital infrastructure, providing a cross-sectional view of states’ prioritization of digital infrastructures. To assess local planning and zoning ordinances, we collected two types of documents: (1) city and county zoning ordinances, and (2) available staff reports on data centers. We searched for publicly available zoning ordinances of corresponding cities and counties in our sample on the cities’ and counties’ official websites. For each city and county, we searched whether data center appeared to be a keyword mentioned in the ordinance. If the data center was not mentioned, we focused our attention on the industrial use section. If the jurisdiction provides a designated policy on data centers, we kept it for further analysis. To analyze zoning ordinances, we first established the coding protocol based on a list of professional reports (Lauber Municipal Law, 2024; Berkley Group, 2023; Bohler, 2024). These reports offer technicalities of land use and design guidelines (see Supplementary Table 2 for specifics of coding items), documenting the requirements for minimum lot size, maximum building height, distance from the metro line and residential area, setback, façade, screening, lighting, and building codes. The purpose of content coding is aimed to capture the existence of policy requirements; one coder deductively analyzed zoning ordinances in a binary manner following the coding procedures for planning documents (Lyles and Stevens, 2014). For instance, if there exists a required minimum lot size, we coded 1; otherwise, 0. Although one coder coded all 50 jurisdictions’ ordinance, we randomly selected 10 jurisdictions’ ordinance and used AI (i.e., Gemini) to double-check the coding results to reach at least 90% agreement between human and AI coding results. To analyze the staff reports, a co-author (a trained urban planner) inductively coded the available documents to document critical design elements across land use, environmental management, social equity, and energy requirements (Bronin, 2008; Burby and May, 1997). This coding followed an iterative process, alternating between examining evolving data center design guidelines and refining the local land use regulations incorporated into the conceptual framework (Table 1). We acknowledge that these staff reports capture a cross-sectional snapshot of current knowledge, with the caveat that not all potential practices could be exhausted. Nonetheless, staff reports offer exceptional value by capturing professional planning judgments regarding land use, even where limited documentation exists (Johnson and Lyles, 2016). 6.3 Limitations A major limitation is the fast-moving nature of every facet of AI and data centers. Our analysis provides a snapshot of the data center development in late 2025 and early 2026. By 2027, the landscape likely will have changed, if not at the national scale, then certainly in some states and localities, given that in many cases a policy could be adapted or even reversed through the action of a single actor (e.g., a governor) or small group of actors (e.g., a majority of a small city or county council). Geographically, our study focusing on a small sample of populous regions overlooks the rural regions which also attracts data center development in the decades to come. We anticipate future studies will extend our baseline work in a number of ways, including: longitudinal analyses of the evolution of state policy frameworks over time, as these are in tremendous flux as the political dynamic of data centers changes; systematic comparative studies of local regulations, perhaps using censuses of city and counties within a state or set of states or using stratified samples across a wide range of states; and large-n regression analyses of the correlations of numerous drivers of data center developments in an effort to move toward understanding the causal mechanisms at play in this policy arena. Such an approach could enable regression modeling or other forms of analysis that move toward causal explanations for changes in the data center regulatory landscape. Likewise, deep case studies involving policy analysis, media analysis, and interviews with key actors will be essential for understanding how the policy frameworks shaping data centers are evolving over time. Nonetheless, our analysis provides an important baseline understanding of this policy landscape and highlights dynamics and trends worthy of further research. 7 Findings 7.1 State preemption The major finding related to our first research question about state policy activity is that state policy frameworks around data centers vary widely within two main lines of activity (Table 2). In one line of activity, states have sought to limit or entirely remove the ability of local governments to block data center development, with a few states moving in the opposite direction by aiming for state-wide moratoria on data center development. In another line of activity, states have designed suites of incentives to attract data centers within their borders. State have taken a range of approaches to pre-emption of local land use, wherein a state government limits or overrides the authority of local governments, often cities and counties, to regulate land use (Kollar, 2026). For example, in Pennsylvania, the State Bill 991, formally titled “Data Center Siting and Permitting Act” focused on accelerating the development across the Commonwealth. The goal of the act is to reduce regulatory friction and shorten the timeline for data center approval, for which cities are encouraged to pre-select at least 15 preapproved sites (e.g., decommissioned power plant) for expedited permitting process intended to bypass cities’ land use review, permitting and approval. In West Virginia, data centers are categorized as high-impact microgrid districts such that any development is subject to the district-level requirements, but not the county or city zoning, building permitting, or licensing (West Virginia Legislature, 2025). Ohio, in a similar vein, applies regulations on the electrical infrastructure footprint. That is, when a data center requires on-site electrical substation or high-voltage transmission line upgrades, land use requirements will be tailored to mega-scale tech corridors for specific setbacks, structural footprint, bypassing city and county zoning codes. On the other hand, the state preemption can be governed and monitored by a specialized committee. South Carolina’s data center is certified by the state Public Service Commission (PSC), a special task force that determines physical land use impacts per development request. Beyond granting permission for construction, PSC also acts as the ultimate authority to set standards for operation (water use, noise control, and light mitigation). On the other end of the spectrum, in New York, Maryland, and New Hampshire state policy makers have introduced proposals to place moratoria on data centers state-wide, although whether these proposals can gain approval remains to be seen. New York is in the process of debating a 3-year moratoria and Oklahoma has adopted a moratorium that will automatically kick in once electricity consumption exceeds 100 million watts in the state. Our categorization decision is mainly driven by three criteria with scoring (1) whether there exists a level of tax-based incentive and duration (1 for yes, 0 for no); (2) whether the tax incentives come with conditions such as job creation, investment thresholds, and others (−1 for yes, 0 for no); and (3) whether the state requires environmental assessment (−1 for yes and 0) (West Virginia Legislature, 2025; SDIA Alliance, 2025). By adding up the three scores, the values of each state rank them based on the level of incentives—incentivizing states receive higher scores, while disincentivizing states receive lower scores. For instance, a state using moratoria will automatically trigger a − 2 in score, resulting from no tax exemption (0), pending conditions for development (−1), and an environmental impact check (−1). The specific incentive baseline reflects states’ commitment to attracting development—the duration of tax incentives, investment requirements, and any conditions or constraints before construction. For each category, we listed a few example states, demonstrating the key characteristics of state policy design. For instance, Minnesota previously had a high incentive but now has a moratorium in place, and the moratorium stops development; hence, no tax exemption is to be received. States like Tennessee, Louisiana, and Ohio have prioritized data center development with long-term tax exemption commitment on sales, use, and property taxes. States with high-level incentives require large capital investments and have minimal land development requirements. For instance, North Dakota requires the minimum development area to be 15,000 square feet. In return, states require job creation depending on the scale of investment. Nevada requires 10 jobs at the $25 million investment and 50 jobs at the $100 million investment levels. States’ tax exemptions for construction and low electricity rates have widely been used for data center development (Fluet, 2021; National Conference of State Legislatures, 2026; SDIA Alliance, 2025). The House Bill 1,223 in Texas, for instance, provides 6.25% sales and use tax exemptions for 10–15 years if a tech facility occupies at least 100,000 square feet with a minimum capital investment of $200 million dollars. Virginia, a state with a great number of data centers, exempts retail and sales tax from equipment and software purchases. This so-called Data Center Retail Sales and Use Exemption was made available at the beginning of July 2020 through 2035, as a sign of long-term commitment in digital infrastructure investment (Goodwin, 2025; Virginia Code §58.1-609.3, 2025; Virginia Economic Development Partnership, 2025). Some states provide moderate tax incentives, and these incentives can come with conditions. The State of Iowa offers a partial tax refund, contingent upon investment levels, but the tax refund is limited to a 5-year term. The exemption is capped at 10 years for urban sites and 15 years for rural sites. Utah provides a tax exemption only for equipment, but not for utility and operation costs. Besides operation costs, Utah requires specific cooling systems—closed-loop cooling (for energy efficiency) or a dry cooling system (to prevent overuse of water)—for new data centers to save water. Such energy-friendly measures indicate awareness of environmental impacts, and states require developers to pay for their own impact studies on water and electricity. States with low or no incentives appears to have a moratorium on the way but have yet to arrive. These states, like Delaware and New Hampshire, provide no tax incentives and started the scrutiny on environmental impacts. Finally, the disincentive states have imposed moratoriums, rolled back, or repealed tax incentives. Examples include Washington and Wisconsin. 7.2 Local land use regulation The core finding related to our second question about local land use regulation is that most counties and cities use conditional permits related to industrial land use to regulate data centers. About half of our sample counties, 24 out of 50, have some building design requirements specific to industrial use (Figure 1). A small number of counties (n = 11) are equipped with a formal statement on their zoning, comprehensive plan, or on the county website that allows for data center development. Note our sample targets those attracts most data centers in a state. Additionally, most counties have siting requirements away from residential and metro boundaries. Specifically, 39 out of 50 counties require a buffer distance between industrial land use and either residential or metro area. A total of 36 counties require some form of setback. Façade is not among the top priorities for industrial building requirements, although a few counties suggest special façade material to mitigate noise (Cumberland County, 2025). Although energy efficiency is referenced in staff reports, specific measures and performance monitoring for data center operations remain unclear. Regarding social equity and environmental concerns, all four jurisdictions have focused on the tangible benefits and potential harm brought by data centers. They clearly delineate boundaries between data centers and residential areas, supported by enhanced buffering requirements. Public funding streams are made available to support neighborhood infrastructure. For instance, Linn County, Iowa, establishes a Community Betterment Fund in partnership with developers to support parks, open space, and public facilities (Descant, 2026). Other strategies include comprehensive impact studies, transparent public engagement evaluation, historic preservation efforts, and water management plans. Local communities have reached a consensus that data centers should not be built on the promise of undermining locally available natural resources. Developers must address long-term electricity and water needs without affecting residents. 8 Discussion and implications Findings highlight tensions and coordination among national, state, and local policy frameworks amid the rapidly changing landscape of planning practice driven by data center development. Building on our exploratory analysis of these frameworks, we propose a multi-level governance conceptual framework that helps explain states’ ever-shifting policy stances (Figure 2). At one end, states with strong incentive structures and land-use preemption (Quadrant 4) centralize planning power, streamlining control to align with state priorities (Kollar, 2026; Warner and Bravo, 2025). This overriding of authority curtails municipal autonomy, allowing developers targeting these states to secure robust tax incentives while bypassing local friction. For instance, West Virginia’s 2025 Power Generation and Consumption Act established exempt districts to bypass county and city zoning, expediting review and approval processes (Kollar, 2026). Although West Virginia categorized as a moderate-incentive state, offering tax benefits alongside limited environmental constraints, project-based interventions illustrate similar dynamics. Figure 2 Intergovernmental policy framework. Counties whose goals align with state-level digital infrastructure expansion tend to establish special districts through refined zoning ordinances to streamline development. For instance, Prince William County, Virginia, created a data center opportunity zone overlay to attract high-tech industrial development while acknowledging potential environmental externalities (Prince William County Planning Office, 2026). Such synchronization between state and local governance reflects the intergovernmental coordination depicted in Quadrant one. Conversely, counties and cities with robust land-use authority that remain insulated from state intervention often exhibit policy misalignment with the state level (Quadrant two). This dynamic characterized by state-level incentives clashing with local opposition frequently mobilizes residents to voice concerns and push back against data center proposals (Macur, 2026). For example, New York has proposed a statewide moratorium to help reconcile these multi-level governance tensions. This process, wherein local friction attempts to reshape the state-level narrative, exemplifies bottom-up coordination. Localities in the third quadrant are characterized by flexible land-use regulations at both local and state levels. Our baseline analysis operates within this quadrant, assuming localities initially maintain a neutral stance while managing large parcels of land. A typical Quadrant three scenario involves municipalities lacking explicit policies either supporting or restricting development. Such default conditions remain flexible, easily shifting into any of the other three quadrants depending on historical contexts and evolving planning mandates involving data centers. Ultimately, aggressive state economic development packages can effectively drive digital infrastructure siting in these flexible environments where economic imperatives outweigh other considerations. States that delegate less land-use authority to local jurisdictions tend to exert greater influence over development through preemption and economic incentives. This intergovernmental policy challenge can appear unprecedented given the potentially transformative nature of AI, the exponential acceleration of the industry, and the rapid transition of data centers from obscurity to prominent local political debates. Yet, the coupled AI-data center policy challenge shares deep structural parallels with other complex land-use domains, such as siting renewable energy infrastructure (Njuguna et al., 2025; Rochas, 2025; Zhou et al., 2024), mitigating natural hazards like floods and heat waves (Smith et al., 2013; Keith and Meerow, 2026), and addressing affordable housing shortages (Ríos-Ocampo and Gary, 2025). These fields all grapple with multi-tiered incentive structures, tensions between top-down state initiatives and deeply entrenched traditions of local home rule, and the fundamental conundrum of balancing shared societal resource trade-offs with individual property rights. Consequently, widespread community-led opposition has sparked waves of moratoria, offering policymakers critical windows to restructure multi-level governance and accommodate priorities at various levels. Ultimately, fostering more robust public engagement strategies will be essential to bridging intergovernmental divides and facilitating constructive state-citizen dialogue. We identified a list of factors, including employment opportunities and job transformation, negotiated utility and infrastructure upgrade, and co-location of renewable energy, that may ultimately benefit a locality but are rarely made it to the decision-making process. The implication is a need for a “resource-first” land use framework that transcends traditional buffering. County and city leaders often struggle to adapt land-use planning systems to foster economic benefits, mainly in the form of short-term construction jobs and increased property tax base, while mitigating the highly localized social and environmental risks, which range across a wide swath of concerns detailed below. This understanding of the dynamics of AI and data centers across scales helps explain why the explosion in controversies has unfolded in hearings in city and county commission rooms in virtually every state in the nation. As demonstrated by Utah’s mandate for closed-loop cooling and New York’s proposed environmental impact moratoriums, the future of data center planning must move beyond visual screening to address the “invisible” impacts on the electrical grid and water table. For instance, initiating the data center conversation by strengthening the existing electricity supply infrastructure and make them more sustainable and efficient regionally (Lei et al., 2025). Although our findings stemmed from data center-dense jurisdictions, we have seen a development trend extending to rural areas, signifying needs for scrutinizing regional natural resource exploitation and maintain sustainable regional development. Literature indicates that equitable planning extends beyond informational meetings; it necessitates transparent participation and Community Benefit Agreements that prevent data centers from becoming exploitative instruments of capital accumulation. For practitioners, this requires a paradigm shift, moving from a reactive stance that merely designates industrial land to a proactive approach that integrates energy-use reporting and water management plans into the earliest stages of site review. Building on our multi-level governance analysis, we recommend that planners critically evaluate policy gaps across levels of government, identify design aspects with the potential to adversely impact residents, and weigh the long-term community benefits against infrastructural costs. Accurately diagnosing state-local policy (mis) alignment helps foster regulatory adjustments that balance economic growth with environmental and social sustainability. 9 Conclusion Our study highlights how the rapid expansion of AI and digital infrastructure has outpaced existing intergovernmental policy frameworks, creating a fractured landscape of state and local authority. This tension manifests as a stark continuum: at one end, “incentivizing” states utilize legislative preemption to strip local zoning control and fast-track data center siting, while at the other, “disincentivizing” states enact moratoria to safeguard local resources. This friction between centralized state economic priorities and municipal autonomy mirrors long-standing land-use battles over affordable housing production and renewable energy infrastructure. Ultimately, navigating this multi-tiered governance challenge requires an understanding of how state-level interventions can systematically override local community agency, leaving city leaders to deal with highly localized impacts. If controversy around data centers continues to grow in the next few years, as seems likely, planners may be forced to shift from a reactive posture of merely making industrial land available for conforming land uses to proactive postures involving contentious and prolonged reworking of zoning codes and related regulations. Rather than relying on traditional, passive buffering techniques like setback requirements or visual screening, data center proposals may push planners to create zoning ordinances integrating performance standards (e.g., utility, water, and environmental impact assessments). If so, planners should consider explicitly incorporating overlays that mandate closed-loop cooling systems, tie site approvals to localized grid-capacity thresholds, and require transparent, front-end energy use reporting. By embedding resource-first metrics directly into the local development code and leveraging tools like Community Benefit Agreements, planners can ensure that digital infrastructure development respects ecological boundaries and delivers genuine, long-term equity to host communities.
