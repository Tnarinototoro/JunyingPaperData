Sambath * AS Ananyaa Subramanian DS Dhanisha Sethupathy School of Computer Science and Engineering, Vellore Institute of Technology, Chennai, India Article metrics View details Abstract Introduction: In fast-urbanizing areas, municipal solid waste collection still runs on a fixed schedule that ignores real-time bin state, wasting fleet capacity and leaving overflow-prone bins unserviced. The main problems are as much about governance as engineering: service delivery is unlinked from need, with no accountability signal; statutory segregation duties are unenforceable for want of an evidentiary base; enforcement instruments are ungraduated; and the distributional burden is invisible. Smart-bin systems presently address sensing or routing in isolation, funneling telemetry through one cloud tier whose wide-area latency is incompatible with time-critical hazard alerts. Methods: We present SWMS, a three-tier edge/fog/cloud system in which smart bins sense fill level, methane, temperature, and weight, district fog nodes evaluate thresholds and compute routes locally, and a cloud tier runs graduated compliance enforcement and an Eco-Score layer. All results are computational, not measurements of a deployment. No physical prototype was deployed, and all bin data are synthetic; the compliance and gamification layers were implemented but not validated by any user study, though the sensor error model was calibrated to real gas-sensor data. Results: A seeded Monte Carlo model places the fog-mediated alert path at 30.9 ms against 233.1 ms for a cloud counterfactual, confirmed by two discrete-event simulations. A comparison with a proven optimal solution shows that greedy+2-opt closes to within 5.1%, matching the best tuned metaheuristic at three orders of magnitude lower compute. A service-parity comparison cuts daily collection distance by 37.9%, reproduced at 38.7% on an actual OpenStreetMap street network. Scalability to 20,000 bins follows O(n1.72), and fault injection raises overflow-alert F1 from 0.70 to 0.80 at 50% sensor faults. Discussion: We situate the design in the public-governance and regulatory-governance literatures and identify the obstacles a deployment faces: statutory authority, due process, administrative discretion, attribution in communal bins, distributive incidence, legitimacy, data protection, and municipal capacity. Because a non-zero sensor error rate entails a non-zero wrongful-penalty rate, we conclude that the compliance layer should first be deployed as a non-penal, evidentiary, and advisory instrument rather than a penal one. 1 Introduction Growing urban populations and tightening sustainability constraints put increasing pressure on municipal solid waste (MSW) management. India's Central Pollution Control Board reports that urban areas generate roughly 150,000 tonnes of solid waste a day, of which under 30% is scientifically treated [Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB), 2022]. Collection wastes journeys: trucks run fixed routes on fixed schedules, ignoring bin state and servicing empty bins while overflowing ones wait. The overflow causes soil pollution, vector-borne disease risk and unaccounted greenhouse-gas emissions—especially methane, whose 100-year warming potential is some 25–28 × that of CO2. IoT sensing is a tangible response, and a growing literature instruments bins with fill-level and, more recently, gas sensors (Section 2). Two structural gaps remain. First, most deployments route all telemetry to a single cloud tier, adding wide-area latency to what should be a sub-second alert path for fire risk and toxic methane build-up. Second, sensing and routing are treated as the whole problem: telemetry drives a route, and that is it. Municipal operations also need policy—graduated enforcement for chronic non-compliance, and incentives that make compliance the easier choice. Routing optimizers that ignore compliance underuse their telemetry, and fines imposed without predictive dispatch generate friction for no benefit. 1.1 The governance problems this system addresses Presenting this system merely as a sensing-and-routing optimization does not do justice to what municipal authorities are trying to fix. Municipal solid waste is a classic case of a public service whose failures are governance failures before they are engineering failures (Kaza et al., 2018; Guerrero et al., 2013). The technology has existed for a century, yet coverage, source segregation, and disposal standards remain poor across much of South Asia (Doron and Jeffrey, 2018; Mani and Singh, 2016): much of the waste generated stays uncollected or poorly processed. The engineering fixes exist; what shortens the shelf life of any design is institutional, regulatory, and implementation failure. We therefore state at the outset the five concrete governance problems the architecture is designed to act on, and return in Section 7 to the regulatory and policy obstacles each of them raises. Allocative failure with no service-accountability signal: fleet capacity is allocated by timetable rather than by state, so trucks service empty bins while full ones wait. The core issue is informational: neither authority nor citizen holds a durable, timestamped record of which bins overflowed and for how long, so under-service is unattributable and cannot be made the object of an enforceable performance standard. Performance-based regulation relies on exactly the measurement base that is missing (Sparrow, 2000; Dunleavy et al., 2006). A statutory duty without an evidentiary base: India's Solid Waste Management Rules 2016 (Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, Government of India, 2016) impose duties directly on every waste generator: Rule 4(1)(a) requires segregation into three streams and handover to authorized collectors, Rule 4(2) prohibits throwing, burning or burying waste on streets and public spaces, and Rule 4(3) obliges payment of the user fee set in local bye-laws. Rule 15(zf) in turn requires local authorities to frame bye-laws containing the criteria for levying spot fines on persons who litter or fail to comply. Urban local bodies nonetheless hold no systematic generator-level record of conduct, making enforcement episodic, discretionary, and hard to defend when contested. This is the classic regulatory-capacity problem: inspection resources are scarce, and targeting them rationally requires data the regulator does not have (Sparrow, 2000; Black and Baldwin, 2010; Hood et al., 2001). Instrument-choice failure: sanction without graduation, information without escalation: where enforcement does occur, the penalty is typically flat, with no escalation path—the pattern the regulatory literature associates with resistance and legitimacy loss rather than compliance (Bardach and Kagan, 1982). Pure information campaigns, conversely, exhaust their effect quickly. Responsive regulation prescribes an enforcement pyramid: persuasion first, escalating sanctions only against persistent non-compliance (Ayres and Braithwaite, 1992); smart regulation prescribes complementary instruments mixes rather than single instruments (Gunningham et al., 1998; Hood and Margetts, 2007; Howlett, 2019). The three-warning monthly buffer followed by a graduated fine, with a positive Eco-Score incentive running in parallel (Section 3), implements that pyramid—a framing that the earlier version of this article left implicit. A time-critical hazard that the administrative cycle cannot serve: a municipality can be held liable for duty-of-care hazards such as methane build-up and bin fires, but an alert path completing a wide-area round trip cannot underwrite a response-time commitment under load. Response-time standards are precisely the enforceable service obligation a regulator can write into a contract or bye-law (Baldwin et al., 2012); an architecture that demonstrably meets one is a prerequisite for writing it. Distributive invisibility: without per-bin longitudinal data we cannot identify which neighborhoods absorb chronic under-service, so inequity in a basic public service persists unmeasured and uncontested. Longitudinal telemetry is thus an equity-audit instrument before it is an efficiency instrument—an application of urban data the smart-city governance literature treats as among the more justifiable (Kitchin, 2016; Meijer and Bolívar, 2016). Put this way, the contribution is a policy instrument, not just an optimization. The reframing cuts both ways: instruments assigning burdens to identifiable people attract legal, procedural, and distributive constraints that an optimizer does not, and Section 7 treats those as design requirements rather than deployment details. 1.2 Positioning and contributions We do not claim novelty in any single sensing, routing, or fog-placement technique; Section 2 makes the prior art on each explicit. By taking the compliance-relevant decision—threshold evaluation and route update—out of the cloud critical path, the fog tier allows a graduated enforcement engine and a citizen gamification layer to be coupled end-to-end. (2015) added GPS-based fleet tracking on a cloud-centric pipeline. Recent multi-sensor deployments have closed much of the gap this article might otherwise have claimed. Mehta et al. (2025) tested ESP8266 bins with ultrasonic, MQ-4 methane, MQ-136 H2S, and MQ-137 NH3 sensors and GPS in Malad, Mumbai, validating readings against a Testo 340 analyzer at 96–97% agreement and reporting a greedy dynamic-CVRP routing layer with statistically validated results (paired t = 4.73, p = 0.0012). We cite this prior art rather than elide it, as it is nearest to our sensing and routing halves. Alsayaydeh et al. (2025) performed a five-sensor retrofit with a 30-day, 12-bin municipal trial and GPS-guided routing. Alnanih et al. (2025) and Bou-Rabee et al. (2025) contributed software-engineering-integrated monitoring and a battery-free design, respectively, both lacking a fog tier. Chen et al. (2025) performed waste-type recognition through audio and RGB feature fusion, while Da Silva and González (2025) and Henaien et al. (2024) performed more general multi-parameter capture. Kumar et al. (2025) extend the AI-IoT paradigm to hazardous hospital waste, combining real-time monitoring with machine-learning classification for segregation and compliance tracking. It is therefore evident that integrating sensing, policy enforcement, and sustainability objectives in one architecture has value—the principle we apply to the municipal context. None of the above places a fog tier between bins and cloud, and none couples sensing to compliance enforcement; both are what we add, not what we invent. 2.2 Fog computing and edge analytics Bonomi et al. (2012) proposed fog computing as latency-reducing middleware for smart-city IoT; Aazam and Huh (2016) showed fog-cloud cooperation filters 40–70% of telemetry redundancy before the cloud; and Mahmud et al. (2018) studied module-placement policies motivating the offload of latency-sensitive logic to intermediate nodes. Recent work sharpens this: Sfaxi et al. (2024) propose a proactive latency-aware placement policy, Hossam et al. (2024) benchmark energy-aware placement in iFogSim against cloud-only and latency-aware baselines—the protocol we mirror in Section 4.5—and Hassan and Mehmood (2025) evaluate a multi-tier edge-fog framework in iFogSim2, noting simplified topological simulation as a limitation. We take the same care: our main latency number sits within an analytical component-budget model with Monte Carlo uncertainty propagation, which we have cross-checked against a discrete-event iFogSim run of the same topology (Section 5.2) that agrees within the model's sensitivity band. Hardware parameterization (MIPS, RAM, and bandwidth) follows iFogSim (Gupta et al., 2017), still the reference toolkit. 2.3 Dynamic routing and logistics heuristics Waste collection routing is a version of the Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP). Kim et al. (2014) utilize adaptive large neighborhood search, while Buhrkal et al. (2012) and Ghahramani et al. (2022) claim that greedy nearest-neighbor heuristics refreshed from live sensor triggers are competitive at far lower cost than exact or metaheuristic solvers—a claim we do not accept and test directly (Section 5.3). The 2024–2025 literature has shifted to learned and hybrid solvers: Wang H. et al. (2025) improved an ant-colony algorithm for MSW transport in an Internet-of-Vehicles setting, Zhao et al. (2024) combined Q-learning hyperheuristics with particle swarm optimization for energy-aware dynamic routing, Khallaf et al. (2025) with several CEVRP papers (Wang C. et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025) applied deep reinforcement learning to capacitated routing more broadly, and Sepúlveda-Campos et al. (2025) provided a fill-level-dependent service-time formulation more rigorous than threshold-triggered dispatch. A study that advocates plain greedy nearest-neighbor alone makes a case against a stronger field than it recognizes, which is why Section 5.3 benchmarks ours directly against this landscape—2-opt, a genetic algorithm, ant colony optimization, and particle swarm optimization on the same instances. 2.4 Public governance, policy instruments, and regulatory governance The smart-waste systems literature has developed largely in isolation from the public-governance literature, and the omission is as much ours as the field's. This subsection outlines the work bearing on an architecture whose output is a penalty, an incentive, and a service obligation. 2.4.1 Regulatory governance and enforcement design Baldwin et al. (2012) give the standard account of regulatory strategy and instrument selection, and Hood et al. (2001) show that regulatory regimes are shaped less by the objective size of a risk than by the information and institutional context around it—directly relevant to a system whose principal claim is to supply information previously absent. The responsive regulation of Ayres and Braithwaite (1992) supplies the enforcement pyramid that our warning-then-fine buffer instantiates: because starting at the top of the pyramid provokes resistance, sanctions escalate only as cooperation fails. Bardach and Kagan (1982) document that resistance empirically as “regulatory unreasonableness.” Black and Baldwin (2010) argue that risk-based regulation must account for regulators' limited attention and changing behavior, and Sparrow (2000) points out that effective regulation proceeds problem-by-problem on measured risk concentrations rather than by uniform inspection. Both describe what telemetry-driven targeting would enable, and both warn that a measurement system determines which problems become visible and hence which get addressed. Gunningham et al. (1998) argue for complementary instrument mixes in environmental policy rather than single instruments, the position our combined incentive-plus-sanction design adopts. On why people comply at all, May (2004) distinguishes affirmative from deterrence-based motivations, and Tyler (2006) shows that perceived procedural fairness predicts compliance more reliably than expected penalties—a finding with sharp implications for an automated fine (Section 7). 2.4.2 Policy instruments and policy design Hood and Margetts (2007) classify the government's tools by the resources they draw on: nodality, authority, treasure, and organization. Our system is unusual in engaging all four at once, being a detector (nodality), a penalty (authority), a fee and reward scheme (treasure), and a dispatch system (organization). Howlett (2019) treats the choice and calibration of instruments as a design problem with its own failure modes, notably incoherent instrument mixes. Dunleavy et al. (2006) characterize digital-era governance as reintegration and needs-based holism facilitated by digitization, which is a fair description of what a three-tier municipal stack attempts. 2.4.3 Administrative discretion and algorithmic regulation According to Lipsky (2010), policy is effectively made by street-level officials exercising discretion under scarcity, not by the statute alone. Bovens and Zouridis (2002) illustrate how information systems convert street-level bureaucracies into system-level bureaucracies: discretion therefore migrates from the officer to the system designer, with consequences for constitutional control. Peeters and Widlak (2018) describe the resulting structure as a “digital cage,” in which citizens cannot correct the records that determine how they are treated. The basics of technological due process are set out by Citron (2008): notice, reasons, auditability, and a genuine opportunity to contest. Coglianese and Lehr (2017) examine when automated decisions can lawfully carry administrative authority at all. Yeung (2018) provides an important critical account of algorithmic regulation, and the companion analysis of “hypernudge”. Yeung (2017) describes exactly the mechanism of a continuously updated behavioral score such as our Eco-Score. Veale and Brass (2019), Janssen and Kuk (2016), and Binns (2018) address, respectively, public-sector machine learning in practice, the limits of technocratic data-driven governance, and what algorithmic accountability requires of public reason. 2.4.4 Smart-city governance and its critics Meijer and Bolívar (2016) study smart urban governance and find that most “smart city” claims emphasize technology rather than governance arrangements. Kitchin (2016) sets out the ethical and political questions urban data systems raise; Vanolo (2014) argues that smart-city discourse operates as a disciplinary technology that relocates responsibility onto citizens; and Cardullo and Kitchin (2019) show empirically how thin “smart citizen” participation typically is—a caution we apply directly to our own gamification layer. On data protection specifically, Edwards (2016) and Van Zoonen (2016) examine why smart-city telemetry resists conventional privacy governance. 2.4.5 Waste governance and the informal sector Kaza et al. (2018) and Guerrero et al. (2013) establish that institutional and financial arrangements, rather than technology, dominate waste-management outcomes in developing cities. For India specifically, Doron and Jeffrey (2018) and Mani and Singh (2016) describe the policy landscape in which the Solid Waste Management Rules 2016 (Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, Government of India, 2016) operate. Critically for an enforcement system, Demaria and Schindler (2016), Gidwani (2015) and Gutberlet (2015) document that a large share of actual recycling labor is performed by informal workers whose livelihoods a formalized, penalty-bearing system can displace rather than support; Eubanks (2018) provides the general case that automated administrative systems tend to fall hardest on the poor. 2.4.6 What is missing The smart-waste systems we reviewed in Sections 2.1–2.3 do not engage this literature, and the governance literature in turn rarely engages the architectural choices—where a decision is computed, with what latency, and with what error rate—that determine whether a governance mechanism is administrable at all. We link the two together: Section 7 interprets the quantitative properties measured in Section 5 as regulatory parameters. 2.5 Digital governance and gamification Botsman and Rogers (2010) research sharing-economy incentive dashboards, and Thaler and Sunstein's nudge theory Thaler and Sunstein, (2008) motivates positive-reinforcement compliance. Our prototype does not clear the empirical bar set by recent gamified-recycling studies: Venturi et al. (2025) divide 26 participants across paper, app, and gamified-app conditions; Rosenlund et al. (2025) field-test a gamified recycling station with 15 households; and Saeedi (2024) designs a blockchain loyalty program structurally close to our Eco-Score. Our three-warning-then-fine buffer is structurally a graduated Pay-As-You-Throw tariff, and PAYT has a mature econometric literature we had not engaged: Rizzo and Secomandi (2024) study threshold tariffs directly, Compagnoni and Torbert (2026) provide a difference-in-differences causal evaluation, and Ghimire et al. (2024) study willingness-to-pay in Kathmandu, bearing on whether INR fines of our magnitude are realistic here. None of them legitimizes our particular fine schedule or Eco-Score curve, which is why we do not present our policy numbers as validated behavioral results (Section 5.9). 2.6 Comparison table and identified research gaps Table 2 summarizes a dozen of the most directly comparable systems across six capability axes. Logic is divided across three tiers. Figure 1 The proposed three-tier Smart Waste Management architecture. Threshold evaluation and route computation happen at the fog tier, off the cloud critical path (quantified in Section 4.5); the cloud tier carries only non-time-critical compliance and gamification state. Edge tier (smart bins): a microcontroller along with four sensors, namely ultrasonic for fill level, MQ-4 for methane, a load cell for weight, and a thermistor/DS18B20 for temperature, performing sampling, plausibility filtering, and timestamping before transmission. Fog tier (district nodes): a gateway of Raspberry Pi 4B class (2,800 MIPS, 4 GB RAM) takes in edge streams over a low-latency WLAN link and carries out threshold evaluation and route recomputation. This tier determines the alert-to-actuation latency budget (Section 4.5) and runs independently of the cloud round-trip for that reason. Cloud tier (global analytics): the compliance engine, longitudinal master records and city-wide gamification aggregation run on a shared 44,800 MIPS analytics server. Each experiment fixes a unit of replication: a generated routing instance, a paired static/dynamic 28-day run, a fault-rate run, or a repeated timing of one bin count. Replicate counts are chosen so the 95% confidence half-width on the primary metric is below 5% of its mean—n = 30 for the routing, dispatch, and fault studies, n = 3 per size for scalability, where run-to-run variance is small (CV < 2%). Reported intervals are 95% confidence intervals, with a bias-corrected bootstrap CI (10,000 resamples) additionally given for the dispatch saving. The dispatch comparison is paired on the same demand realization, so we use a paired t-test, apply the Shapiro–Wilk test to the paired differences to check normality (W = 0.967, p = 0.42), and use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test as a distribution-free confirmation; the effect size is Cohen's d for paired samples. The scalability exponent is obtained by ordinary least squares regression of log(time) against logn, with SE and CI taken from the regression. We apply no multiple-comparison correction, since each experiment tests one pre-specified primary hypothesis, and all p-values are two-sided. 4.7.1 No physical hardware prototype has been deployed, and no field-collected sensor data exists As every dataset is synthetic, these statistics measure how much the simulation varies under reseeding, not the sampling uncertainty one would see for a real population; practical significance is discussed alongside magnitude in Section 6. 5 Results 5.1 Provenance and status of the results No physical hardware prototype has been deployed, and no field-collected sensor data exists. Given the included set B with Haversine great-circle distances dij and a fixed depot b0 at the city centroid, a solver returns a permutation π of B minimizing from b0. The tour is open, with no forced return leg, since trucks continue to the disposal facility. The benchmark focuses on the sequencing sub-problem: instances are sized to one truck-load, so the 35-stop capacity constraint—matched to the 240 L-bin × compactor-volume ratio of Section 5.4—is not binding within an instance. Multi-vehicle assignment and capacity triage are handled upstream by the fullest-first queue of Section 5.4, not by the solver under test, and time windows and traffic are out of scope (Section 9). The instances are 30 independent replicates of 40 bins each, with coordinates from the city-centroid spatial model of Section 4.4 (σ≈0.045°) and NumPy PRNG seed 42. 5.3.2 Reference solver and metaheuristic tuning The reference is a proven optimum, not a heuristic bound. routing_exact_and_tuning.py solves each 41-node instance to optimality with a Dantzig–Fulkerson–Johnson mixed-integer formulation, branch-and-cut in CBC, and lazy subtour-elimination cuts added until the relaxation returns a single tour. All 30 evaluation instances and the 10 held-out tuning instances achieved proven optimality (Optimal status, mean solve time 0.36 s), with optimal tours averaging 103.5 km (95% CI 100.7–107.1 km), so every gap below is a true optimality gap. All scalability measurements were performed on a single core, normalized to Raspberry Pi 4B-class throughput (2,800 MIPS, 4 GB RAM). For each bin count n∈{100, 250, 500, 1000, 2, 500, 5, 000, 10, 000, 20, 000}, bins were sampled around a city centroid within a realistic municipal service radius (σ≈0.045°≈5 km). This preserves the spatial density of the 120-bin dataset (Section 4.4) at roughly 150 bins per 10 km × 10 km area, with three repetitions per size at different seeds. Measured wall-clock time scales as O(n1.72) (R2 = 0.995, regression p = 4.9 × 10−8), sub-quadratic in practice thanks to vectorized distance evaluation, against a theoretical O(n2) worst case for greedy nearest-neighbor. The fitted exponent is 1.72 (SE = 0.052), giving a 95% CI of [1.62, 1.83]. At n = 10, 000, route computation takes 1,335 ms and at n = 20, 000, 5,235 ms (Figure 6), with peak on-demand memory of 0.48 MB against 3,200 MB for a materialized matrix. Extrapolation beyondn = 20, 000should be treated cautiously, since the fit may not hold where memory-hierarchy effects dominate. Beyond single-node capacity, bins are sharded across district fog nodes, and the per-node cost scales as O((n/k)2) for k districts. Single-node performance therefore degrades gracefully to that scale, and sharding is the intended mitigation beyond it rather than an untested assumption. By mode, dropout and stuck-at chiefly cause missed alerts, since sensor silence reads as “no problem,” while drift and spike chiefly cause false alerts. The filter is accordingly most effective against dropout and stuck-at, and least effective against gradual drift, which stays within physical bounds while being factually wrong. 5.7 Deployment cost At pilot-volume retail pricing, a per-bin bill of materials (ESP32-WROOM-32, HC-SR04P ultrasonic, MQ-4 methane, DS18B20, 50 kg load cell with HX711, 18650 cell with BMS, solar panel, IP65 enclosure, NB-IoT/LoRa radio and assembly) gives a CAPEX of INR 3,160 (USD 37.62), with municipal tender pricing typically 30–40% lower. Adding a five-year amortized share of a district Raspberry Pi 4B gateway serving 250 bins, consistent with Section 5.5, plus annual connectivity and maintenance opex, gives a 5-year total cost of ownership of INR 6,493 per bin—itemized and currency-explicit rather than an unstated assumption of affordability. 5.8 Sustainability impact The figures in this section are model-based estimates, not measured real-world savings, and every input assumption is stated so the estimate can be recomputed or contested. They come from the simulated routing saving of Section 5.4 (38.86 km/day/route) rather than being assumed independently, using IPCC 2006 Guidelines (Vol. 2, Ch. 3) diesel emission factors and BS-VI heavy-duty limits. Spread across a modeled 12-route municipal fleet, the estimate gives ≈170,222 km/year saved, ≈59,578 liters of diesel avoided, ≈159.67 tonnes of CO2-equivalent avoided and ≈INR 5,481,136/year in fuel cost avoided. Carrying the [34.8%, 41.0%] confidence interval through gives roughly ±8% on each figure, before further unquantified uncertainty in the emission factor, fuel price, and fleet size. The 12-route figure is a stated scaling choice, not a measured fleet size. Actual savings depend on real road distances, traffic, vehicle mix, driver behavior, and load factors, none modeled here; these should be read as an order-of-magnitude upper estimate pending the GPS-tracked field study of Section 9.2. They also support UN SDG 11 and SDG 13 as the relevant policy framing. 5.9 Policy layer: simulated, not field-validated The prototype exercises the warning buffer, automated fining, paid-disposal mode, and Eco-Score gamification against synthetic citizen-behavior traces. The policy and gamification layer is a software prototype, not a field-validated behavioral intervention. We report it as an implementation-feasibility demonstration—that warning buffer, fine calculation, paid-disposal transition, and Eco-Score computation integrate architecturally with the sensing and routing pipeline—not as evidence of behavioral change or policy effectiveness. 5.9.1 Socio-technical considerations Generating automated fines from sensor readings raises questions of fairness, due process, privacy, legitimacy, and distributive incidence that a production deployment must resolve. As these are regulatory and policy constraints rather than implementation details, we treat them in Section 7: appeal and due process in Section 7.2, fairness, income-sensitivity and informal-sector displacement in Section 7.5, behavioral acceptance and the legitimacy of the Eco-Score in Section 7.6, and data protection in Section 7.7. Fog placement is therefore a structural requirement for time-critical safety alerts, not an optimization preference. 6.2 The routing quality–latency trade-off favors simple heuristics with local search Plain greedy nearest-neighbor, the default in much of the prior waste-routing literature, carries a 21.4% gap to the proven optimum that the original manuscript did not disclose, and a single 2-opt pass closes about 16 of those points for 0.67 ms (Section 5.3). Given that tuned ant colony optimization is statistically indistinguishable in quality but costs ~1,670 × more compute, and that tuned GA and PSO are worse on both axes, fog-tier routing need not sacrifice quality for speed—but it does need to move beyond vanilla nearest-neighbor. 6.3 The policy layer is architecturally integrated but empirically unvalidated The compliance and gamification pipeline shows that policy logic can coexist with operational routing in a single system, but every number describing citizen behavior is a property of the simulation's assumptions. Section 7 argues that the binding constraints on this component are, in any case, regulatory rather than technical, which makes it a hypothesis-generating prototype rather than a validated intervention. 6.4 Broader implications For municipal authorities, this work lays down a reproducible computational baseline against which hardware prototypes and field pilots can be evaluated, with the seeded scripts and JSON artifacts providing transparent benchmarks for latency, routing quality, scalability, and fault tolerance rather than each paper constructing an ad-hoc evaluation setup. For the research community, benchmarking routing against a proven optimum supplies a reference comparison that prior work, typically benchmarked against a single baseline, has lacked. 7 Regulatory and policy challenges Section 1.1 argued that the system is a policy instrument. Instruments that impose burdens on identifiable people are constrained in ways that optimizers are not, and those constraints are not deployment details to be resolved later: several of them determine whether the mechanism as designed is lawful and administrable at all. This section sets out the principal regulatory and policy challenges, and—where our own measurements bear on them—quantifies the constraint rather than merely naming it. Table 14 summarizes the analysis. We emphasize that this is a conceptual and literature-based analysis: no municipal legal review, administrative-law opinion, or consultation with an urban local body has been obtained (Section 9). 7.1 Legal authority and the delegation of penal decisions A fine is an exercise of statutory power, and the first question a deploying authority faces is whether the power exists and whether a machine may exercise it. The Solid Waste Management Rules 2016 (Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, Government of India, 2016) are specific on this point. Rule 15(zf) requires local authorities to “frame bye-laws and prescribe criteria for levying of spot fine for persons who litters or fails to comply with the provisions of these rules and delegate powers to officers or local bodies to levy spot fines as per the bye laws framed.” The penal power is therefore conferred by delegation on a named class of legal persons—officers, or local bodies—and the Rules envisage such an officer forming a view, not a threshold predicate firing. A sensor reading is evidence; the finding belongs to the officer. Automating the step from evidence to penalty, therefore, raises a familiar administrative-law question: whether a power delegated to an officer may be sub-delegated to an automated process, and whether doing so without express enabling provision is ultra vires. Coglianese and Lehr (2017) survey the conditions under which automated systems can carry administrative authority and conclude that the constraint is rarely technological; it is the presence of an accountable human decision-maker in the chain. The practical implication for this system is specific: the compliance engine should generate a proposed penalty with its supporting evidence package for ratification by an authorized officer, not an executed one. That is a change in the software's authority model, not merely in its user interface, and we did not implement it. 7.2 Technological due process and the wrongful-penalty rate Where an automated decision is adverse to an individual, Citron's account of technological due process (Citron, 2008) requires at minimum notice of the decision and its basis, a statement of reasons, an audit trail sufficient to reconstruct the decision, and a real opportunity to contest it before an independent reviewer. These are all missing from the prototype (Section 5.9). Our fault-injection findings quantify why this matters. At a 10% sensor-fault rate, the fog-tier mitigation filter still yields 3.17 false overflow alerts per 200 bins per evaluation cycle (Table 13), about 1.6 per 100 bins. If the same threshold-crossing logic were used to attribute a penalty, and the system were run daily across a 50,000-bin city, that corresponds to an order of 800 wrongful penalty events per day; at the 50% fault rate the figure is roughly 4,100. These are indicative rather than exact, because the fine predicate of Equation 2 spans four parameters while the fault study targets overflow alerts, and because a real deployment would presumably not run at a 50% fault rate. The point is structural: a non-zero sensor error rate is a non-zero wrongful-penalty rate, and it does not fall to zero as the filter improves. An appeals process is therefore not an optional citizen-experience feature but the mechanism by which the system's known error rate is reconciled with the requirement that penalties be correct. Treating it as an engineering quantity has a useful consequence: the tolerable wrongful-penalty rate becomes a parameter a regulator can set ex ante, and the sensor-fault rate at which the system may operate follows from it rather than from convenience. 7.3 The automation of administrative discretion Sanitary inspection has never been mechanical enforcement: an officer may warn rather than fine, recognize a household in crisis, or distinguish a first lapse from a pattern. Lipsky (2010) showed that such discretion is not a defect in implementation but the place where policy is actually made. Bovens and Zouridis (2002) describe what happens when information systems absorb it: the street-level bureaucracy becomes a system-level bureaucracy, discretion migrates from the officer at the point of contact to the designer who wrote the threshold, and the constitutional controls attaching to official decisions do not migrate with it. Peeters and Widlak (2018) detail the consequences for citizens who cannot get an incorrect record corrected. Our three-warning buffer is a deliberate, if crude, attempt to encode forbearance, but it does so uniformly, which is precisely what discretion is not. It cannot distinguish three lapses caused by indifference from three caused by hospitalization, and it is not obvious that it should try: a system that adjudicates hardship acquires a far larger surveillance footprint. The more defensible design reserves the automated tier for detection and evidence assembly and preserves a human waiver channel with recorded reasons, which is also what makes the resulting decisions reviewable. 7.4 Attribution, communal bins, and collective action Household-level liability presupposes household-level attribution. In much of the target context, bins are communal, and a communal bin has the two properties that define a common-pool resource in Ostrom's sense (Ostrom, 1990): its capacity is subtractable, in that waste deposited by one household is capacity denied to the next, and exclusion is difficult, in that a bin on a public street cannot practically be restricted to registered users. Its observed state is therefore the joint product of many users' behavior, and no telemetry stream can decompose that state into individual contributions. The system can measure that a bin is overflowing; it cannot measure who overflowed it. Attribution therefore fails in one of two directions: either the breach is unattributable, and no enforcement follows, or it is attributed by proxy—nearest registered household, last recorded disposal—and some penalties are simply wrong, regardless of sensor accuracy. This is a limit on instrument choice, not a gap to be engineered away. Where attribution fails, the appropriate instruments operate at the level at which the outcome is actually produced: ward-level service standards, collective incentives, and investment in provision, rather than individual fines. A deployment should therefore confine individual liability to bins with genuine single-user attribution and apply collective instruments elsewhere—a partition our current design does not make. 7.5 Distributive incidence and the informal recycling economy A flat fine is regressive: the INR amounts in Equation 2 represent a materially different burden across income groups, and Ghimire et al. (2024) show that willingness to pay for unit-based waste tariffs in a comparable South Asian setting is both low and unevenly distributed. Eubanks (2018) provides the general pattern for automated administrative systems, which tend to concentrate their errors and their burdens on those least able to contest them. There is a second, more specific distributive risk. A large share of the recycling actually performed in Indian cities is carried out by informal waste workers operating around and inside the municipal stream (Demaria and Schindler, 2016; Gidwani, 2015; Gutberlet, 2015; Doron and Jeffrey, 2018). A sensor-instrumented, penalty-bearing bin network can criminalize or displace that activity: by treating bin disturbance as tampering, by attributing bin-state anomalies to nearby households, or simply by formalizing a stream from which informal livelihoods are excluded. This is foreseeable and, in our view, the most serious social cost of the instrument, and it appears in no metric reported in Section 5. Instruments that integrate rather than displace informal workers—registration, cooperative contracting, and weight-based payment for delivered recyclate—exist in the literature (Gutberlet, 2015) and should be designed in from the start rather than retrofitted after displacement has occurred. 7.6 Procedural legitimacy and the limits of behavioral instruments Deterrence is not the primary driver of compliance. Tyler (2006) shows that perceived procedural fairness predicts compliance more reliably than expected sanctions, and May (2004) distinguishes affirmative motivations, which erode when enforcement is experienced as arbitrary, from deterrence-based ones. The implication runs against the intuition that a technically more accurate enforcement system is necessarily a more effective one: a system that issues correct penalties through a process citizens experience as opaque and uncontestable can reduce compliance overall. Bardach and Kagan's account of regulatory unreasonableness, Bardach and Kagan (1982) describes the same dynamic, and the pyramid of Ayres and Braithwaite (1992) is a response to it. The gamification layer carries a parallel risk. Yeung's “hypernudge,” Yeung (2017) describes a continuously updated, personalized behavioral score that shapes conduct without deliberation, which is a fair description of the Eco-Score. Vanolo (2014) argues that smart-city instruments of this kind shift responsibility for a structural service failure onto individual citizens, and Cardullo and Kitchin (2019) find that “smart citizen” participation is usually consumer-grade rather than political. A municipality that has not provided adequate collection capacity, and then scores households on their disposal behavior, is vulnerable to exactly this criticism. We take the implication to be that the incentive layer should be deployed only alongside a demonstrated improvement in service provision, and that its scoring rules should be public and contestable rather than tuned as an engagement mechanic. 7.7 Data protection, purpose limitation, and function creep Linking per-bin telemetry to a household identifier to attribute penalties constitutes processing of personal data under India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (Government of India, 2023): s. 2(t) defines personal data as any data about an identifiable individual, and the UID linkage is what makes the telemetry identifiable. Fill-level and weight series are moreover behaviorally revealing in ways a fill sensor's designers may not intend, communicating occupancy, absence, household size, and consumption patterns (Section 5.9). Edwards (2016) and Van Zoonen (2016) examine why smart-city telemetry resists conventional privacy governance: the data are collected for an uncontroversial operational purpose and acquire new purposes afterward. That trajectory is visible in our own design. Telemetry justified by collection efficiency is reused for enforcement, which is a change of purpose, and the Act does not permit it to inherit the original basis. Section 4 prohibits processing except for a lawful purpose on a recognized ground, and s. 5 requires the notice accompanying consent to state the purpose for which the data will be processed, so enforcement must be specified as a purpose rather than arrive later. Section 8(7), which requires erasure once the specified purpose is no longer served, supplies a statutory basis for a retention limit rather than leaving it to good practice. The concrete requirements this implies—separating the identity store from the telemetry store, pseudonymous attribution resolvable only on appeal or audit, discarding sub-daily readings once the compliance window closes, and a published lawful basis naming enforcement—are stated in Section 5.9 and implemented in none of the prototypes. 7.8 Institutional capacity, fragmentation, and procurement Even a lawful, fair, and privacy-respecting design fails if no organization can operate it. Kaza et al. (2018), and Guerrero et al. (2013) find institutional and financial arrangements, not technology, to be the binding constraint on waste outcomes in developing cities, and Meijer and Bolívar (2016) find that most smart-city initiatives change technology without changing governance arrangements. Three constraints are specific enough to state. First, responsibility is fragmented: sanitation departments, municipal IT functions, and contracted collection operators would each hold part of this pipeline, and the enforcement decision sits across the seam. Second, the five-year cost of ownership we report (Section 5.7) must survive annual municipal budget cycles and the incentive, familiar from the literature on digital-era governance Dunleavy et al. (2006), to procure a proprietary end-to-end platform whose data cannot be exported; an enforcement record that a citizen may need to contest should not be locked in a vendor's schema. Third, algorithmic accountability requires capacity that the deploying body may not have: Binns (2018), Veale and Brass (2019), and Janssen and Kuk (2016) all note that public bodies frequently cannot explain, audit, or contest the systems they operate. Publishing thresholds and fine schedules, maintaining an append-only decision log, and reporting wrongful-penalty and appeal-outcome statistics are the minimum, and all three are cheap to build if designed in and expensive to retrofit. 7.9 Implications for design: an evidentiary instrument first Taken together, these constraints suggest a different deployment order from the one the architecture implies. The sensing, fog-tier alerting, and dynamic dispatch components address governance problems 1, 4, and 5 of Section 1.1 and raise comparatively modest regulatory difficulty: they improve service and generate the measurement-based performance standards and equity audits require. The compliance and gamification components address problems 2 and 3, but carry nearly all of the legal, procedural, and distributive risk analyzed above. We therefore modify the claim the architecture makes. The defensible first deployment uses the compliance layer as an evidentiary and advisory instrument—building timestamped, contestable records that support human enforcement decisions and make service failures attributable—rather than as an autonomous penal one. Automated penalty execution should follow only once the statutory authority is explicit, an appeal mechanism with independent review is operating, attribution is restricted to genuinely single-user bins, income-sensitive calibration and informal-sector integration are in place, and a wrongful-penalty rate target has been set and is being measured against. This is a weaker claim than the original framing supported, and we take it to be the correct one. 8 Conclusion This article proposes SWMS, a three-tier edge/fog/cloud architecture integrating multi-parameter bin sensing, fog-tier dynamic routing, and cloud-based compliance governance into one pipeline. The contribution is vertical integration rather than algorithmic novelty: no sensing, routing, or fog-placement technique here is new in isolation, but coupling all three with a graduated enforcement engine and a citizen gamification layer has not been demonstrated before. Every quantitative result relies on a seeded, reproducible experiment. Fog-tier offloading cuts alert-to-actuation latency 7.5 × ; greedy+2-opt reaches a 5.1% gap to a proven optimum at sub-millisecond cost, matching the best tuned metaheuristic at three orders of magnitude less compute; telemetry-driven dispatch cuts daily distance 37.9% at matched overflow rates; scaling to 20,000 bins stays sub-quadratic; and fault injection quantifies the fog tier's ability to hold alert reliability under sensor degradation. The system functions better as a policy instrument than as an optimizer. It targets five governance problems—service delivery decoupled from need with no accountability signal, a statutory segregation duty with no evidentiary base, ungraduated enforcement instruments, a hazard class the administrative cycle cannot serve, and the invisibility of the distributive burden (Section 1.1)—and its enforcement design implements the responsive-regulation pyramid rather than an ad hoc fine schedule. Reading it as an instrument also makes its constraints legible. Section 7 finds that statutory authority, technological due process, the automation of administrative discretion, attribution in communal bins, distributive incidence on informal waste workers, procedural legitimacy, data protection, and municipal capacity each bear on whether the compliance layer is lawful and administrable, and that a non-zero sensor error rate entails a non-zero wrongful-penalty rate that no filter removes. We therefore conclude that the compliance layer should first be deployed as an evidentiary and advisory instrument supporting human enforcement decisions, not as an autonomous penal one. This is a weaker claim than the architecture alone would support, and the one the analysis warrants. That framing is intentional: a reproducible computational baseline with its gaps explicitly stated is more useful than claims that cannot be traced to executable experiments. The next steps are a hardware-in-the-loop deployment to validate the latency model against physical measurements, a structured household pilot—preferably under the regulatory-sandbox arrangement of Section 9.3—to replace simulated compliance figures with observed data, and integration of live traffic feeds. We release the experiment scripts and artifacts so future empirical studies can compare directly. 9 Limitations and future work The conclusions of Section 8 rest on computational evidence and are bounded accordingly. We set out those bounds in three groups—the evidence base, the policy layer, and the regulatory analysis—and then describe the work that would remove them. 9.1 Current limitations The first group concerns the evidence base. No physical prototype was built: the hardware parameters (Table 8) and cost figures (Section 5.7) rest on datasheets, retail pricing, and estimates, and the deviation we expect from sensor drift, load-cell vibration sensitivity, and long-term calibration drift has not been measured. The headline latency is analytical (Section 4.5); an executed iFogSim run and a queued SimPy model across all four load scenarios corroborate it to within ±25% (Section 5.2), but neither substitutes for instrumented hardware (Section 9.2). The distance saving holds on a real OpenStreetMap street network (Section 5.4), yet the objective remains shortest distance, not travel time under congestion: no live traffic feed reroutes a vehicle. Every study also assumes trusted sensors and links: telemetry spoofing and replay, sensor tampering, denial of service against a fog node, and record tampering are analyzed only qualitatively (Section 5.9), and no authentication, integrity, or intrusion-detection mechanism has been implemented or tested. The second group concerns the policy layer. Its compliance and gamification mechanisms are simulated, not field-tested (Section 5.9); fine amounts were not checked against willingness-to-pay evidence for the target context [cf.Ghimire et al. (2024)], and their impact on low-income households was not assessed. Nor are the constraints identified in Section 7 implemented or modeled. Encryption-at-rest and anonymization for the household UID-to-telemetry linkage, appeal and human-review procedures, income-sensitive fine calibration and integration instruments for informal waste workers are all absent, and who would bear automated fines, given communal bins and income variation, is not simulated. Table 14 states what each would require, but stating a requirement is not the same as meeting it. The third group concerns the regulatory analysis, which is conceptual rather than institutional. Section 7 draws on the literature alone: we have not obtained an administrative-law opinion on the delegation of penalty decisions, reviewed the bye-laws of any specific urban local body, or consulted municipal officials, sanitation workers, informal waste workers, or residents. The requirements in Table 14 are therefore analytically derived rather than validated, and each would need testing in a particular legal and institutional setting. The wrongful penalty estimate is likewise indicative. It maps overflow-alert false positives onto penalty events one-to-one, whereas the fine predicate spans four parameters and would filter further; it establishes that the rate is structurally non-zero and materially large but is not a calibrated forecast. 9.2 Hardware-in-the-Loop validation roadmap Because the largest limitation is the absence of physical measurement, we regard a hardware-in-the-loop study as the single most important next step. Even a minimal study would answer questions no computational experiment can. It would measure behavior under the 5–30% packet loss realistic for urban NB-IoT and LoRa links, including whether threshold evaluation and route recomputation degrade gracefully, and when a fog gateway goes offline, including how edge bins buffer and resume telemetry. It would record real, non-stationary drift profiles for load cells and MQ-series gas sensors over weeks to months of field exposure, and battery state-of-charge across seasons and the maintenance-visit frequency the per-bin hardware requires (Section 5.7). It would also let the stack be hardened with encryption, authentication, and intrusion detection for a municipal deployment, while GPS-tracked vehicles would test whether simulated savings translate into real fuel and time savings under actual traffic. 9.3 Future directions Several technical extensions follow from the results. The iFogSim run should be extended from the latency scenario to a full load sweep (balanced, stress, and energy) under a tuned tuple scheduler, and any divergence from the analytical model reconciled. The proven optimum of Section 5.3 quantifies the headroom a deep-reinforcement-learning routing policy could target—about 21 points over plain greedy and 5 over greedy+2-opt—although none was implemented here, and fog-tier routing should use live congestion data to optimize travel time rather than distance. Encryption-at-rest for the UID-to-telemetry linkage and an anonymization layer decoupling fine attribution from raw sensor history would meet the data-protection requirements of Section 7, and a permissioned blockchain could keep tamper-evident Eco-Score and fine records once the policy mechanism has field validation to record. The policy layer, however, needs field evidence and institutional grounding more than further engineering. A structured household pilot modeled on Rosenlund et al. (2025) and Venturi et al. (2025) would replace the simulated Eco-Score and compliance figures with observed data, including fairness assessment across income groups and appeal-procedure design. Co-design with a single urban local body would test the requirements of Table 14 against its actual bye-laws, enforcement practice, and organizational capacity, ideally under a regulatory-sandbox arrangement in which the compliance layer runs in advisory mode and its proposed penalties are compared against the decisions human officers actually make; that comparison is the cheapest available test of whether the automated predicate tracks defensible enforcement. Finally, residents and, in particular, informal waste workers—whose livelihoods the instrument can displace (Section 7) and who are absent from every evaluation reported here—should shape its design before deployment rather than after.
