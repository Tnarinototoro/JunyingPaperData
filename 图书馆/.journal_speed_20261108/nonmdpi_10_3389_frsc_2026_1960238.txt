Department of Economics (DSE), University of Bologna, Bologna, Italy Article metrics View details Abstract Reducing carbon emissions from holiday air travel—an important contributor to overall emissions in Western countries—is crucial for the transition to a post-carbon society. This article presents short-term findings from a randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted in Switzerland, examining the effect of a digital nudging intervention aimed at reducing demand for holiday air travel. In 2025, 204 self-selected volunteers were randomized to control and treatment groups; 146 completed both baseline and first follow up surveys over a three-month intervention period. Rooted in the Model of Action Phases, the treatment consisted in a multi-component intervention combining app-based GenAI (GPT-3.5) and peer-generated content shared through the app and Instagram, leveraging social-norm and feedback mechanisms, in a gamified framework. Using individual fixed-effects panel models, we assess the short-term effects of the multi-component treatment on key psychological determinants and behavioral intention to reduce holiday air travel. The intention-to-treat analysis provides no evidence of average treatment effects on either psychological outcomes or flying intentions. However, higher levels of engagement with the intervention are associated with lower intentions to fly for holiday purposes, and with a smaller reduction in perceived descriptive norms around holiday flying. While these findings should be interpreted cautiously given differential attrition (39% in the treatment group vs. 18% in the control group) and the study's limited power to detect small-to-moderate effects, they suggest that sustained engagement with interventions combining GenAI and peer-to-peer content sharing via social media may have the potential to influence the psychological antecedents and stated intentions related to holiday air travel. 1 Introduction In the hyper-accelerated society of the Global North, where flying is the norm for long-distance leisure travel, tourism accounts for around 5% of global carbon emissions, 43% of which are attributable to air transport (Higham et al., 2016; Peeters and Dubois, 2010). The spread of low-cost airlines has made air travel more accessible to a broad segment of the population (Graham and Shaw, 2008), contributing to its emergence as the standard mode of holiday travel for many people, due to its affordability, speed, and prominent place in contemporary holiday imaginaries (Gössling and Dolnicar, 2023; Schafer and Victor, 2000). Indeed, driven by a desire for worry-free leisure time, individuals often ignore the carbon impact of their holidays, thereby contributing significantly to global emissions (Barr et al., 2010; Gössling et al., 2012; Ventimiglia et al., 2024). Traditional policy instruments and technological solutions aimed at reducing aviation emissions—such as carbon pricing, fuel taxes and hydrogen-powered planes—face political and economic constraints (Raihan, 2025). Research suggests that technology and policy solutions will not be able to satisfy the growing demand for flying in the next three decades, making it uncertain when the decarbonization of long distance travel will actually be achieved (Song et al., 2025). Gössling and Dolnicar (2023) argue that policy and technology solutions must be combined with behavioral interventions in order to achieve decoupling from aviation emissions more quickly. Policy makers in fact increasingly point to the need for complementary demand-side approaches that can operate alongside price-based and regulatory measures (Klenert et al., 2018). Without substituting for standard instruments, behavioral interventions may act on margins that conventional policy does not directly target: the salience of emissions at the moment of trip planning, the perceived social normality of flying, and the visibility and attractiveness of lower-carbon substitutes (Allcott and Rogers, 2014). These mechanisms may be especially relevant among frequent flyers—a group that contributes disproportionately to aviation emissions and therefore represents a potentially high-leverage policy target (Gössling and Humpe, 2020). This paper contributes to the literature on holiday behavior and climate change mitigation by presenting short-term evidence from a randomized controlled trial (RCT) on the effects on air travel intention and psychological antecedents of air travel of treatment with a multi-component digital nudging tool promoting “sufficient holidays” and a reduction in holiday air travel demand. Coherently with the literature on “sufficiency” (Princen, 2005; Jungell-Michelsson and Heikkurinen, 2022), we define “sufficient” holidays as lower-impact holidays in terms of carbon emissions and resource use. This may be achieved either through modal shift, whereby leisure air travel is replaced by ground-based travel, or through absolute reduction, whereby flying short breaks are substituted with close-to-home leisure activities. The intervention consists in a multi-component digital tool, called Treeps, combining GenAI with social media and peer-based content to promote sufficient holidays. The RCT aimed at testing its effect was run in real-world conditions in Lugano (Switzerland) in 2025. The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 briefly reviews the literature on tourism, air travel, climate change and digital nudging. Section 3 presents the theoretical framework underpinning the study, with particular attention to the socio-psychological mechanisms relevant to behavior change, and details our research questions. Section 4 describes the Treeps intervention and its main components, the research methodology, including the experimental design, the outcome variables, and the modeling strategy. Section 7 concludes. 2 Literature review Gössling and Higham (2021) argue that tourism must cut emissions as an essential, unavoidable goal. The sector's dependence on high-carbon travel—especially flying—is incompatible with climate targets. In practice, this means reducing air travel emissions by choosing alternative transport modes, traveling closer to home, and staying longer in fewer destinations (Gössling and Higham, 2021)—i.e. by performing sufficient holidays. In this context, scholars called for rethinking the very idea of holidays and leisure time (Peeters et al., 2019; Scott et al., 2012; Higham et al., 2014). Social movements have also emerged, such as the Swedish flygskam (flight shaming) or tågskryt (boasting train use), that respectively stigmatize the use of planes or encourage the use of trains (Becken et al., 2021). A substantial body of literature shows that digital nudging tools, defined as the deliberate design of digital environments to steer individuals' behavior in predictable ways without restricting choice or significantly altering economic incentives (Weinmann et al., 2016), have been used to promote behavioral change in sustainability-related domains, including sustainable mobility (Cellina et al., 2020; Vacondio et al., 2025; Ferron et al., 2019), water conservation (Novak et al., 2018; Fraternali et al., 2019), and energy saving (Fraternali et al., 2017; Cellina et al., 2024). Such tools combine behavioral features with gamification elements, such as progress tracking or reward offering, to increase user satisfaction and engagement (Morganti et al., 2017; Lu et al., 2025). The tourism context represents a particularly suitable domain for digital nudging interventions, as digital tools are already widely used in all phases of the travel experience, from planning and booking to on-site activities and post-travel experience sharing (Wang et al., 2014; Lai, 2015; Dorcic et al., 2019; Dickinson et al., 2014). The high level of digitalization in tourism suggests a strong potential for interventions that leverage digital interfaces to influence tourism-related decision-making, and particularly the travel-related component. Furthermore, the recent huge developments in digital technologies may provide novel opportunities to support behavioral change interventions delivered through digital nudging tools. In particular, the literature suggests that the growing diffusion of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) may enhance user interaction, personalization, and community-based engagement (Shanahan et al., 2023; Banh and Strobel, 2023; Bentley, 2025), thereby facilitating pro-environmental behavior change. Although previous studies suggest that digital nudging and emerging GenAI technologies may encourage more sustainable travel choices, evidence remains limited regarding the behavioral mechanisms through which such interventions influence holiday travel decisions. Understanding these mechanisms is particularly important given the highly planned and socially embedded nature of holiday air travel. 3 Theoretical framework and research questions Several frameworks have been used to explain pro-environmental and sustainable travel behavior. The theory of planned behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991) identifies attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioral control as key determinants of behavioral intention, while the Norm Activation Model (NAM) emphasizes the activation of personal norms and moral obligation (Schwartz, 1977). These frameworks have been widely applied to travel behavior and provide important insights into the psychological determinants of behavioral intentions. However, they are less explicit about how individuals progress over time from established habits and routines toward goal formation, behavioral implementation, and maintenance of new behaviors. Building on those frameworks, the Model of Action Phases (MAP), originally proposed by Heckhausen and Gollwitzer (1987) and subsequently taken up by Bamberg (2013a,b), conceptualizes behavior change as a dynamic process unfolding across phases. Rather than replacing constructs from the TPB and NAM, the MAP incorporates them within a broader stage-based perspective that links motivational and action-oriented processes over time. Accordingly, we adopt the MAP as the overarching theoretical framework for this study, as it not only incorporates key determinants of behavior identified by TPB and NAM, but also conceptualizes how these determinants contribute to progression across different phases of behavior change. The MAP conceptualizes behavior change as a process through four different phases (Bamberg, 2013b), as shown in Figure 1 for the behavior change process toward sufficient holidays. At pre-decision, individuals are used to frequent flying; acting on personal and social norms helps them to start setting a goal for change. Personal norms represent the beliefs that flying for holiday purposes is right or wrong (Bamberg and Möser, 2007). Social norms instead refer to individuals' tendency to adapt their behavior to the behavior of other people around them, especially those whose opinion they value (Cialdini and Goldstein, 2004). Social norms encompass the descriptive representation of the behavior of others and the injunctive indication of what others perceive to be proper behavior (Bicchieri, 2016; Cialdini et al., 1990). Figure 1 The MAP model acting on the process toward sufficient holidays. Progress between one behavior phase and the next one is favored by activating the psychological factors indicated by the arrows. If individuals begin to perceive their flying holidays as problematic and recognize that others have adopted, or approve of, sufficient holidays, they enter the pre-action phase and start forming an intention to change. In this phase, positive attitudes toward sufficient holidays and higher perceived behavioral control support goal setting. Together with personal and social norms, these factors facilitate progression to the action phase, where individuals begin to implement sufficient holidays. In the post-action phase, rewards and social support help sustain these behaviors, address implementation challenges, and prevent relapse to habitual flying behavior. Recent evidence suggests that acting on the MAP psychological constructs can effectively reshape preferences regarding transport mode choices for both local (Giubergia et al., 2024) and long-distance travel (Gerosa and Cellina, 2025). However, experimental evidence on whether and how digital interventions activate the MAP mechanisms in the context of holiday air travel remains limited. This study thus investigates whether and how, immediately after its use, the Treeps multi-component digital tool activated the MAP psychological mechanisms toward sufficient holidays. Since holiday air travel is a highly planned activity, dependent on prior bookings, behavioral changes cannot be expected immediately after the trial period. Nevertheless, an effect on future holiday intentions may occur. We thus address the following research questions (RQs) about the effect on antecedents of behavioral change: what are the effects of use of the Treeps tool on the psychological factors highlighted by the MAP (RQ1) and on the intention to fly for holiday purposes (RQ2)? Additionally, the study conducts an exploratory analysis on the relationship between the engagement with the Treeps tool features and the same research outcomes regarding MAP psychological factors and intention to fly for holidays: does any of these outcomes change with the level of engagement with the Treeps features (RQ3)? Unlike treatment assignment, the level of engagement with Treeps was not randomized or predetermined. Participants were free to use the intervention as much as they wished, and their engagement may therefore reflect pre-existing characteristics, such as their interests, attitudes, motivation, or familiarity with digital tools. Consequently, participants with higher engagement may systematically differ from those with lower engagement. The analyses addressing RQ3 should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and associational rather than causal. These research questions guide the empirical strategy of the paper, which combines a randomized controlled trial design with fixed effect panel data analysis to estimate short-term changes in both psychological factors and behavioral intentions associated with the use of Treeps. 4 Materials and methods In this section, we first introduce the Treeps digital tool, and then the methodology we use to address our research questions and estimate its effects. 4.1 The Treeps digital nudging tool Treeps consists of two main elements aimed at triggering progress between the MAP phases by acting on the psychological determinants of behavior, whose features were identified in a co-design process held in 2024 in Lugano, Switzerland: a smartphone application (app) including a chatbot based on GPT-3.5 and an Instagram page, whose key characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Through the chatbot, the app provides users with suggestions for sufficient holiday destinations, targeting attitudes toward sufficient holidays. These suggestions are accompanied by feedback on carbon emissions saved compared with flying holidays, which target personal norms, and by practical information on how to reach the destinations, which targets perceived behavioral control. In addition, the app collects information on users' sufficient holiday experiences, defined as trips using low-carbon modes of transport such as trains, overnight trains, buses, bicycles, or walking, and encourages users to share them with the community through both the app and the connected Treeps.ch Instagram account. This peer-based experience-sharing component is intended to reinforce social norms and provide social support. The app is organized into three main sections (Figure 2): the “Home page,” the list of suggested destinations (“Places”) and the experiences shared by other users (“Community”). The Home page includes a chatbot, called TreepsBot, that was trained to stimulate the users by both providing them with suggestions for sufficient holiday destinations and inviting them to share their experiences within the user community. The destination suggestions are taken from a curated data-base of 200 holiday destinations identified by the research team, which are then proposed to the user by the chatbot, using an informal and friendly language. The instruction prompt used to train the chatbot to perform these activities is reported in the Supplementary Materials (Section S1). A gamified system also rewards users with points based on the number of sufficient holiday experiences they share with Treeps, and the like and comments received on Instagram by the related posts. The top three users who received points the most were rewarded with a voucher prize of 50 Swiss Francs (about 50 Euros). The Places section reports the list of the destinations recommended by the chatbot. The 200 destinations developed by the researchers were gradually unlocked as the users interacted with the chatbot: once the chatbot recommended a new destination, it appeared in the Places section. Each destination is featured with an attractive picture, a description encouraging users to visit the destination, and logistic information on travel times by public transport—with a direct link to book a train ticket from the Swiss Federal Railways to reach it—as well as the carbon emissions saved by traveling on the ground rather than air. The Community section showcases all the sufficient holiday experiences shared by Treeps users. Each experience reports a picture, the estimate of saved CO2 by reaching the destination by traveling on the ground rather than by plane, and a message aimed at reinforcing injunctive social norms in favor of sufficient holidays, selected from a curated list developed by the research team. Only experiences involving sustainable means of transport, such astrains, overnight trains, buses, bicycles, or walking, can be shared. Experiences submitted through the app are also posted on the associated “Treeps.ch” Instagram account. During onboarding, users are invited to link their Instagram account to Treeps, so that so that shared experiences can be automatically reposted and the contributing user tagged. In addition, an Instagram group chat allows interested users to interact with one another, exchange comments, and discuss how sufficient holidays can be implemented in practice. Finally, the app includes an automatic push notification system. On Thursdays, users are invited to explore a sufficient holiday destination for the upcoming weekend, while on Tuesdays they receive a prompt encouraging them to share a sufficient holiday experience with the Treeps community. 4.2 The Treeps RCT Our research questions aimed to determine whether use of Treeps affects the psychological and intention variables that, according to the MAP model, are antecedents of behavior change toward sufficient holidays. To address them, we designed and implemented a randomized controlled trial (RCT) in Lugano, Switzerland. The intervention consisted of providing access to the Treeps multi-component digital tool for a period of three months, from May to August 2025. The RCT followed a panel design with five questionnaires conducted once every three months, starting from immediately before the trial, allowing the estimation of within-individual treatment effects over a one-year period using fixed-effects panel models. Since the GenAI chatbot, peer-generated social media content, social-norm, carbon feedback, and gamification features were delivered jointly, the findings concern the effects of Treeps as a whole, as an integrated, multi-component digital nudging intervention. The RCT was pre-registered on the AsPredicted platform (registration number #220688). The research design was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of applied sciences where the research team is affiliated, and collected data were pseudo-nymized prior to use and storage on a cloud platform in Switzerland. Although the full trial design includes five questionnaire waves administered at three-month intervals, the present paper focuses exclusively on short-term effects and analyzes data between the baseline survey (T0) to the first follow-up survey (T1), respectively conducted immediately before and immediately after the three-month intervention period. Note that, as flying is an activity planned in advance, it is unlikely that any behavioral changes will be observed in the short term; for this reason, behavioral outcomes are included in the RCT, but they are not analyzed in the short-term presented here. Coherently with the RCT pre-registration, the average treatment effects (ATEs) of Treeps addressing RQ1 and RQ2 were evaluated using a fixed-effects panel model within an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis framework. This framework implies that all participants in the treatment group are considered treated, regardless of whether they actually used Treeps or not. The ITT framework provides an objective assessment of the digital nudging tool in a real-world setting, acknowledging that not everyone is equally inclined to use digital tools. The analyses on the heterogeneity of the effects depending on the engagement addressing RQ3, are performed on the same outcomes as those used for RQ1 and RQ2, again coherently with the RCT pre-registration. Regarding the MAP behavior antecedents (RQ1), we hypothesized that, after using Treeps and compared to the control group, individuals in the treatment group exhibit higher descriptive and injunctive social norms, higher personal norms, and higher perceived behavioral control, more positive attitudes toward sufficient holidays, as well as less positive attitudes toward air travel for holiday purposes. Regarding behavior intention (RQ2), we hypothesized that, after using Treeps and compared to the control group, individuals intended to fly less for holiday purposes. Finally, for RQ3 we hypothesized that, as the number of interactions with Treeps features increased, so did the effect of Treeps on the outcome variables tested for RQ1 and RQ2. Prior to starting the trial, a power analysis was conducted regarding the needed sample size to detect an effect on the number of air travels per person and year (see the Supplementary Materials, Section 2). The analysis showed that a sample size of n = 274 participants is required to detect an effect of halving the number of air travels per person and year, whilst a sample size of n = 140 is required to detect a reduction to 0.2 air travels per person and year, starting from the current average value of 0.68 leisure air travels per year (OFS, 2017). 4.3 Sample recruitment and characteristics Starting in March 2025, participants were recruited through the newsletter of the Lugano Living Lab, the innovation lab of the municipality of Lugano, which has over 10,000 subscribers. The recruitment message explicitly targeted lovers of traveling and discovering new holiday destinations. The eligibility criterion set for participation was to live, work, or study in the region of Lugano. Participation in the trial was incentivized through a lottery system, which offered 10 prizes of 50 Swiss Francs each, for each of the five questionnaires, for a total of 2,500 Swiss Francs. People interested to join the trial were invited to sign up to Treeps via an initial form, which only asked for their municipality of residence and email address. A total of 364 people applied, but only 294 were deemed eligible. The eligible participants where then invited to answer the baseline questionnaire at T0, immediately before the start of the trial. Questionnaire data were collected through online surveys administered via the Qualtrics platform, using personalized pseudo-nymized links, which allowed respondents to be tracked across questionnaire waves. The baseline questionnaire collected information on socio-demographic characteristics, holiday behavior, and psychological MAP factors and intention to travel by air in the following months. In total, 204 participants completed the baseline questionnaire at T0 (April 2025) and were then assigned, using simple randomization, to either the treatment group or the control group. The treatment group (n = 102) received an invitation to access and use the Treeps digital nudging tool for a three-month period, from May to August 2025. The control group (n = 102), instead, did not receive any treatment. The follow-up survey (T1) was administered immediately after the conclusion of the three-month intervention period, in August 2025. A total of 146 participants completed the T1 survey (84 from the control group and 62 from the treatment group) and constitute our analytical sample (Figure 3). RQ1 and RQ2 outcome variables and survey items. 4.5 Statistical models The ITT average treatment effect (ATE) of Treeps on participants' psychological variables (RQ1) and air travel intention (RQ2) was estimated via a fixed-effects panel model, using the “plm” package in R (Croissant et al., 2015). In our case with only two observations over time, a fixed-effects panel model is algebraically equivalent to a Difference-in-Difference (DiD) regression. All fixed-effects outcome models were estimated using the analytical sample of 146 participants who answered both T0 and T1 surveys. As each participant contributed one observation at each survey wave, the resulting balanced panel comprised 292 person-wave observations. This model allowed us to compare the T0 and T1 values of the outcome variables specified in Table 3 for the treatment and control group, while controlling for unobserved individual heterogeneity due to time-invariant individual characteristics that may affect the outcome values. Such a decrease could hint at presence of systematic attrition—namely, a dropout that is non-random and related to participant or treatment characteristics, and the analytic sample that systematically differs from the original one on which randomization was performed. If so, this would lead to biased model estimates. Furthermore, at T1 our analytic sample also showed different dropout rates between the treatment group and the control group (respectively, 39% and 18%), which could hint at presence of differential attrition, even more critically biasing our ATE estimates. The possible presence of systematic or differential attrition requires specific methodological care to ensure internal validity and overall reliability of model outputs. To check presence of attrition in our analytic sample we created an attrition indicator at the individual level, via a binary indicator Ai taking value 1 if an individual did not answer the T1 questionnaire (dropout) and 0 otherwise (non-dropout). Nevertheless, as shown by chi-square test and t-test whose results are reported in the last column of Table 2, show that the treatment and control groups constituting our analytic sample are non statistically different in key baseline observed socio-demographic characteristics, MAP phase and holiday behavior. Unobserved factors (e.g. intrinsic interest, motivation, or availability of time to comply with the study activities) may have caused participants' dropout of the trial. Thus, we cannot completely exclude our results are biased due to differential attrition and thus affected by limited internal validity. To increase reliability of results from the regression models use to estimate the Treeps effect, and at least address possible attrition biases on observed covariates, in our models we explicitly account for attrition between T0 and T1, by applying inverse probability weighting (IPW) to the regression models. The IPW procedure assigns each participant remained in the sample at T1 a weight based on the inverse of the estimated probability of remaining in the study from T0 to T1, conditional on treatment assignment and baseline characteristics. The baseline characteristics on which the probability of remaining in the sample is calculated are age, gender, educational level, employment status, number of flights at T0, MAP phase and treatment assignment. To reduce the influence of extreme observations, stabilized weights were trimmed at the 5th and 95th percentiles. The ITT results indicate no effect across the entire sample; conversely, heterogeneity by engagement suggests an association between engagement and the intention to fly less, as well as a counterintuitive association with descriptive social norms on the diffusion of holiday air travel. 6.1 No statistically detectable average effect The absence of significant effects in the ITT analysis is compatible with at least three, non-mutually exclusive, explanations: (i) Treeps has genuinely no effects on behavior change antecedents even under full exposure to the Treeps treatment condition; (ii) an effect exists but the analytic sample lacks the statistical power to detect it; (iii) an effect exists but is attenuated or obscured by residual bias from differential attrition on unobservable sample characteristics, that cannot be addressed via IPW. Regarding the first explanation, the data provide limited evidence against a fully null effect interpretation: the heterogeneity analysis shows that, among treated participants, higher engagement with Treeps is associated with a lower intention to fly. Consequently, the engagement results do not provide evidence against the null hypothesis on the ITT effect, but they suggest that the possibility of a treatment effect cannot be dismissed. Regarding the second, non-exclusive explanation, the ITT analysis may be underpowered to detect effects of the size typically observed in digital nudging and social norm interventions. In fact, although at RCT pre-registration a priori power calculations were conducted for the primary outcome of the broader Treeps trial (reduction in the number of flights per year, requiring N = 140–280 participants depending on the targeted effect size), the trial was not specifically powered to detect effects on the short-term psychological and intention outcomes examined here, which are secondary outcomes of the broader Treeps trial. An illustrative post-hoc power calculation based on the observed effect for descriptive social norms (β = 4.58; SD = 15.8) indicates that approximately N = 194 participants (97 per group) would have been required to detect such an effect with 80% power and α=0.05. Given that the final analytical sample is substantially smaller, the current study is likely underpowered for detecting small-to-moderate changes in psychological outcomes. The null ITT results should therefore be interpreted as an absence of evidence, rather than evidence of absence: the study may simply lack the precision to detect small-to-moderate psychological shifts. Finally, the third, non-exclusive explanation concerns the mechanisms generating differential attrition between the treatment and control groups (39% vs. 18%). As discussed in Section 4.6, the treatment assigned significantly predicted dropout, indicating the presence of differential attrition. At the same time, balance tests among respondents who completed the T1 survey showed no significant differences between treatment and control groups on the observed baseline characteristics included in the analyses. This suggests that differential attrition did not generate observable imbalances in the analytical sample, although bias arising from unobserved characteristics cannot be excluded. Several factors may have contributed to the observed attrition, including the effort required to engage with a multi-component digital intervention and technical limitations of some Treeps features. The effort required by using the app may have prompted users of the treatment group to withdraw from the trial before completing the T1 survey. Some users may have been put off by using the app because they were not confident enough with GenAI and social media. Others may not have downloaded the app and, feeling they had not fully participated in the experiment, they may have stopped responding to the questionnaires. The chatbot's malfunctions may have further led to systematic drop-out, as well as lower engagement. More importantly, participants who dropped out may have differed from those who remained in the study in ways that are not observable in the available data. For instance, participants with less time or motivation for pro-environmental behavior change may have been both more likely to drop out and less likely to show psychological shifts. While the IPW procedure corrects for attrition predictable from observed covariates, and the balance tests show no significant imbalance on those observed covariates at T1, neither procedure can rule out bias from unobserved factors correlated with dropout and with the outcome variables. Taken together, the available evidence suggests that the null ITT findings should be interpreted cautiously. The absence of detectable ITT average treatment effects may reflect a genuine lack of effect, insufficient statistical power, residual bias associated with differential attrition, or some combination of these factors. Consequently, the present results should be interpreted as indicating that no average treatment effect was detected under the present study conditions, rather than as evidence that Treeps had no effect on the MAP antecedents or on flying intentions. 6.2 Instagram use, engagement with Treeps, and descriptive social norms The exploratory post-hoc analysis suggests that Instagram account ownership may help explain the unexpected relationship observed between engagement and descriptive social norms. While higher engagement with Treeps was associated with lower intentions to fly, it was also associated with a smaller reduction in the perceived prevalence of holiday air travel. The additional analyses indicate that this pattern was particularly evident among participants who did not have an Instagram account but nevertheless engaged extensively with Treeps. One possible interpretation is that, in a community like Treeps where only a small subset of users regularly shared posts of their experiences, highly active contributors—who, per the results above, are disproportionately non-Instagram users—may come to see themselves as exceptions rather than as part of a broader trend. If only a limited number of users regularly shared sufficient-holiday experiences, highly engaged participants may have become more aware of the rarity of such behavior, leading them to perceive holiday flying as more widespread among the general population than previously assumed. By contrast, participants with Instagram accounts engaged with Treeps less actively, but they may have been more frequently exposed to peer-generated holiday-related content through Instagram itself—including posts by the Treeps.ch account—which could have reinforced a more favorable perception of how common sufficient holidays are among their contacts. Such exposure may have reinforced the visibility of sufficient-holiday practices and contributed to a more favorable perception of their prevalence among others. In support of this interpretation, the T0 survey indicated that 42 out of 51 participants with an Instagram account use Instagram to view content at least weekly. These explanations remain speculative. The analyses were conducted post hoc, were not part of the pre-registered design, and rely on Instagram account ownership as an imperfect proxy for actual exposure to Instagram content. Moreover, we lack data on how often Treeps' posts of sufficient holiday experiences—which were posted almost daily on Treeps.ch on Instagram as shown in Supplementary Figure S2—appeared in these participants' Instagram feeds. The findings should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating, and future research should investigate more directly how different forms of social-media exposure shape perceptions of social norms related to low-carbon travel. Additionally, these results suggest that Treeps was used considerably more by non-Instagram users, even though they represent only a small share of the treatment group. This pattern of use may have practical implications for the design of future digital nudging tools like Treeps that incorporate social media. In particular, as discussed in Section 4.6, it suggests that engagement with digitally mediated sustainability interventions may not necessarily coincide with engagement in social-media platforms themselves. 6.3 Limitations Despite the care we devoted to designing and implementing the Treeps RCT, our work is affected by a few limitations. First, the short-term focus of this paper restricts the analysis to psychological antecedents and stated intentions rather than actual flight behavior. Because holiday air travel is typically planned and booked well in advance, the T0–T1 observation window is too short to capture realized behavior change. Consequently, the study can only provide evidence on changes occurring in the early pre-decision and pre-action phases of the MAP model, together with changes in future travel intentions. While these psychological antecedents and intentions constitute important elements in the behavior change process conceptualized by the MAP, the present study cannot determine how the observed changes ultimately translate into actual travel behavior. Assessing progression through the action and post-action phases of the MAP model requires longer-term observation, which will only become possible through the subsequent follow-up waves of the Treeps trial. This limitation is particularly relevant given the well-documented possibility that favorable attitudes and intentions do not always result in corresponding behavioral changes, a phenomenon commonly referred to as the attitude-behavior gap, for instance extensively documented by Juvan and Dolnicar (2014). Second, the analytic sample was affected by differential attrition between treatment and control groups, and while IPW and covariate balance checks mitigate bias from observed characteristics, residual bias from unobserved characteristics being correlated with both dropout and the outcomes cannot be ruled out. Relatedly, the study was not powered a priori for the short-term psychological and intention outcomes examined here (only for the pre-registered longer-term flight-frequency outcome), so null ITT results should mostly be interpreted as absence of evidence. Furthermore, in addition to differential attrition, the sample consisted of self-selected volunteers and was predominantly highly educated, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Third, the Engagement Index used to address RQ3 is strongly right-skewed, with a small number of highly active users driving most of the observed variation. This limits generalization possibilities for the engagement findings beyond the analytic sample. Moreover, engagement with Treeps was not randomized and may reflect pre-existing participant characteristics, including motivation, digital familiarity, social-media use, interest in sustainable travel, or prior travel intentions. Participants with different levels of engagement may therefore differ systematically from one another, making it impossible to disentangle the influence of Treeps use from underlying differences between participants. As a result, the observed association between higher engagement and lower flying intentions should be interpreted as suggestive rather than conclusive evidence, and should not be given a causal interpretation. Fourth, the exploratory post-hoc analyses on Instagram ownership as a moderator were conducted on a small, non-randomized subsample and were not part of the pre-registered analysis plan; they should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. 6.4 Recommendations for future research The insights collected by the short-term analyses on use of the Treeps trial suggest two key recommendations for future trials aimed at strictly estimating the effects of digital nudging tools to reduce air travel. The first recommendation concerns the negative association between descriptive social norms and engagement. Although it was observed in a relatively small subgroup of participants, that includes the 18% of participants who do not have an Instagram account, future interventions should avoid it takes place. If, as it is likely, engagement is driven by a small, highly engaged subgroup within the sample, novel ways to engage the less active users should be explored. Future interventions might strengthen descriptive social norm mechanisms by ensuring that participants are exposed to a large enough number of visible examples of sufficient holidays. One way to achieve this could be to supplement user-generated posts with clearly labeled researcher-provided examples whenever community-generated content is scarce. This would prevent the genuinely active users from thinking they are the only ones opting for sufficient holidays. The second key recommendation concerns the app. We recommend devoting as much attention (and budget) as possible to the chatbot's development. In the Treeps trial, the malfunctioning of the chatbot's destination suggestion feature may have played a key role in causing a drop in engagement before Treeps could produce its effects: if the chatbot kept repeating the same destination suggestions, users were not exposed to the richness of the destination data-base, which included 200 destination suggestions. Given current technological advancements, and considering that Treeps exploited GPT-3.5 while at the time of writing GPT 5.5 is available, improvements in the chatbot performances should be relatively easy to obtain. Furthermore, we recommend unlocking all destinations straight away and making them accessible within the app in the most intuitive way possible. Finally, future research may adopt a broader scope, and also assess any potential side effects on business travel, as the behavioral change regarding holiday travel may make users of apps like Treeps more familiar with traveling by train in general, not only for holidays. 7 Conclusions This paper contributes to the literature on behavior interventions aimed at reducing carbon emissions of holiday travel. We examined the short-term effects on the antecedents of behavior change of Treeps, a multi-component digital nudging tool that combines GenAI, peer-to-peer interaction, and feedback mechanisms to promote sufficiency in holiday travel. Using a randomized controlled trial (RCT), we evaluated the effect of Treeps on psychological antecedents of behavior change and intentions to travel by air for holiday purposes. The analytical sample was affected by differential attrition between treatment and control groups, and was likely underpowered for detecting small-to-moderate effects on the short-term psychological and intention outcomes examined in this paper. Although we applied inverse probability weighting (IPW) procedures to mitigate bias associated with observed predictors of attrition, residual bias arising from unobserved characteristics cannot be excluded. Accordingly, the absence of a detectable average treatment effect in the ITT analysis should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of ineffectiveness, as it may also result from limited statistical power, differential attrition, or both. At the same time, the exploratory engagement analyses indicate that higher engagement with Treeps was associated with lower stated intentions to fly for holiday purposes, suggesting that the relationship between exposure to the intervention and behavioral intentions may warrant further investigation in future studies with larger samples and stronger levels of sustained participation. The evidence on descriptive social norms points to more complex and heterogeneous patterns. In particular, differences emerge between participants with and without Instagram accounts, suggesting that exposure to peer-generated content may be associated with different perceptions of how widespread sufficient holidays are. However, given the exploratory and post-hoc nature of this analysis and the limited subsample size, these findings should be interpreted with caution and cannot be taken as evidence of a causal mechanism. Overall, given these limitations, together with the short observation window and the skewed distribution of engagement observed in the trial, the findings should be treated as preliminary. Furthermore, as we only analyzed antecedents of behavior and travel intentions, they should not be interpreted as evidence of a reduction in holiday air travel. Nonetheless, our findings suggest that tools combining GenAI, gamification, and peer-generated content may offer a promising avenue for encouraging reflection on holiday travel choices and supporting transitions toward more sufficient forms of tourism. Future research should further investigate the mechanisms through which such interventions influence travel-related intentions and social norms, while also improving chatbot performance, reducing attrition, and fostering broader and more sustained user engagement.
