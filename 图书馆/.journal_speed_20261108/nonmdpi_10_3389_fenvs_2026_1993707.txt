Maiju Technology Group Co., Ltd., Xiong’an, Hebei, China See more Article metrics View details Abstract The Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Greater Bay Area is marked by pronounced urban heterogeneity, so that a uniform account of its carbon emission drivers is unlikely to hold. Using panel data for 11 cities from 2015 to 2023, this study applies the Generalized Divisia Index Method to decompose eight drivers of emission change and to classify the cities by their factor contribution structures. Economic growth (G) and output carbon intensity (CI) are the largest positive and negative contributors in ten of the eleven cities (mean G = +19.18%, mean CI = −9.94%), the exception being Macao. An exploratory Ward clustering of the four most variable factors, cross-validated against k-means, distinguishes four types of driving structure—carbon-intensity suppression, economic-scale driven, energy-scale expansion, and a specialized service-structure type (Macao); an innovation index combining research and development (R&D) intensity and patent density varies independently of these types. Decoupling is likewise uneven, ranging from strong decoupling in three cities (Foshan, Zhongshan and Hong Kong; Tapio elasticity t = −0.152 to −0.437) to weak decoupling in six (0.133 ≤ t ≤ 0.462) and expansive coupling in Huizhou and Macao. Per-capita emissions and energy-structure carbon intensity show marked dispersion. These findings inform a three-tiered policy discussion—differentiated measures, collaborative governance, and data support—for coordinated low-carbon governance in urban agglomerations. 1 Introduction Global climate change poses a severe challenge to sustainable development, and the Paris Agreement has forged a broad consensus on a green, low-carbon transition. In 2020, China announced its “dual carbon” goals—peaking carbon emissions by 2030 and achieving carbon neutrality by 2060—which hinge on progressively decoupling economic growth from carbon emissions. Because urban agglomerations concentrate both economic activity and energy use, understanding their differentiated carbon-emission drivers is central to operationalising the dual-carbon strategy. The Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Greater Bay Area (GBA) is one of China’s major development platforms and comprises the “9 + 2” configuration of nine mainland cities and two Special Administrative Regions. Its member cities differ markedly in industrial structure, development stage, resource endowment, and institutional system. These differences imply that the driving structure of carbon emissions is unlikely to be uniform across the region and that a single, undifferentiated mitigation policy is unlikely to be effective. A systematic, city-level comparison of emission drivers is therefore needed. From a methodological standpoint, Index Decomposition Analysis (IDA) is a classic tool for quantifying emission drivers, and the Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) is its mainstream variant (Ang, 2005); however, the product-form construction of LMDI fixes the factor set in advance: at most one absolute quantity can enter as the leading term of a single product chain, and the remaining factors must enter as ratios. The Generalized Divisia Index Method (GDIM) addresses both points through a system of constraint equations that captures the intrinsic relationships among factors (Vaninsky, 2014); a detailed comparison of the two methods is given in Section 2.1. The validity of GDIM has been demonstrated across sectors and scales—from China’s mining sector (Shao et al., 2016) and the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region (Yan and Yin, 2017) to the Yangtze River Delta (Wan et al., 2025a), the Southwest region (Wan et al., 2025b), and international comparisons between China and the United States (Zhang N. et al., 2022). The framework has also evolved beyond historical attribution: combined with Monte Carlo simulation and LSTM networks, it has been used to project industrial carbon-peaking windows (Li et al., 2023) and to assess carbon lock-in risks in urban buildings (Shi et al., 2026); it has also been extended to VOC emissions (Zhu et al., 2025) and, when coupled with decoupling indicators, to energy-related PM2.5 emissions (Yu and Fang, 2021), industrial carbon decoupling (Shen et al., 2024), and regional grouping strategies (Chen L. et al., 2025). These studies confirm GDIM’s suitability for multi-factor, interrelated systems, but they do not provide a unified decomposition framework covering all GBA cities. Existing GBA-related research has approached the issue from several directions. One stream identifies emission drivers using regression-based tools such as machine learning (Qiao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025) and geographically weighted regression (Chen M. F. et al., 2025); while informative, these approaches do not quantify each driver’s contribution to changes in emissions. A second stream examines energy transition and reduction efficiency (Zhang P. F. et al., 2022) and draws international decoupling comparisons (Xie et al., 2025; Qiao and Wu, 2025). A third stream develops urban typologies using aggregate or efficiency indicators (Ramaswami et al., 2018; Tong et al., 2018; Zhong et al., 2021; Kuncheria et al., 2025; Xing et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Chen J. et al., 2025); these classifications describe the “external manifestations” of low-carbon development rather than the “internal driving logic” of emission change. Finally, several GBA-specific studies have examined transport (Wang et al., 2023), building operations (Tang et al., 2025), CO2–VOC synergy (He et al., 2025), county-level urban expansion (Ming et al., 2024), collaborative governance (Wu, 2025), spatiotemporal evolution (Wang S. J. et al., 2026) and city–industry relations (Li X. J. et al., 2022), yet none applies a unified decomposition framework to the whole GBA city cluster. Three research gaps therefore remain. (1) Methodological gap. GDIM, which is capable of handling multi-factor constraints, has not been systematically applied to all cities of the GBA, leaving the region without a unified, comparable decomposition. (2) Classification gap. Existing city classifications rely mainly on aggregate or efficiency indicators (e.g., GDP, industrial structure) and do not incorporate the contribution structure of emission drivers, so they cannot reveal the underlying logic of emission change across cities. (3) Policy-alignment gap. Research on collaborative governance is largely qualitative and lacks an empirical basis for aligning quantitative decomposition results with differentiated mitigation strategies. To address these gaps, this study applies GDIM to the complete “9 + 2” city cluster using panel data from 2015 to 2023, decomposing the eight drivers of carbon-emission change over 2016–2023. Its objectives are: (1) to quantify the contribution of the eight factors (G, CI, E, CSI, P, PC, PG, EI) to emission change in the GBA and its constituent cities; (2) to classify the 11 cities according to the contribution structure of these drivers; and (3) to outline a three-tiered policy discussion—differentiated measures, collaborative governance, and data support—for the GBA’s low-carbon transition. This study contributes in three respects. Methodologically, it extends the application of GDIM to a highly heterogeneous megaregion. Empirically, it derives an empirical typology of low-carbon development in the Greater Bay Area from the contribution structure of driving factors rather than from aggregate indicators: the types are obtained by formal clustering, their labels follow the driving dimensions that define them, and innovation intensity is examined as a separate dimension rather than being built into the type names. Practically, it links the decomposition results to the binding target of reducing CO2 emissions per unit of GDP, offering a reference for regional collaborative governance. 2 Research methods and data 2.1 Method selection and theoretical basis Several families of methods can be used to analyse the drivers of carbon emissions, and they differ in the type of question they can answer. Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) is informative but requires input–output tables, which are not available annually for all GBA cities. STIRPAT-type and panel-regression models are well suited to estimating the elasticity of emissions with respect to socio-economic covariates, but they return marginal effects rather than an exact, additive attribution of the observed change in emissions and depend on functional-form assumptions. Index Decomposition Analysis (IDA) addresses precisely the latter question, and the Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) has become the mainstream IDA variant because it is residual-free and easy to interpret (Ang, 2005). However, LMDI is built on the product form of the Kaya identity, and this fixes the factor set in advance: the terms must form a single product chain that exhausts the identity, so only one absolute magnitude can enter as the chain’s leading term, while the others are absorbed into ratios and cannot stand as separate factors on an equal footing with them. Because that identity is exactly identified, LMDI also has no mechanism for enforcing several accounting constraints at once, which is what the eight-factor system used here requires. These are limitations of capability rather than of accuracy: a driver left outside the chosen identity is not merely estimated less precisely under LMDI, it is absent from the decomposition altogether. The Generalized Divisia Index Method (GDIM) overcomes both limitations. By introducing a system of constraint equations that encodes the intrinsic relationships among factors, GDIM allows absolute and relative indicators to be analysed within one framework and separates the contributions of mutually related factors without double counting (Vaninsky, 2014). This makes GDIM the most suitable method for the present study: its analytical object is the contribution structure of all eight drivers (G, E, P, CI, CSI, PC, PG, EI), including the absolute-magnitude drivers (G, E, P), of which a single LMDI product chain can carry only one as its leading term, the rest being absorbed into ratios, and the urban typology in Section 3.3 depends on exactly this structure. The choice is therefore dictated by the requirements of the analysis rather than by convenience. 2.2 Model construction of GDIM The Generalized Divisia Index Method (GDIM) provides a rigorous mathematical framework for analyzing multi-factor driven systems (Vaninsky, 2014). The path integral was evaluated by summing over consecutive years, using the trapezoidal rule on the projected gradient (i.e., the mean of the end-point gradients multiplied by the annual change of each factor), which reproduces the discrete sum requested for panel data. Because the constraint projection removes the redundant contributions arising from the intrinsic correlation among the variables—that is, from the accounting identities that link them—the eight factor contributions are additively exact: across all 88 city–year units (11 cities × the eight annual intervals from 2016 to 2023), the largest absolute closure residual is 2 t of CO2 (mean 0.55 t), consistent with the one-tonne granularity implied by the four-decimal storage of the output values (in units of 104 t) rather than with any model misspecification; the full unit-by-unit check is reported in Supplementary Appendix D (Supplementary Table D1). 2.3 Analytical framework: contribution rates, classification and decoupling Based on the GDIM framework established in Section 2.2, this study applies the eight-factor decomposition to the Greater Bay Area. CI, CSI and EI are composite ratios defined by accounting identities, and their variation therefore jointly reflects industrial composition, energy mix, efficiency and the statistical boundary of the data. A decline in CI is reported as an intensity-related mitigation effect and is not evidence of technological progress as such. 2.3.2 Formal classification of driving structures The eight factor contributions are linked by the identities used to construct them and are not statistically independent: four eigenvalues of their correlation matrix are effectively zero, and E and CI are almost collinear across cities (r = 0.95). Entering all eight into a distance-based classification with eleven observations would therefore be unstable. The clustering input was restricted to the four factors with the largest between-city dispersion—E, CI, EI and PC, with standard deviations of 12.85, 9.69, 9.64 and 7.78 percentage points—each standardised to zero mean and unit variance. Cities were grouped by Ward’s hierarchical method with squared Euclidean distances (Ward, 1963). K-means with 200 random restarts run on the same matrix returns identical partitions at every k (adjusted Rand index = 1.00; Hubert and Arabie, 1985). The number of groups was selected from the merge schedule, with the internal-validity indices reported alongside it in Supplementary Appendix E—the Calinski–Harabasz index (Caliński and Harabasz, 1974), the silhouette coefficient (Rousseeuw, 1987) and the Davies–Bouldin index (Davies and Bouldin, 1979). With eleven observations these indices exclude solutions with five or more groups but do not discriminate reliably among smaller numbers of groups, so the choice of four rests on the merge schedule, whose decisive discontinuity is reported in Section 3.3. Type labels were assigned ex post from the group centroids on the eight original factors. The partition was then examined in two ways: jackknife re-runs omitting one city at a time (Hennig, 2007), and re-estimation under three dimension-reduction schemes (all eight factors; the four-factor scheme; the first two principal components). Given the sample size, the typology is read as an exploratory ordering of cities along two driving axes rather than as a hard partition; the groups that are stable across all checks are identified in Section 3.3. 2.3.3 Decoupling elasticity (Tapio index) To assess whether economic growth has been decoupled from carbon emissions within a formally recognised framework, this study adopts the decoupling elasticity index proposed by Tapio (2005), , where and denote the relative changes in CO2 emissions and gross regional product between two periods. Eight states are distinguished following Tapio (2005): strong decoupling , weak decoupling , expansive coupling , expansive negative decoupling , and, symmetrically, strong negative decoupling, weak negative decoupling, recessive coupling and recessive decoupling when output stagnates or declines . The Tapio index is a dimensionless elasticity and is conceptually distinct from the cumulative contribution rate defined in Section 2.3.1, which is normalised by base-year emissions; the two measures are therefore reported separately and are not compared on a common percentage scale. 2.4 Data sources and processing To guarantee the reliability, consistency and comparability of empirical data, this study compiles balanced panel data for the “9 + 2” cities in the Greater Bay Area covering the period 2015–2023. All raw data are retrieved from authoritative official statistical sources. Data sorting and carbon emission accounting are implemented following the principles of transparency and reproducibility. Rigorous pretreatment is conducted to address missing values, differences in statistical scope and other data anomalies. 2.4.1 Data sources This study involves three categories of core data: socioeconomic data, energy consumption data, and accounting coefficients. 2.4.1.1 Socioeconomic data The Gross Regional Product (GRP) and year-end permanent resident population data of the nine mainland cities are sourced from the Guangdong Statistical Yearbook and the statistical yearbooks of each city. Hong Kong’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and population data are obtained from the Census and Statistics Department of the Hong Kong Government. Macao’s GDP and population data are derived from the Macao Statistics and Census Bureau. 2.4.1.2 Energy consumption data The total energy consumption and fuel-specific consumption (including coal, petroleum products, natural gas, etc.) of the nine mainland cities are retrieved from the statistical yearbooks of each city. For Hong Kong, the terminal energy consumption by fuel type (coal, petroleum products, natural gas, electricity) is sourced from the Hong Kong Energy Statistics Yearbook. For Macao, the terminal energy consumption by fuel type (automotive gasoline, diesel, heavy fuel oil, gaseous fuels, etc.) is obtained from the Macao Statistics and Census Bureau. 2.4.1.3 Accounting coefficients Energy-to-standard-coal conversion factors are taken from Appendix 4 of the China Energy Statistical Yearbook (2023). Because energy statistics are compiled on different statistical bases across the eleven jurisdictions, two nested accounting boundaries are explicitly distinguished and applied consistently in the decomposition. Boundary B1—Total energy consumption (denominator). For six mainland cities (Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Huizhou, Jiangmen and Zhongshan), Hong Kong and Macao, the total final energy consumption is the jurisdiction-wide figure published in the official yearbooks: for the mainland cities, the “total energy consumption” reported in the energy chapter (primary energy consumption, i.e., inclusive of energy transformation and transmission losses); for Hong Kong, the territory-wide end-use energy consumption by fuel in the Hong Kong Energy Statistics Yearbook; for Macao, the territory-wide final energy consumption by fuel published by the Statistics and Census Service. For the remaining three mainland cities—Foshan, Dongguan and Zhaoqing—the yearbooks continuously publish only the comprehensive energy consumption of industrial enterprises above designated size; this industrial total is therefore used as a proxy for total energy consumption. This proxy systematically understates the absolute level of energy consumption—and hence of CO2 emissions—in these three cities, because energy use in the residential, commercial and transport sectors is not covered, and it may weaken the characterisation of non-industrial drivers (see Section 4.3). Boundary B2—Fuel-mix structure (numerator shares). City-level yearbooks do not release society-wide consumption for each individual fuel. For all nine mainland cities, the fuel-mix shares are therefore estimated from the category-specific consumption of industrial enterprises above designated size and applied to the corresponding total energy consumption under a structure-similarity assumption; Hong Kong and Macao use their officially published fuel-specific data directly. Implementation proceeds in three steps: (1) obtain the physical volume of each fuel consumed by industrial enterprises above designated size from each municipal yearbook and compute its share in the industrial total; (2) assuming that the society-wide fuel-mix structure is consistent with that of the industrial sector, apply these shares to the city-level total energy consumption; (3) convert the resulting physical volumes into 10,000 tce using the conversion factors of the China Energy Statistical Yearbook and calculate CO2 using the coefficients in Tables 2, 3. Treatment of the boundary difference. Only Boundary B1 differs across the sample, and only for Foshan, Dongguan and Zhaoqing. Because the GDIM decomposition is estimated independently for each city (each city’s result depends solely on its own 2015–2023 series), this boundary difference does not affect the per-city decomposition of the other eight cities; it only shifts the cross-city descriptive statistics and the typology, which are sample-dependent. Its economic-scale effect (G = 28.23%) is also the highest in the region. The structure points to a prominent risk that per capita carbon emissions will continue to rise alongside economic expansion, and to a severe carbon lock-in risk if green access thresholds are not imposed on new industrial capacity. Its position should therefore be read as the extreme end of the scale axis rather than as a category of one. 3.3.4 Specialized service-structure type: Macao Macao separates from all other cities at every clustering setting examined and forms a single-city group. Macao is thus the only city in the region with a positive CI value and the largest positive carbon-intensity effect in the whole region; specified on a year-by-year basis rather than as a single constant coefficient, this positive effect becomes even more pronounced, indicating that Macao’s economic expansion has been accompanied by a marked decline in carbon productivity. Macao thus presents a distinctive transition path. The gaming and tourism industries drive a dramatic expansion of energy consumption scale, which acts as the dominant promoting factor. At the same time, benefiting from the rapid substitution of power-generation fuel from heavy oil to natural gas, Macao achieves a strong inhibitory effect on energy-structure carbon intensity; it also shows strong mitigation performance in terms of per capita output and energy intensity. The result is a developmental pattern of high energy consumption under multi-dimensional strong carbon constraints. Hong Kong does not share this group. Its driving structure—the region’s lowest economic-scale effect (G = 10.87%) against a strong intensity effect (CI = −15.91%) and a negative energy-scale effect (−4.07%) — places it with Guangzhou, Foshan and Zhongshan in Section 3.3.1. It measures the aggregate carbon pressure per resident and reflects the combined intensity of production and consumption; because the numerator also covers emissions from industry, power generation, and services, PC cannot by itself isolate household consumption patterns or individual lifestyles. In this study PC is therefore interpreted strictly as a composite per capita indicator, and cross-city differences are discussed alongside each city’s industrial and energy structure. Huizhou (19.95%) shows the strongest driving effect on per capita carbon emissions, followed by Macao (5.34%), Zhaoqing (5.14%), Jiangmen (5.08%), Shenzhen (4.69%), Zhuhai (2.36%) and Dongguan (2.14%). It indicates that per capita carbon pressure has risen markedly alongside economic expansion and urbanization in these regions. In contrast, Zhongshan (−6.84%), Foshan (−6.65%), Hong Kong (−6.58%) and Guangzhou (−2.28%) present prominent inhibitory effects, revealing that these cities have achieved substantial easing per capita carbon pressure as their economies matured. This pattern is broadly consistent with the typology of Section 3.3. The economic-scale driven type (Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Dongguan, Jiangmen, Zhaoqing) records positive per capita carbon effects on average, whereas the carbon-intensity suppression type (Guangzhou, Foshan, Zhongshan, Hong Kong) records negative ones; Huizhou, the energy-scale expansion type, is the extreme case (PC = 19.95%). 3.4.2 Diversified paths and contradictions of energy system transition The contributions of energy consumption scale (E) and energy structure carbon intensity (CSI) reflect distinctly different transition logics and challenges amid heterogeneous resource endowments and industrial structures. As illustrated in Figure 4, the energy-system transition across the Greater Bay Area spans a wide spectrum rather than following a single path. Macao anchors one end, combining the region’s strongest energy-structure mitigation (CSI = −21.38%) with its only positive output-carbon-intensity effect (CI = +16.93%): the supply side has been transformed, yet carbon productivity has declined. Shenzhen anchors the other end, recording the region’s only positive energy-structure carbon-intensity effect (CSI = +4.57%; Section 3.3.2): carbon productivity continues to improve, but the low-carbon upgrading of the power supply has lagged. The two cases mirror each other and carry the same implication—that the direction of a city’s energy-system effect is set by whether the supply-side transition runs ahead of or behind the change in output composition, not by its level of economic development. The full-period conclusions are therefore not driven by any single sub-period. 3.5.3 Sample robustness As noted in Section 2.4, the energy-consumption accounts of Foshan, Dongguan and Zhaoqing follow an above-scale industrial boundary, whereas those of the other cities follow a whole-society boundary. Where a city’s industrial base is organised around electricity-intensive, high-value-added manufacturing, output growth tends to raise the demand for power faster than the supply side can be decarbonised, so the energy-supply margin tends to become the binding constraint. Mitigation in such cities may therefore depend less on local fuel switching than on the cleanliness and stability of imported electricity, which makes regional power-grid coordination an important enabling condition for their own decarbonisation. Cross-city disparities in per capita carbon emissions track differences in economic structure and industrial composition (Figure 5). Because the per capita indicator is an aggregate ratio, household-level behaviour cannot be separated from it (Section 5.3); public engagement and the development of low-carbon lifestyles remain relevant prerequisites for a society-wide low-carbon transition (Zuo, 2023; Xia and Huang, 2023), and the growing share of small households (one- and two-person families) has been identified in the literature as a micro driver of residential energy consumption (Fu et al., 2021). These factors remain an open question for micro-level research. FIGURE 5 Ranking of per capita carbon emission contribution rate of cities in the Greater Bay Area. Inter-city heterogeneity can be further explained by divergent institutional and historical development trajectories. The nine mainland cities and the two SARs have experienced distinct industrialisation and energy-system evolution over recent decades. Hong Kong and Macao completed industrial restructuring earlier, with manufacturing largely relocated to the mainland Pearl River Delta; their current energy demand is dominated by commercial, transport and service sectors, which differs greatly from manufacturing-intensive cities such as Foshan and Dongguan. For the mainland cities, differences in the timing of reform and economic opening, in the timing of industrial transfer and in policy support have shaped uneven economic scales, energy endowments and structural and efficiency-related capabilities. Such historical and institutional differences complement the GDIM decomposition results and help explain why a single uniform emission-reduction policy is unlikely to be equally effective across the GBA urban agglomeration. 4.2 Comparative analysis with previous research findings The four-category urban classification in this study complements existing urban typology research. International studies typically classify Chinese cities into industrial, commercial and mixed types based on economic attributes (Ramaswami et al., 2018), which resonates with the economic-scale driven and carbon-intensity suppression types identified here. Traffic-based urban classification applied in the San Francisco Bay Area also illustrates the feasibility of multi-dimensional urban grouping (Kuncheria et al., 2025). Studies of China have adopted K-means clustering to divide Guangdong’s prefecture-level cities into four low-carbon types (Zhong et al., 2021), and structural heterogeneity across cities has been widely documented in urban agglomeration research (Li M. et al., 2024). Different from previous classifications relying on total carbon emission levels, this study classifies cities based on factor contribution structures, focusing on how emission changes are composed within cities. Traditional classifications reflect external disparities in urban development stages, whereas this study highlights differences in emission driving mechanisms. Compared with trade-based production–consumption city typologies (Mi et al., 2016), the proposed framework describes urban heterogeneity in terms of structural composition and temporal evolution, offering an empirical basis for differentiated carbon mitigation policy formulation. This study’s findings show both consistency and divergence with existing Greater Bay Area-related decomposition research. The corroborating evidence is reported in Section 3.1; the divergences arise mainly from differences in city coverage, observation window and decomposition method. First, most previous studies covered only the nine mainland GBA cities and excluded Hong Kong and Macao (Ming et al., 2024), whereas this study incorporates the complete “9 + 2” city cluster; including these service-oriented special administrative regions, with their distinctive energy systems, enriches the heterogeneity analysis. Second, this study extends the research period to 2023, covering the post-pandemic recovery and the early dual-carbon implementation stage, whereas most earlier studies terminated their observation before 2021 (Wang et al., 2026), which generates discrepancies in the estimated contribution characteristics. Third, this study employs the GDIM model, which accounts for the inherent correlations among variables, whereas the conventional approach is LMDI; such methodological differences redistribute factor contributions and partly explain the divergences observed. 4.3 Theoretical basis for regional collaborative emission-reduction Heterogeneous decoupling statuses reflect urban complementary advantages and provide a basis for regional collaborative emission reduction. In principle, complementary resource endowments and functional positioning can lower the cost of regional decarbonisation and help balance equity and efficiency. Industrial restructuring and efficiency improvement are identified in the literature as core drivers of strong decoupling (Li and Xiao, 2026), which aligns with the transition path of cities such as Foshan and Zhongshan, whose decoupling elasticities are −0.152 and −0.201. Notably, theoretical complementary advantages cannot be directly transformed into effective regional collaboration. The GBA involves three customs territories, two legal systems and multi-level administrative governance, creating institutional barriers to cross-regional policy coordination. Differences in fiscal capacity, environmental regulation intensity and industrial policy orientation further hinder a unified cost–benefit allocation for collaborative emission reduction. For example, developing peripheral cities may regard strict carbon constraints as barriers to economic growth, while the region’s leading cities may lack incentives for low-carbon technology transfer without reasonable benefit compensation. Effective regional collaboration therefore requires systematic institutional design, benefit-sharing mechanisms and a clear division of responsibilities. Accordingly, this study offers a three-tier policy discussion that could help match differentiated urban transition demands and address cross-regional coordination dilemmas. 5 Conclusions and policy implications 5.1 Main research conclusions Based on empirical decomposition and mechanism analysis, this study adopts the GDIM to investigate carbon emission driving mechanisms across 11 cities in the Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Greater Bay Area (GBA) during 2016–2023, yielding four main conclusions. First, economic growth and carbon-intensity reduction constitute the two dominant components of the region’s emission change. These contributions are associational decompositions of accounting identities and do not imply causality. Economic scale (G) is positively associated with carbon emissions, while output carbon intensity (CI) serves as the primary mitigating factor, forming a gradient across the region’s cities. Foshan and Zhongshan record strong decoupling, their growth and intensity effects nearly offsetting (t = −0.152 and −0.201), and Hong Kong records the largest negative elasticity in the region (t = −0.437); Shenzhen and Guangzhou display a high-growth, high-mitigation pattern; Huizhou is dominated by economic expansion with insufficient carbon constraints; and Macao, with a positive CI value, exhibits a distinctive decline in carbon productivity alongside economic growth. Second, the driving-structure classification distinguishes four types in the GBA. Carbon-intensity suppression cities (Guangzhou, Foshan, Zhongshan and Hong Kong) combine the strongest intensity effect in the sample (CI = −15.90% on average) with negative per capita carbon effects. Economic-scale driven cities (Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Dongguan, Jiangmen and Zhaoqing) pair the highest economic-scale effect among the multi-city groups (G = 21.39%) with a weaker intensity response, and they span the full range of the innovation index reported in Section 3.3.5. Huizhou constitutes an energy-scale expansion type (E = 26.15%, PC = 19.95%, CI = −3.25%), and Macao a specialized service-structure type whose combination of energy expansion, rising carbon intensity and strongly negative per capita output and energy-intensity effects has no analogue among the manufacturing-oriented cities. Third, per capita carbon emissions (PC) and the energy-system indicators account for much of the variation in reduction effects across cities. PC is an aggregate ratio and is therefore read alongside each city’s industrial and energy structure (Section 5.3); it is markedly uneven, dominating the positive side in Huizhou (PC = 19.95%) and remaining positive in Macao and in most of the economic-scale driven cities, while curbing per capita carbon pressure in Foshan, Zhongshan and Hong Kong (PC between −6.6% and −6.8%). The contrast in energy-structure carbon intensity (CSI) between Shenzhen (+4.57%, the only positive value in the region) and Macao (−21.38%) points to different stages of the energy transition and to conditions on which a regional collaborative framework could build. Fourth, the analytical sequence adopted here—decomposition of the driving structure, classification of cities by that structure, and identification of the factors that matter most—may also be applicable to other urban agglomerations, although its transferability is not tested here. The study illustrates the applicability of GDIM to multi-factor, strongly correlated and structurally heterogeneous carbon-emission systems, and shows how quantitative decomposition results can be linked to policy discussion. 5.2 Policy implications The following measures are indicative directions derived from the decomposition results. Their feasibility depends on local institutional, fiscal and data conditions, and would require further ex-ante assessment before implementation. They illustrate how the empirical findings can inform policy design. 5.2.1 Tier 1: differentiated regulation Carbon-intensity suppression cities (Guangzhou, Foshan, Zhongshan and Hong Kong) could consolidate the intensity margin that defines them, maintaining dual constraints on carbon emission quantity and intensity and reinforcing the mitigation that has already delivered the region’s strongest decoupling. Led by provincial authorities, a manufacturing green transformation alliance could be established to replicate Foshan’s experience, with fiscal and tax incentives for industrial parks and enterprises that achieve effective carbon offsetting through intensity reduction. Hong Kong, whose driving structure places it with these cities rather than with Macao, seems better matched by intensity-oriented instruments; its energy-scale effect is already negative (E = −4.07%), so volume-based instruments are no longer the binding lever. Economic-scale driven cities (Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Dongguan, Jiangmen and Zhaoqing) could align the pace of expansion with the intensity response. Shenzhen, whose energy-structure carbon intensity is the only positive value in the region, faces its binding constraint on the energy-supply margin: green power procurement ratios and the share of external clean energy supply could be incorporated into its assessment systems to ease this structural carbon pressure. Huizhou, the energy-scale expansion type, would be better served by mechanisms that keep new growth on a low-carbon path and avoid high-carbon lock-in. Strict carbon evaluation for new projects and industrial planning would be required, matched with distributed new energy deployment. Green urbanisation policies could curb the growth of per capita carbon emissions, which are the highest in the region (PC = 19.95%). Macao, the specialized service-structure type, would need instruments matched to its exceptional profile (E = 42.06%, CI = +16.93%, CSI = −21.38%): explicit energy-consumption ceilings and refined energy management, shifting from extensive energy consumption towards refined low-carbon governance. 5.2.2 Tier 2: collaborative governance The GBA could consider building a unified regional green technology transfer and certification platform. Standardized green technology catalogues and certification criteria would facilitate the transfer of mature low-carbon technologies from cities that have already decoupled to those whose carbon pressure is still rising. A regional green power trading mechanism could be piloted. Green power packages drawing on Yunnan–Guizhou hydropower and Guangdong offshore wind could be supplied to cities facing a rising energy-structure carbon intensity, such as Shenzhen. A special GBA carbon neutrality development fund could be considered. Jointly funded by the region’s core cities and provincial finance, the fund could support low-carbon infrastructure in peripheral cities and subsidise cross-city green cooperation projects. 5.2.3 Tier 3: data support Unified regional carbon accounting standards could be developed. Guangdong’s statistical departments, together with the Hong Kong and Macao authorities, could formulate common GBA urban greenhouse-gas accounting guidelines, adopting consistent emission factors for fossil fuels and region-specific electricity coefficients to address inconsistencies in statistical scope and definitions. A long-term monitoring and evaluation system for carbon driving factors could be developed based on the GDIM framework. Regular updates of factor contribution rates and of the urban typology would support periodic tracking of regional carbon structural change, improving the relevance and adaptability of low-carbon policy formulation. Taken together, these three tiers illustrate how the empirical findings of this study could inform policy design across the GBA’s low-carbon transition. 5.3 Limitations and future prospects This study has several limitations that indicate directions for further research. First, data limitations exist. Restricted by public statistical data, partial energy consumption statistics of several cities only cover industrial sectors, which may affect the accurate identification of overall carbon driving structures. Future research can supplement multi-sector energy and enterprise micro-data to optimize the accounting system. Second, the GDIM method adopted in this study provides static retrospective decomposition of historical emission changes, lacking dynamic scenario simulation. Subsequent studies can combine system dynamics or CGE models to predict carbon emission evolution trends under diverse collaborative emission reduction scenarios. Third, this study focuses on territorial direct and electricity-related indirect emissions, ignoring embodied carbon emissions in cross-regional supply chains. Future research can adopt multi-regional input-output models to re-evaluate GBA carbon footprints from a consumption-based perspective. Fourth, two measurement caveats should be noted. Electricity emission factors are applied in the model on a standard-coal-equivalent basis, as are the other energy types, and their year-to-year variation follows the officially published annual grid emission factors; were electricity-related emissions instead computed on a terminal-electricity (kWh) basis, the absolute magnitude of the electricity component would be adjusted accordingly, although this does not materially alter the relative contributions that are the focus of the decomposition. In addition, the per capita indicator used here is an aggregate ratio based on territorial emissions and cannot isolate residential consumption; household-level analysis would require survey or micro-data that are beyond the scope of this study. Fifth, the decoupling elasticities reported in Section 3.4.3 are computed from GRP at current prices, whereas Tapio (2005) conventionally uses real growth. The elasticities are therefore lower bounds: a real-price calculation would raise them and would soften, though not reverse, the decoupling conclusions for the weakly decoupled cities.
