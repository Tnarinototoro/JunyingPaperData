To this end, this analysis scrutinizes the impacts of renewable energies, GDP per capita, and environmental policy, in particular market-based and non-market-based environmental strategies, on GHG emissions in selected European Union countries. The Instrumental Variable Method of Moments Quantile Regression is used to control for endogeneity. In addition, new techniques, such as quantile slope homogeneity, quantile unit root, quantile cross-sectional dependence, and quantile causality tests, are used. The main findings reveal that the negative effect of renewable energies persists across the distribution of GHG emissions. However, this effect is weaker at high emission levels than at low emission levels. Concerning market-based instruments, they reduce GHG emissions at low and medium levels. Furthermore, the magnitude of this effect progressively diminishes as GHG emissions increase. When considering non-market-based instruments, their impact is negative at medium and high levels of GHG emissions. In addition, their effect is increasing with the level of GHG emissions. Finally, the quantile causality test and robustness checks using Smoothed Instrumental Variables Quantile Regression and Quantile Regression with Common Shocks confirm the baseline results. These results emphasize the necessity to adapt environmental instruments to the level of GHG emissions to attain optimal results. Clinical trial registration This is not applicable. European Economic Law Environmental Economics Resource and Environmental Economics Econometrics Public Economics Quantitative Economics 1 Introduction Mitigating greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions while continuing economic expansion is one of the most challenging obstacles to achieving sustainable development goals. Over many decades, population growth and economic expansion have increased energy demand, which is mainly met by fossil fuels. This increase in the supply of fossil fuels is considered the main source of GHG emissions. As European Union (EU) countries continue their path of economic growth, environmental issues are becoming very serious. To mitigate the use of fossil fuels and the ensuing GHG emissions, the EU Commission has strengthened the environmental obligations. In fact, the EU has realized great achievements in terms of pollution reduction and prevention against climate change threats. The European Green Deal is considered one of the most ambitious frameworks in the world, putting the EU countries at the forefront of global climate action. The implementation of the European Green Deal is supported by the European Climate Law, which provides a legal framework that transforms environmental objectives into legal obligations. This law entered into force in July 2021 and aims to reach carbon neutrality by 2050. It also sets a transitional target of curtailing GHG emissions by at least 55% by 2030, with reference to 1990 levels. The transition to clean energies and environmental regulation present the key pillars of the European Green Deal. In fact, EU countries have set ambitious goals to expand renewable energies (RE), phase out coal and nuclear energy, and alleviate reliance on fossil fuels. In addition, the EU has adopted rigorous environmental strategies, such as the EU Emissions Trading System. Actually, almost all regions of the world have considered the transition to RE an inevitable path to combat the dangers of climate change by curtailing the consumption of fossil fuels. In particular, the recent Conferences of the Parties have recognized RE as an optimal strategy to attain climate commitments. According to REN 21 [ 44 ], 133 countries had net zero targets, while 89 countries had RE targets in 2024. Despite global efforts to hasten the diffusion of RE, their share of total final energy consumption remained low at 13.4% in 2022. In the EU countries, the share also remains low at 25.2% in 2024 compared to the objective of 42.5% by 2030. Hence, it is clear that the remaining gap remains large, and efforts should be made to increase this share. Consequently, strict environmental policies are required to hasten this green transition. The EU has used a panoply of environmental strategies to reduce GHG emissions, internalize environmental externalities, and accelerate the shift from fossil fuels to renewables. These objectives are achieved through mechanisms such as energy taxes, emissions trading, emission limit values, pollution caps, renewable support schemes, feed-in tariffs, and clean innovation incentives. According to OECD [ 36 ], over the last two decades, the EU has increased the stringency of its environmental strategies in a remarkable way, although some cross-country differences remain in both policy design and implementation. 1.1 Motivation of the study The EU context constitutes a very interesting empirical setting characterized by ambitious environmental targets, strict environmental policies, and an advanced stage of RE adoption. This context makes it ideal for scrutinizing the effectiveness of the different environmental policies, along with the RE transition, in achieving climate targets. In addition, RE transition is a pillar of the European Green Deal, which attempts to reduce Europe’s dependency on external suppliers. Furthermore, environmental policy stringency (EPS) is one of the most effective channels through which EU countries are trying to reduce GHG emissions and foster the RE transition. By jointly analyzing the impacts of GDP per capita, RE transition, and environmental governance, this paper allows us to determine whether EU countries are truly moving toward a green growth model aligned with the logic of the European Green Deal. 1.2 Research objective and central research questions The primary objective of this study is to assess the impacts of RE, GDP per capita, and environmental strategies on GHG emissions in a panel of selected EU countries from 1990 to 2020. In addition, by analyzing the effects of the sub-indices of the EPS index, this study gives a detailed investigation that clearly differentiates between market-based strategies (MS) and non-market-based strategies (NMS). Finally, the Instrumental Variable Method of Moments Quantile Regression (IV-MMQR) is used to yield divergent quantile-based findings and to elucidate the most effective environmental instruments at every level of GHG emissions. To attain this objective, this study will give responses to the following research questions: i. How do RE, GDP per capita, MS, and NMS jointly affect GHG emissions in selected EU countries? ii. To what degree do the levels of GHG emissions affect the effectiveness of these measures? iii. Which type of environmental policy is more effective in curtailing the different levels of GHG emissions? iv. What are the directions of the causal links between the independent variables and GHG emissions in EU countries? 1.3 Main contributions This analysis offers an important contribution to EU countries’ research on pollution drivers by addressing a gap in the literature on effective strategies to reduce GHG emissions. Although many papers have analyzed the determinants of carbon emissions, few have focused on GHG emissions, which are also highly dangerous. Furthermore, many papers have investigated the effects of EPS on carbon emissions, these analyses are insufficient, as the NMS are more oriented toward reducing nitrate and sulfur, not carbon emissions. In this sense, it is much more informative to investigate the impacts of these strategies on GHG emissions as a whole. In addition, this is the first paper to analyze the impacts of MS and NMS on GHG emissions within the EU context, while controlling for endogeneity. In fact, this study uses the IV-MMQR to assess the effectiveness of RE and environmental strategies in mitigating GHG emissions. Actually, the IV-MMQR framework builds on the MMQR approach by integrating instrumental variables, thereby enabling consistent estimation when regressors are endogenous. This method shows the different effects of RE, MS and NMS across the conditional distribution of GHG emissions, which addresses the issue of focusing only on the conditional mean. Actually, EU countries have different levels of GHG emissions, and even within each country, emissions differ over time. Hence, it is important to elucidate the most effective strategies for every level of GHG emissions. Methodologically, this paper differs from existing studies by adopting a quantile approach across all methodological steps: quantile cross-sectional dependence (CSD) test, quantile slope homogeneity test, quantile CIPS test, and quantile causality. In addition, to test the robustness of our results, two recent methods are used. First, to control for endogeneity, the Smoothed Instrumental Variables Quantile Regression (SIVQR) method of Kaplan and Sun [ 25 ] is adopted. Second, to account for common factors in the case of the EU countries, the Quantile Regression with Common Shocks (QR-CS) of Chiang et al., [ 9 ] is also used. The current study is structured as follows: Sect. 1 contains the introductory part. Sect. 2 contains the theoretical underpinning and the literature review. Sect. 3 details the methodology. Sect. 4 presents a detailed discussion of the results. Sect. 5 develops the causality analysis and robustness check. Finally, Sect. 6 contains the conclusion. 2 Literature review 2.1 Theoretical underpinning This study falls within the framework of environmental economics, which offers a robust theoretical basis for comprehending the role of environmental instruments in mitigating polluting emissions. Environmental economics is a branch of natural resource economics that argues for the need to put a price on pollution; this approach is called the internalization of externalities. This approach requires implementing regulatory measures and economic instruments that allow polluters to bear the full cost of their actions. Instruments for internalizing externalities are generally classified as regulatory and economic instruments. Regulatory instruments consist of standards imposed by the state to ensure that the optimal level of pollution is achieved. For their part, economic instruments encompass a range of tools, including taxes and pollution permit markets. Within this framework, Weitzman [ 52 ] distinguished between price-based and quantity-based instruments. Pigou, [ 41 ] pioneered the advocacy of pollution taxes, while Coase [ 13 ] suggested negotiation for the efficient allocation of resources. Issues such as the international dimension of environmental problems, the effects of environmental policies on competitiveness and innovation, and the choice of the most appropriate instruments have generated much discussion in environmental economics [ 6 ]. More recently, the Porter assumption [ 42 ] holds that environmental governance benefits firms by stimulating innovation and increasing productivity. 2.2 Studies on the drivers of GHG emissions While the studies concerning the drivers of carbon emissions are numerous, those on the determinants of GHG emissions are scarce. The positive effect of GDP on GHG emissions has been demonstrated by many studies, including Aslam et al., [ 2 ], Dahmani [ 14 ], and Fatima et al., [ 16 ]. In the following paragraphs, we will only focus on the literature concerning the effects of RE and EPS. 2.2.1 RE and GHG emissions nexus Many recent studies have investigated the impacts of RE on carbon emissions, including Javed and Rapposelli [ 24 ] and Xuan [ 55 ]. Regarding the drivers of GHG emissions, Chien et al., [ 10 ] confirmed that both renewable electricity generation and RE consumption are effective in curtailing GHG emissions in ASEAN economies. Similarly, Chien et al., [ 11 ] used the CS-ARDL model to assess the effects of RE and urbanization on GHG emissions in Asian countries. While the impact of RE is negative, that of urbanization is positive. Complementing these findings, some recent studies considered the case of the G7. In particular, Manjang et al., [ 33 ] investigated the effects of RE, fossil fuels, natural resources, environmental taxes, and green technologies, on GHG emissions in G7 nations. The main findings revealed the long-term benefits of green technologies, eco-taxes, and renewables in protecting the environment. Furthermore, using an energy mix index across G7 countries, Adebayo et al., [ 1 ] demonstrated the beneficial impact of the energy transition on the abatement of GHG emissions. They adopted quantile-on-quantile regression and the KRLS estimator. Subsequent studies have extended this line of research by considering the income level. For instance, Khan et al., [ 27 ] analyzed a panel of high-income countries and validated the decreasing effect of RE, along with tourism and financial development, on GHG emissions. Recently, Kocak et al., [ 29 ] demonstrated that green electricity supports the alleviation of both GHG emissions and energy poverty in a panel of economies with different income levels. Further evidence is revealed by Zhang et al., [ 58 ], who used the non-linear ARDL model and demonstrated that RE reduce GHG emissions, in Indonesia, whereas inflation increases them. Finally, Raihan , [ 43 ] confirmed the fact that both green energy and technological innovation have negative influences on GHG emissions in South Korea. This result, obtained using the ARDL method, was validated using the FMOLS and DOLS models. H1. Renewable energies reduce GHG emissions. 2.2.2 The EPS and GHG emissions nexus Many recent studies have scrutinized the impacts of EPS on carbon emissions, including Gibba al., [ 20 ] Ma et al., [ 31 ], Wolde-Rufael and Mulat-weldemeskel [ 54 ], and Zheng et al., [ 59 ]. Recently, Dmytrenko et al., [ 15 ] analyzed the impacts of EPS and research and development on GHG emissions in European nations. The outcomes confirm the negative effect of EPS only in Western European countries. Along similar lines, Usman et al., [ 48 ] used the MMQR in the EU to demonstrate that financial development, trade openness, and EPS reduce pollutant emissions. In the case of OECD economies, Fatima et al., [ 16 ] focused on the interaction between energy transition and EPS using the MMQR. The main findings reveal that a 1% increase in this interaction term curtails GHG emissions by 0.271% to 0.3% across all quantiles. A more recent study by Wei and Joo [ 51 ] has also focused on OECD countries. The parametric results demonstrated that EPS is effective in mitigating GHG emissions from the agriculture sector but ineffective in the other sectors. Regarding NMS, they are ineffective at reducing GHG emissions across all sectors. When it comes to MS, they are effective at reducing GHG emissions from agricultural, energy use, and energy use in manufacturing industries and construction. Furthermore, Su et al., [ 46 ] analyzed the drivers of GHG emissions in OECD countries using quantile regression. The main results demonstrate that EPS and green electricity negatively affect GHG emissions at lower quantiles. Recent studies have increasingly explored special instruments, such as taxes and pollution rights trading. For instance, Filipiak and Wyszkowska [ 17 ] demonstrated that environmental taxes are a significant driver of GHG emissions abatement in EU countries. Furthermore, Tang et al., [ 47 ] scrutinized the environmental impacts of carbon emissions trading policies and pollution rights trading, and their main findings reveal their effectiveness in reducing carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide emissions. Even though an expanding body of literature places emphasis on a group of countries, some recent studies have considered the case of a single country. For instance, Aziz et al., [ 4 ] investigated ten provinces in Canada from 1995 to 2019. The outcomes reveal that while environmental governance has a mitigating impact on GHG emissions in Canada, GDP has an increasing effect. Finally, Carrilho-Nunes and Catalão-Lopes [ 8 ] demonstrated that while environmental strategies reduce GHG emissions in Portugal, technology transfer has a rather positive effect. H2. The strictness of environmental policies reduces the GHG emissions. H2a. The strictness of MS reduces GHG emissions. H2b. The strictness of NMS reduces GHG emissions. 2.3 Research gap This analysis deviates from existing studies by the following aspects. First, this is the first investigation into the impacts of the sub-indices of EPS and RE on GHG emissions in the context of EU countries. Usman et al., [ 48 ] used the MMQR to investigate the effect of EPS on GHG emissions in EU countries, but they did not use the sub-indices of EPS nor integrate the RE variable. Second, despite a large number of studies examining the link between EPS and environmental problems, the sub-indices of EPS remain an under-analyzed field of research. Actually, some recent papers have emphasized the effects of EPS and its sub-indices on some environmental factors, such as public green finance [ 3 ], environmental technology [ 19 ], load capacity factor [ 26 ]. However, there is only one study on the impacts of the sub-indices of EPS on GHG emissions, that of Wei and Joo [ 51 ] in OECD countries. However, the current study differs by adopting a different panel of countries and methodology. Third, the current study uses the IV-MMQR to assess the impacts of environmental strategies on GHG emissions while controlling for endogeneity, which gives very robust estimates compared to other methods. This method illustrates the different impacts of MS and NMS across the conditional distribution of GHG emissions, rather than only on the conditional mean, as traditional methods do. Finally, this study adopts a quantile approach from the CSD test to the causality test and robustness check, and includes recent methods, such as QR-CS of Chiang et al., [ 9 ] and SIVQ of Kaplan and Sun [ 25 ]. 3 Methodology 3.1 Data description This study analyses the drivers of GHG emissions in 16 EU countries during the period 1990–2020: Austria, Belgium, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Slovakia, Spain, and Sweden. Since MS and NMS indices have values from 0 to 6, the sample is limited to EU countries for which the indices are strictly positive over the period 1990–2020. This choice ensures that the logarithmic transformation can be applied to the full panel. The choice of the study period is due to the availability of data up to 2020. It can be divided into three main steps: preliminary analysis, model estimation, and robustness checks and causality analysis. The first step is the preliminary analysis. In fact, the quantile slope homogeneity test is applied. Then, to verify the presence of CSD and determine the order of integration, the quantile CSD and CIPS tests are employed. Subsequently, the Durbin-Hausman cointegration test is applied. The second step consists of estimating the model using the MMQR. Then, a set of post-estimation diagnostic tests specific to the MMQR are conducted to assess the suitability of the data and the econometric framework. Subsequently, the potential endogeneity of the regressors is assessed. Once the endogeneity issue is confirmed, the relevance and validity of the proposed instrumental variables is assessed using three diagnostic tests. Finally, the IV-MMQR framework is adopted to account for endogeneity while upholding the advantages of the MMQR approach. The third step contains the robustness check and causality analysis. In fact, the SIVQR and QR-CS are used as robustness checks. In addition, the causality among the dependent and independent variables is assessed using a quantile causality test. After the estimation, a set of diagnostics, as suggested by Machado and Santos Silva [ 32 ], is presented in Table 9 . First, the model is internally coherent, which means that the fitted MMQR scale function is strictly positive for all observations, as required by Machado and Santos Silva [ 32 ]. Then, the null assumption of no scale effect is rejected, which means that quantile slopes vary across quantiles. Furthermore, there is rejection of the null assumption of homogeneity of the scale fixed effects, indicating that the fixed effects are distributional. Finally, a bootstrap Hausman-type test contrasts the MMQR and Canay and [ 7 ] coefficient paths (1000 bootstrap replications). This test rejects the location-shift restriction ( p = 0.037, Bonferroni across tau). This result advocates for MMQR compared to Canay-type estimators. In general, the diagnostic tests confirm the suitability of the data to the MMQR framework. Table 8 Results of the MMQR Full size table Table 9 Full diagnostic for the MMQR Full size table Endogeneity is an important econometric issue, which may generate biased estimates. In fact, in our model, the relationships between MS, NMS, REC, GDP, and GHG may involve reverse causality and omitted factors. In this case, it is required to test for endogeneity and, if confirmed, adopt a relevant instrumental-variable estimation approach. The endogeneity test confirms the null hypothesis that NMS is exogenous. However, it rejects the null hypothesis for GDP, REC, and MS, supporting the use of an instrumental-variable approach for these variables. Lagged values of the regressors are used to address potential endogeneity. In this case, the instruments are sufficiently related to the endogenous explanatory variables they instrument. This is very common in many recent studies, such as that of Işık et al., [ 23 ]. However, the relevance of the instruments is not sufficient, and the exclusion restriction condition must be respected. To verify this, adequate diagnostic tests are applied. The results of the diagnostic tests confirm the validity of the lagged values of RE and MS. However, the lagged values of GDP are not used because the diagnostic tests do not validate them. For this reason, education expenditure as a percentage of gross national income and labor force participation rate, which are collected from WDI [ 50 ], were included in the model as instrumental variables to address potential endogeneity issues between GDP and GHG emissions. The choice of these two instruments is based on their links with GDP through human capital, productive capacity, and economic activity. In addition, the Hansen J statistic fails to reject the null assumption that the instrumental variables are uncorrelated with the error term. Hence, after considering all variables in the model, these instruments affect GHG emissions only through the endogenous variables they stand for, and not through other variables. This result supports the exclusion restriction condition. Furthermore, three diagnostic tests are conducted to assess instrument validity. The results conducted on IV-2SLS estimation are detailed in Table 10 . First, based on the Kleibergen and Paap [ 28 ] LM statistic, the null assumption of under-identification is rejected, implying that the excluded instruments provide adequate information to identify GDP, MS, and REC. Moreover, the Kleibergen-Paap rk Wald F statistic values exceed the critical value reported by Stock and Yogo [ 45 ] (for a 10% maximal IV size), confirming that the instruments are not weak. Finally, the J-test of Hansen [ 21 ] does not reject the null assumption of valid instruments (uncorrelated with the error term). Hence, there is no statistical evidence against the validity of the excluded instruments. Consequently, these tests support the use of the IV-MMQR and confirm that the findings are robust to the endogeneity of GDP, REC, and MS. This reduction effect is confirmed in all quantiles. In addition, the results confirm that the negative impact of REC is stronger at the low-emission quantile than at the high-emission quantile. For instance, at quantile 0.2, a 1% upsurge in REC mitigates GHG emissions by 0.159%, while at quantile 0.8, this reduction is about 0.149%. This beneficial effect of REC in mitigating GHG emissions is confirmed by the studies of Chien et al., [ 10 ] in ASEAN economies and Raihan and [ 43 ] in South Korea. This result confirms that the diffusion of RE is effective even in a high-emission context, but it may be insufficient alone and needs to be reinforced by the encouragement of energy efficiency and cleaner industries. In fact, pollutant emissions at the upper quantile are not solely the result of the energy mix; they are also influenced by many other factors. Furthermore, the effect of GDP per capita is positive across all quantiles. Hence, in EU countries, economic growth continues to drive pollution by stimulating energy use and increasing demand for products. This finding is confirmed by many studies, including that of Ben Youssef and Dahmani [ 5 ]. In addition, the results confirm that the positive impact of GDP is stronger at the high-emission quantile (0.488 at Q 0.80 ) than at the low-emission quantile (0.401 at Q 0.20 ). Concerning MS, they reduce GHG emissions across all quantiles, except the upper one, where the effect is statistically non-significant. In fact, a 1% increase in the strictness of MS mitigates GHG emissions by 0.272%, 0.190%, 0.151%, and 0.099% at quantiles Q 0.20, Q 0.40 , Q 0.50 , and Q 0.60 , respectively. Hence, it is evident that MS, including carbon taxes, are effective in mitigating pollutant emissions, however this effect is more pronounced at lower quantiles and statistically non-significant at the upper quantile. The effectiveness of MS in reducing GHG emissions is confirmed by Wei and Joo [ 51 ] in OECD countries. More precisely, results in Table 11 confirm that MS persistently lessen GHG emissions in low (Q 0.20 ) and medium (Q 0.40 , Q 0.5 , Q 0.6 ) emission contexts. In addition, the intensity of the impact decreases gradually as pollutant emissions increase. This result could be explained by the fact that MS, which include environmental taxes, emissions trading schemes, feed-in tariffs, and green certificates, represent strong price signals and market-driven mechanisms capable of internalizing environmental externalities. In fact, these policy tools are intended to give a price to pollution while also offering financial incentives to encourage emissions reduction. These strategies encourage firms to adopt cleaner procedures and increase RE use. However, in high-emission regimes (the upper quantile), MS become ineffective due to the presence of structural rigidities, such as energy-intensive industries, and the need for large-scale national investments to make structural changes, so MS alone are not effective and need to be part of a national environmental strategy. When considering NMS, the impact is negative in medium and high quantiles. Actually, a 1% increment in the strictness of NMS mitigates GHG emissions by 0.081%, 0.10%, 0.125%, and 0.18% at the quantiles Q 0.4 , Q 0.5 , Q 0.6 , and Q 0.8 , respectively. First, it is clear that the effect is smaller than that of MS at the quantiles Q 0.4 and Q 0.5 , but becomes greater at the quantiles Q 0.6 and Q 0.8 . Hence, the impact of NMS is statistically insignificant at low GHG emissions but becomes significant at medium and high levels. This finding indicates that NMS work more efficiently when GHG emissions are intermediate and high, suggesting that, in such a context, regulation is capable of influencing firms and orienting them to emissions-reduction behaviors. In addition, regulatory instruments become increasingly effective in high-emission contexts by including environmental standards, energy-efficiency regulations and clean technology requirements, which can engender structural changes. On the other hand, in low-emission contexts, emission-reduction targets are already met, which means that regulations become ineffective. Consequently, the impact intensity of NMS increases gradually as pollutant emissions rise, whereas that of MS decreases with GHG emissions and becomes ineffective at the upper quantile (Q 0.8 ). Using data from 1990 to 2020, the IV-MMQR is used to investigate the main effective strategies to attain net-zero emissions in the EU context, while controlling for endogeneity. In addition, robustness checks and a causality test were also applied to strengthen the results. The main findings confirm the mitigating effect of green energies on GHG emissions in all quantiles. Hence, the effectiveness of the transition to RE in mitigating GHG emissions is confirmed. In addition, the results confirm that, even as the EU adopts stringent environmental policies, economic growth remains environmentally costly. In fact, an increase in GDP per capita exerts a positive impact on GHG emissions across all quantiles. When it comes to environmental strategies, the results reflect some heterogeneity. In fact, MS are effective at lessening pollutant emissions at low and medium levels of GHG emissions. However, this diminishing effect decreases as emissions rise until it becomes totally ineffective at the upper quantile. This result confirms that MS alone are insufficient when GHG emissions are high. Finally, NMS are also showing that their effectiveness depends on the level of GHG emissions. In fact, the results demonstrate that NMS are effective measures for reducing GHG emissions at medium and high levels. However, their impact is statistically non-significant at the lower quantile (Q 0.20 ). 6.2 Policy suggestions According to the above results, the following policy implications are suggested for EU countries: - It is demonstrated that the effectiveness of environmental instruments depends on the level of GHG emissions. Since the EU carbon-neutrality strategy is uniformly applied, it will be more effective to set different regimes according to the level of GHG emissions and to set the most efficient strategies for each level. Hence, rather than a unique climate policy, adopting a differentiated environmental policy approach is recommended. For instance, in the case of low GHG emissions, the focus should be on market incentives to encourage innovation and RE use. In addition, in the case of a medium level of GHG emissions, MS and NMS, along with the development of RE, should be jointly applied to attain steady mitigation of GHG emissions. Finally, in the high-emission regime, NMS with important investments in RE are required to mitigate emissions. -Even though it is proven that RE mitigate GHG emissions at all quantiles, their effect declines steadily when GHG emissions increase. Hence, in a high-emission regime, RE promotion is effective but alone is still insufficient and should be complemented by carbon storage, grid modernization, etc. -The mitigation effect of MS is greater than NMS for low and medium levels of GHG emissions, which confirms the effectiveness of the EU ETS and carbon pricing as the backbone of the EU carbon neutrality policy. However, in a high- GHG-emission regime, NMS are more effective, reflecting the importance of an adequate regulatory framework in reducing high levels of pollutant emissions. 6.3 Limitations and future recommendations This research has neglected the institutional factor. In future research, it will be of great interest to jointly analyze the effect of institutional factors and their mediating role in accelerating the impacts of environmental strategies. In addition, this study is limited to 16 EU countries, it is recommended in future research to analyze all 27 EU countries. An analysis of the impacts of a carbon tax or EU ETS is recommended. Furthermore, this paper has neglected geopolitical risk because data are not available for all countries. In fact, it is a very interesting element to consider in analyzing the energy transition, energy trade, and pollution [ 22 , 57 ]. Finally, it is also recommended to assess the impacts of these determinants on GHG emissions across energy, agriculture, and industry.
