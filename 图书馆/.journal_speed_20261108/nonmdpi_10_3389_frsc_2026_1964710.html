Housing insecurity, displacement, and the right to adequate housing in the Gaza Strip: a mixed-methods study amid protracted conflict

  • FY

    Faisal Yousef Sabah *

  • Faculty of Arts, Arab American University, Tulkarm, Palestine

Article metrics

View details

Abstract

Background::

Housing insecurity is a critical humanitarian concern in conflict-affected settings, where home destruction, repeated displacement, overcrowding, and disrupted services affect health, dignity, and recovery. In the Gaza Strip, the war that began in October 2023 has intensified housing and shelter insecurity among displaced and war-affected households. This study examined the extent, patterns, correlates, and perceived consequences of housing insecurity.

Methods:

A convergent mixed-methods design combined a household survey with semi-structured interviews. Quantitative data were collected from 420 households across the five governorates of the Gaza Strip within the study period (2 April−4 August 2026). Qualitative data were obtained from 34 displaced household members and 10 key informants. Differences in severe housing insecurity, measured with a study-specific six-component index, were expressed as crude prevalence ratios, while qualitative data were thematically analyzed and integrated with the quantitative findings.

Results:

Of the sampled households, 88.6% had been displaced at least once, and 33.6% of displaced households reported four or more displacement episodes. Complete home destruction was reported by 42.9% of households and severe damage by 28.3%. Current shelter conditions were frequently inadequate: 78.1% reported overcrowding, 72.6% lacked sufficient water for hygiene, 68.8% lacked privacy, and 62.6% felt unsafe. Using a six-component study-specific index, 283 of 420 households (67.4%) met the criterion for severe housing insecurity. The prevalence was higher among female-headed households (78.1% vs. 63.4%; crude prevalence ratio [PR] 1.23, 95% CI 1.08–1.40). It was also higher among households including a person with a disability or chronic illness (80.2% vs. 62.2%; PR 1.29, 1.14–1.46) and among those living in tents or informal sites (82.1% vs. 62.4%; PR 1.31, 1.16–1.49).

Conclusion:

In this sample, housing insecurity in the Gaza Strip was multidimensional, extending beyond physical shelter loss to include repeated displacement, inadequate services, protection risks, economic hardship, documentation loss, and psychosocial distress. The findings also point to two exploratory concepts, housing recovery uncertainty and documentary housing insecurity, as potentially important dimensions of housing insecurity in protracted conflict. Responses should integrate shelter, water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH), health, protection, cash, livelihoods, documentation support, and longer-term rights-based housing recovery.

Introduction

Housing is more than a physical structure; it is a foundation for safety, health, dignity, family life, privacy, social stability, and access to essential services. In humanitarian and human rights frameworks, adequate housing is understood not only as shelter, but also as a set of conditions that include security of tenure, availability of services, affordability, habitability, accessibility, appropriate location, and cultural adequacy (CESCR, 1991; OHCHR and UN-Habitat, 2009). During armed conflict and prolonged displacement, these dimensions are often severely disrupted, transforming housing from a basic social right into a daily struggle for survival.

The Gaza Strip represents one of the most complex and severe housing crises in the contemporary humanitarian context. Even before the war that began in October 2023 (hereafter, “the war”), the Gaza Strip had experienced years of blockade, repeated military escalations, restrictions on movement and construction materials, high population density, poverty, and fragile infrastructure. These structural pressures had already weakened the housing sector and limited the ability of families, municipalities, and institutions to maintain, repair, or expand adequate housing (UNCTAD, 2020; World Bank, 2021). As a result, many households entered the war with limited resilience, overcrowded living conditions, and reduced capacity to absorb further shocks.

The war has intensified this pre-existing vulnerability and produced large-scale destruction of residential buildings, public infrastructure, and community facilities. Reports by humanitarian and development agencies have documented extensive damage to housing units, widespread displacement, and severe pressure on shelters, host families, schools, informal settlements, and other temporary accommodation arrangements OCHA (2024); World Bank et al. (2024). For many families, displacement has not been a single event, but a repeated and unstable process shaped by insecurity, evacuation orders, loss of income, overcrowding, limited access to water and sanitation, and uncertainty about return or reconstruction. In this context, housing insecurity is closely linked to broader humanitarian concerns, including public health risks, protection threats, psychological distress, gender-based vulnerability, and the erosion of social support networks.

Conditions remained severe at the time of data collection in 2026. A ceasefire was announced on 10 October 2025, but insecurity, casualties, and restrictions continued to be reported (OCHA, 2026). The final Rapid Damage and Needs Assessment, published in April 2026, estimated that more than 371,000 housing units had been destroyed or damaged, that around 1.9 million people had been displaced, often more than once, and that over 60% of the population had lost their homes (United Nations et al., 2026). Satellite-based analysis from October 2025 indicated that approximately 81% of all structures in the Gaza Strip had been damaged (UNOSAT, 2025). In June 2026, a Shelter Cluster household assessment found that nearly two-thirds of households were living in tents or makeshift shelters and that more than four in five faced critical or catastrophic living conditions (Palestine Shelter Cluster, 2026), while only about one-third of the more than 1,600 active displacement sites had dedicated site management (OCHA, 2026). Studies conducted in the Gaza Strip since October 2023 have documented inadequate housing, limited access to water and electricity, and severe overcrowding among displaced households (Sabah, 2025; Sabah and Abuzerr, 2025), as well as very high levels of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress symptoms among adults (Zughbur et al., 2025).

The right to adequate housing is particularly relevant in the Gaza Strip because housing loss is not limited to material destruction. The loss of home also affects identity, memory, family continuity, social belonging, and perceptions of future security. A home often functions as a site of emotional attachment, economic investment, intergenerational stability, and social protection. When homes are damaged, destroyed, or abandoned due to conflict, affected populations may experience not only physical displacement but also social and psychological dislocation. Existing literature on forced displacement has shown that the destruction or loss of housing can deepen vulnerability by reducing access to livelihoods, education, healthcare, documentation, community support, and protection mechanisms (Sphere Association, 2018; IDMC, 2024).

Although quantitative data are essential for measuring the scale of housing destruction, displacement, overcrowding, and shelter needs, numbers alone cannot fully capture how displaced people understand and experience the loss of home. Similarly, qualitative accounts provide depth and meaning but may not sufficiently represent the distribution or magnitude of housing insecurity across affected groups. For this reason, a mixed-methods approach is particularly appropriate for studying housing in the Gaza Strip under conditions of war and displacement. By combining quantitative and qualitative methods, researchers can examine both measurable patterns and lived experiences, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of housing insecurity, coping mechanisms, shelter conditions, and perceptions of the right to housing.

Despite the growing body of humanitarian reporting on the Gaza Strip, there remains a need for academic research that systematically examines housing insecurity from both empirical and experiential perspectives. Much of the available evidence focuses on damage assessment, humanitarian needs, or infrastructure loss, while less attention has been given to how displaced households experience housing deprivation, negotiate temporary shelter, maintain family life, and perceive their housing rights during protracted conflict. This gap is important because effective housing recovery and shelter programming require not only technical reconstruction plans, but also an understanding of household priorities, social vulnerabilities, protection concerns, and community-based coping strategies. Earlier mixed-methods work from 2024 examined access to housing and basic services among displaced households (Sabah, 2025); however, less is known about how housing deprivation is experienced after more than 2 years of protracted displacement, and about how severe housing insecurity is distributed across different types of households.

This study therefore aims to explore housing insecurity in the Gaza Strip in light of the war since October 2023 and the resulting protracted displacement, using a mixed-methods design. Specifically, it seeks to assess the conditions of shelter and displacement, examine the social and humanitarian consequences of housing loss, and understand how affected households perceive adequate housing, safety, dignity, and the possibility of return or reconstruction. By integrating statistical patterns with personal narratives, the study aims to contribute to humanitarian policy, housing recovery planning, and rights-based discussions on adequate housing in conflict-affected settings.

Theoretical framework

This study is guided by an integrated theoretical framework based on the right to adequate housing, the multidimensional nature of housing insecurity, and humanitarian perspectives on displacement, shelter adequacy, vulnerability, and housing recovery. Housing insecurity is understood as instability, inadequate living conditions, limited access to essential services, lack of privacy and safety, and uncertainty regarding future housing. The main foundation of the framework is the right to adequate housing. General Comment No. 4 of the United Nations Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights states that adequate housing should be understood as the right to live in security, peace, and dignity (CESCR, 1991).

Adequacy includes security of tenure, availability of services and infrastructure, affordability, habitability, accessibility, appropriate location, and cultural adequacy (CESCR, 1991; OHCHR and UN-Habitat, 2009). This distinction is particularly relevant in conflict settings, where households may have a temporary place to stay while still experiencing overcrowding, inadequate sanitation, poor protection, lack of privacy, or uncertain tenure.

Housing insecurity is increasingly understood as a multidimensional condition that extends beyond the absence of shelter to include poor housing quality, overcrowding, residential instability, inadequate access to basic services, unsafe living environments, and insecure tenure (Dunga and Grobler, 2018; Adams et al., 2020). These conditions may interact with poverty and other vulnerabilities and negatively affect health and wellbeing (Burgard et al., 2012; Pevalin et al., 2017; Ross et al., 2020). Boateng and Adams (2023) further conceptualized housing insecurity through overlapping dimensions related to essential utilities, the built environment, and tenure challenges. This multidimensional perspective is particularly relevant to conflict-affected settings, where housing destruction and repeated displacement may create additional layers of instability, service deprivation, protection risk, and uncertainty about future housing recovery.

Swope and Hernández (2019) describe housing through the dimensions of cost, conditions, consistency, and context. These perspectives are relevant to the Gaza Strip because housing destruction, repeated displacement, poor shelter conditions, service disruption, income loss, and uncertainty about housing rights may affect the same household at the same time.

Repeated displacement is also considered a central dimension of housing insecurity. Displacement may continue through several movements rather than ending after a single relocation. The IASC Framework on Durable Solutions emphasizes that displacement is not resolved simply when movement stops, but requires safety, adequate living conditions, access to services and livelihoods, and restoration of housing and property rights (IASC, 2010). Crisis-driven displacement may also interact with overcrowding, poor shelter conditions, and inadequate infrastructure to increase health and social risks (Deola and Patel, 2014). The Sphere Handbook further emphasizes that shelter should support safety, dignity, privacy, protection from weather, and access to essential services rather than merely provide physical cover (Sphere Association, 2018). These conditions may be especially difficult for households that include older persons, persons with disabilities, people with chronic illness, children, or caregivers.

Housing insecurity also has important health and recovery dimensions. Poor housing, residential instability, overcrowding, inadequate sanitation, and limited accessibility can affect physical and psychological wellbeing (WHO, 2018; Swope and Hernández, 2019; Keene and Blankenship, 2023). In conflict-affected populations, these conditions may combine with poverty, livelihood loss, uncertainty, and repeated movement to increase psychosocial distress (Porter and Haslam, 2005; Miller and Rasmussen, 2010, 2017; Carrere et al., 2022). At the same time, housing insecurity may continue beyond emergency shelter through uncertainty about return, reconstruction, restitution, and tenure. The Pinheiro Principles emphasize housing and property restitution for displaced populations (Pinheiro, 2005), while UNHCR guidance highlights the problems created by loss of ownership, tenancy, identity, or other housing-related documentation during displacement (UNHCR, 2023).

Based on this framework, housing insecurity is understood as a multidimensional condition shaped by housing destruction, repeated displacement, inadequate shelter and services, safety concerns, economic hardship, household vulnerability, and tenure or documentation problems. These factors may interact and accumulate rather than operate independently. The framework guides both components of the mixed-methods study by examining these factors quantitatively while using participants' experiences of safety, privacy, dignity, family life, belonging, and prospects for return or reconstruction to deepen understanding of housing adequacy during prolonged displacement. Housing insecurity in the Gaza Strip is therefore approached as a material, social, humanitarian, health-related, and rights-based condition.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study used a convergent mixed-methods design to examine housing insecurity and displacement among war-affected households in the Gaza Strip. The study was conducted between 2 April and 4 August 2026; survey and interview data were collected within this period, followed by analysis, integration, and interpretation. The exact dates of the first and last interviews were not recorded. Data collection took place after the ceasefire announced on 10 October 2025, during a period of continued insecurity and protracted displacement (OCHA, 2026). Quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently, analyzed separately, and integrated during interpretation. The theoretical framework described above informed the selection of study domains for both components: housing damage, displacement, shelter conditions, overcrowding, access to services, safety and protection, privacy and dignity, household vulnerability, economic disruption, tenure and documentation, and prospects for return or reconstruction. Reporting follows the Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) guideline (O'Cathain et al., 2008), supplemented by the STROBE statement for the survey component (von Elm et al., 2007) and the COREQ checklist for the interviews (Tong et al., 2007); completed checklists are provided in Supplementary File S1.

The study was conducted in the five governorates of the Gaza Strip, with governorate defined as the household's location at the time of the survey. Data collection included households residing in displacement shelters, host family settings, informal tented sites, damaged residential areas (including households living in their original damaged or partially repaired homes, whether never displaced or returned), rented temporary accommodation, and other forms of temporary or unstable shelter. The inclusion of diverse residential settings was intended to capture variation in housing conditions, displacement experiences, access to services, and household vulnerability.

Study population and sampling

The study included adults aged 18 years or older living in the Gaza Strip who had experienced housing insecurity, displacement, housing damage, overcrowding, loss of services, or inability to return home. For the quantitative component, one adult representative from each household was interviewed. A total of 420 households were surveyed using a pragmatic multi-stage sampling approach across displacement sites, host communities, damaged residential areas, and temporary accommodation settings.

Households were eligible if, since October 2023, they had experienced at least one of the following: displacement from their usual residence, damage to their pre-war home, overcrowding, loss of essential services, or inability to return home. Households living in their original homes were therefore eligible if they met at least one of these criteria; 48 households (11.4%) had never been displaced. Because eligibility was defined by exposure to housing-related adversity, all percentages reported in this paper describe the study sample and should not be interpreted as population prevalence estimates for the Gaza Strip.

A pragmatic multi-stage sampling approach was used. First, study sites were purposively selected across the five governorates to represent the main shelter settings (collective shelters, informal tented sites, host-community neighborhoods, damaged residential areas, and rented or other temporary accommodation), taking into account accessibility and security conditions. Second, eligible households within each site were approached and invited to participate. Third, one adult household member (preferably the household head or spouse, or another adult able to report on the household's housing situation) was interviewed. Non-response and household replacement were not systematically recorded during recruitment; it was therefore not possible to calculate a response rate. Because site selection depended partly on access and security, the sample was not designed to be proportional to the population of each governorate; the distribution of households by current shelter type is reported in the Results.

The precision of the achieved sample was calculated after data collection: with 420 households, a proportion of 50% (the value giving the widest confidence interval) can be estimated with a precision of ±4.8% at the 95% confidence level, and the observed prevalence of severe housing insecurity (67.4%) with a precision of ±4.5%; these figures assume simple random sampling, and the effective precision is likely to be lower because of clustering within sites, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

For the qualitative component, purposive sampling was used to capture diverse experiences by gender, age, shelter type, displacement history, household composition, and vulnerability status, aiming for maximum variation. Qualitative data included 34 interviews with displaced or affected household members and 10 key informant interviews with community representatives, shelter workers, service providers, and humanitarian actors. Participants were identified with the support of community representatives and shelter staff, and key informants were selected for their direct knowledge of shelter conditions and humanitarian response.

Data collection

A structured questionnaire was used to collect quantitative data on socio-demographic characteristics, pre-war housing, housing damage, displacement history, current shelter conditions, access to services, protection concerns, coping strategies, and housing priorities. Semi-structured interviews explored lived experiences of housing loss, displacement, privacy, dignity, safety, assistance, and expectations for return or reconstruction. Data were collected in Arabic by trained field researchers under the author's supervision, using face-to-face interviews where possible and remote (telephone) interviews when sites were inaccessible or unsafe. The number of survey interviews conducted face-to-face and by telephone was not recorded; responses could therefore not be compared by interview mode.

tHE questionnaire was developed by the author on the basis of the theoretical framework, the Sphere minimum standards for shelter and for water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH; Sphere Association, 2018), and a previous survey of displaced households in the Gaza Strip (Sabah, 2025), and was adapted to the post-October 2023 context. Field researchers were trained in the questionnaire, informed consent, and trauma-informed, do-no-harm interviewing.

Semi-structured interviews with household members and key informants were conducted in Arabic, either face-to-face in a private space within or near the shelter or by telephone when access was not possible. Quotations selected for publication were translated into English by the author, with attention to preserving their meaning. Interview records were prepared and analyzed in Arabic. Information on interview duration, audio-recording, field notes, repeat interviews, the presence of others during interviews, non-participation in the qualitative component, the return of transcripts or findings to participants, the use of data-management software, and the credentials and gender of individual interviewers, their prior relationships with participants, and what participants were told about them beyond the study purpose was not documented.

Measures and operational definitions

Respondent characteristics (age, sex, and marital status) were measured at the individual level; all other indicators were measured at the household level and reported by the respondent. Housing damage referred to the respondent-reported current condition of the pre-war home, categorized as completely destroyed, severely damaged, partially damaged, or unable to verify. Displacement frequency was the number of times the household had been displaced from one place of residence to another because of the war since October 2023 (never, once, two to three times, or four or more times). Overcrowding, sufficiency of drinking water and of water for hygiene, privacy, and perceived safety were based on the respondent's assessment of the household's current accommodation; perceived shelter insecurity was defined as feeling “unsafe” or “very unsafe.” Sanitation indicators included access to a functional toilet, the number of families sharing it, and access to bathing facilities. Health problems were respondent-reported problems among household members that the respondent associated with current shelter conditions, and psychosocial distress was the respondent's report that at least one household member was experiencing severe stress, fear, sadness, anxiety, or sleep disturbance. These items are not clinical diagnoses or validated screening instruments.

Severe housing insecurity: study-specific operational definition

Severe housing insecurity was defined as a study-specific operational measure; it is not a validated housing insecurity scale. Six binary components were selected to represent the dimensions of adequate housing and multidimensional housing insecurity in the theoretical framework (CESCR, 1991; Swope and Hernández, 2019; Boateng and Adams, 2023): complete or severe damage to the pre-war home (loss of home and habitability); repeated displacement, defined as two or more displacements (residential instability); overcrowding (habitability); lack of adequate sanitation, defined as having no access to a functional toilet or sharing a toilet with more than five families (availability of services); perceived shelter insecurity (security); and lack of privacy, defined as reporting inadequate privacy for family life (dignity and cultural adequacy). In the absence of empirical weights for conflict settings, the components were weighted equally, as in count-based deprivation measures. A household was classified as experiencing severe housing insecurity if it met at least three of the six conditions (i.e., at least half of the dimensions), so as to identify households facing several co-occurring deprivations rather than a single deprivation. This threshold was chosen as a pragmatic cut-off for two reasons. First, at least one deprivation was almost universal in this sample (for example, 88.6% of households had been displaced, 78.1% were overcrowded, and 71.2% reported complete or severe damage to their pre-war home), so a lower threshold would have classified nearly all households as severely insecure and would not have distinguished between them. Second, requiring at least half of the dimensions follows the logic of count-based measures of multidimensional deprivation, in which households are classified as deprived when they experience a specified share of weighted indicators (Alkire and Foster, 2011). A sensitivity analysis using alternative thresholds (at least two or at least four of the six conditions) was not performed; the prevalence of each individual component is therefore reported separately so that readers can judge how the classification depends on the threshold.

Because two of the six components (home damage and repeated displacement) overlap with household exposures of interest, the six-component index is used descriptively only, and associations between household characteristics and severe housing insecurity are reported as crude prevalence ratios; associations involving home damage and displacement are not estimated against this index, as they would be partly determined by its definition.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were cleaned and analyzed using descriptive statistics to summarize household characteristics, displacement patterns, housing damage, shelter conditions, and service access, with counts and denominators reported for all percentages. Differences in the prevalence of severe housing insecurity between household groups were assessed using chi-square tests and expressed as crude prevalence ratios (PRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), which are more directly interpretable than odds ratios for a common outcome. Because home damage and repeated displacement are components of the six-component index, their associations with the index were not estimated. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The number of missing answers per survey item was not recorded; all percentages are therefore reported for the 420 participating households unless another denominator is stated.

Qualitative data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke (2006, 2019). The author familiarized himself with the data through repeated reading of the transcripts, generated initial codes, and developed, reviewed, and defined themes. Coding combined deductive and inductive approaches: an initial set of codes was derived from the dimensions of adequate housing (security of tenure, availability of services, habitability, accessibility, location, and cultural adequacy) and the study domains (displacement, safety and protection, privacy and dignity), and new codes were added as further ideas emerged from participants' accounts. As a result, Themes 3 (shelter without dignity) and 6 (uncertain futures of return and reconstruction) correspond closely to framework dimensions, whereas Themes 1 (loss of home as loss of identity and stability), 2 (displacement as repeated uncertainty), 4 (gendered and caregiving burdens), and 5 (coping under constraint) extend beyond them. Key informant interviews were analyzed with the same codes and used to corroborate and contextualize household accounts. Credibility was supported by maximum-variation sampling and by triangulation across household interviews, key informant interviews, and the survey. The adequacy of the qualitative sample was judged using the concept of information power (Malterud et al., 2016): the study aim was focused, participants were purposively selected for direct experience of displacement and shelter conditions, household and key informant perspectives were combined, and analysis was guided by an established framework, which together supported the adequacy of 44 interviews for the study aims. Quotations are labeled by participant group (HH, household member; KI, key informant). As a Palestinian academic, the author shares the cultural and linguistic background of participants, which facilitated access and rapport but may also have shaped interpretation; this was addressed by grounding themes in participants' own words and comparing them with the survey findings.

Integration followed the convergent design (Fetters et al., 2013). At the design level, the same framework and domains guided the questionnaire and the interview guides. At the interpretation level, after the two datasets had been analyzed separately, each quantitative domain was compared with the corresponding qualitative themes in a joint display, and each pair was classified as convergent (the findings agree), complementary (one strand expands on or explains the other, or addresses an aspect not covered by the other), or divergent (the findings disagree).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University College of Science and Technology Institutional Review Board (UCST IRB/10/2026). Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Given the conflict-affected context, interviews followed a trauma-informed, do-no-harm approach: they were held in a private space or, for remote interviews, at a time when the participant could speak privately; participants could skip questions, pause, or withdraw at any time; and interviewers were prepared to pause or end an interview if a participant became distressed. No unnecessary personal identifiers were collected, participants were assigned codes, and data were anonymized and stored securely with access limited to the research team.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 420 households participated in the quantitative survey. Respondents were recruited from displacement shelters, host-family settings, informal tented areas, damaged residential neighborhoods, and rented temporary accommodation across five governorates of the Gaza Strip. In addition, 34 semi-structured interviews were conducted with displaced household members and 10 key informant interviews were conducted with community representatives, shelter workers, local service providers, and humanitarian actors. The mean age of survey respondents was 39.6 years with a standard deviation of 12.8 years. Women constituted 58.3% of respondents, while men constituted 41.7%. Most respondents were married (76.4%), followed by widowed (9.8%), single (7.4%), divorced (4.0%), and separated (2.4%). Female-headed households represented 27.1% of the sample.

The average household size was 6.7 persons, ranging from 2 to 17 persons. Children under 18 years were present in 84.5% of households, older persons aged 60 years and above were present in 31.2%, and at least one person with a disability or chronic illness was reported in 28.8% of households. Before October 2023, 61.9% of households reported living in owned homes, 25.5% in rented homes, and 12.6% in extended-family or other housing arrangements. Although many respondents described their pre-war housing situation as relatively stable, 39.0% reported that their household had already experienced some form of overcrowding before the war (Table 1).

Extent and patterns of displacement

Overall, 372 of the 420 households (88.6%) had been displaced from their usual residence at least once since October 2023, and 48 (11.4%) had never been displaced. Among displaced households (n = 372), 81 (21.8%) had been displaced once, 166 (44.6%) two to three times, and 125 (33.6%) four or more times. Households described moving repeatedly in response to insecurity, evacuation instructions, destruction of buildings, overcrowding, lack of water, limited sanitation, or inability to remain with host families. At the time of the survey, displaced households had been living at their current location for a median of 7.5 months (interquartile range 5.0–10.0 months).

Among displaced households (n = 372), 72.3% reported that they did not know when they would be able to return to their original homes. A further 64.2% stated that they were uncertain whether their original neighborhood would be habitable even if hostilities stopped. Only 11.5% of displaced households reported having clear information about the condition of their original homes. Qualitative interviews confirmed that displacement was experienced as an ongoing condition rather than a single event. Participants described repeated movement as physically exhausting, emotionally destabilizing, and disruptive to family life. One displaced participant stated:

“We did not leave once and then settle. We moved from place to place. Each time we thought we had found safety, something changed and we had to move again.” (HH, displaced household member)

Housing damage and loss

Reported housing damage was extensive. Among all surveyed households, 42.9% reported that their original home had been completely destroyed, 28.3% reported severe damage, 18.8% reported partial damage, and 10.0% were unable to verify the current condition of their home. No households in this sample reported that their homes were fully intact and safely accessible. Details are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The loss of household assets was also widespread: 81.7% of households reported losing furniture, 74.5% reported losing clothing and personal belongings, 52.6% (221/420) reported losing official or property-related documents, and 37.4% reported losing work tools, equipment, or livelihood assets.

Housing loss was not described only as material destruction. Interview participants repeatedly framed the home as a source of identity, privacy, memory, safety, and family continuity. The destruction or abandonment of home was therefore experienced as a profound emotional and social rupture. One participant explained:

“The house was not only walls and rooms. It was our memories, our children's childhood, our safety, and everything we worked for. When it was gone, we felt that our life before the war had disappeared.” (HH, displaced household member)

At the time of the survey, the most common current shelter types were collective shelters (31.7%), tents or informal sites (25.2%), and host families or relatives (18.6%; Table 2). Shelter conditions were frequently inadequate. The average number of persons per room or enclosed sleeping space was 5.4 persons. Among households living in collective shelters, the average increased to 7.1 persons per room or separated space. Among households living in tents or informal sites (n = 106), 71.7% reported insufficient protection from cold, heat, rain, wind, or dust.

Perceived safety in current shelters was low. Overall, 62.6% of households reported feeling unsafe or very unsafe in their current accommodation. Safety concerns were highest among households living in tents or informal sites (74.5%), followed by damaged or partially repaired homes (69.4%), collective shelters (63.2%), host-family arrangements (48.7%), and rented accommodation (40.0%; Figure 1).

Self-reported health and psychosocial problems

Many households reported health problems among household members that they associated with current shelter conditions. Respiratory symptoms were reported by 230 households (54.8%), skin problems by 177 (42.1%), diarrheal illness by 134 (31.9%), worsening of chronic disease by 120 (28.6%), and injury or mobility-related difficulties by 68 (16.2%). Respondents commonly attributed these problems to overcrowding, poor ventilation, inadequate sanitation, insufficient water, exposure to weather, and limited access to healthcare. Psychosocial distress was also prominent: 308 respondents (73.3%) reported that at least one household member was experiencing severe stress, fear, sadness, anxiety, or sleep disturbance, and child distress was reported by 217 of the 355 households with children (61.0%). Commonly described child-related concerns included nightmares, bedwetting, crying, irritability, withdrawal, fear of loud sounds, and repeated questions about returning home. One participant said:

“The children ask every day when we will go home. I do not know what to say because I do not know if the home still exists.” (HH, displaced household member)

Another participant described the emotional burden of displacement:

“You feel suspended between the past and the future. You are alive, but you do not have stability, privacy, or a normal life.” (HH, displaced household member)

Livelihood disruption and economic barriers to housing recovery

Economic disruption was widespread: 346 households (82.4%) reported that their main source of income had been lost or severely reduced since October 2023, and only 40 (9.5%) had a regular income at the time of data collection. Many households were therefore unable to rent housing, purchase shelter materials, repair damage, or meet basic needs. Among households that had sought rented accommodation, 68.2% reported that rent was unaffordable, 47.6% that available housing was overcrowded or damaged, and 39.7% that they were unable to pay rent in advance. Households that had previously owned homes described a sudden shift from relative housing stability to dependency on aid, relatives, or temporary shelter. Coping strategies were common but often unsustainable: 265 households (63.1%) had borrowed money, 220 (52.4%) reduced food consumption, 175 (41.7%) sold remaining belongings, 152 (36.2%) moved in with relatives, and 119 (28.3%) used improvised shelter materials. One respondent explained:

“We cannot rent, we cannot rebuild, and we cannot return. We are waiting, but waiting itself has become another form of suffering.” (HH, displaced household member)

Tenure, documentation, and future housing claims

Tenure and documentation emerged as important concerns. Overall, 221 of the 420 households (52.6%) reported losing at least one important document, such as ownership papers, rental agreements, identification documents, or municipal records, and a further 102 (24.3%) were unsure whether their documents remained accessible. Concerns about future return, compensation, and reconstruction were common: 290 households (69.0%) were uncertain how housing reconstruction would be organized, and 247 (58.8%) were concerned about proving ownership or tenancy rights. Among the 107 pre-war renters, 78 (72.9%) feared exclusion from future housing support because they did not own property.

Owners and renters both faced insecurity, but in different ways: homeowners described the loss of property, savings, and family inheritance, whereas renters described the loss of stability and fear of being overlooked in recovery planning. One participant asked:

“Even if rebuilding begins, how will people prove what they had? Many papers were lost with the house.” (HH, displaced household member)

Another explained:

“People who owned homes lost everything, but people who rented also lost their stability. Everyone needs a future plan.” (HH, displaced household member)

Humanitarian assistance and unmet shelter needs

Most households had received some humanitarian assistance, but many considered it insufficient or irregular. Overall, 322 households (76.7%) had received food assistance, 244 (58.1%) hygiene items, 188 (44.8%) blankets or mattresses, 132 (31.4%) water-related assistance, 113 (26.9%) shelter materials, and 78 (18.6%) cash assistance. Despite this support, 353 households (84.0%) reported that assistance did not adequately meet their shelter and housing needs. The most frequently reported unmet needs were improved shelter materials (71.2%), mattresses and bedding (64.3%), water and sanitation facilities (62.6%), privacy partitions (59.0%), lighting (53.1%), cooking equipment (49.5%), cash for rent (45.7%), and support for repairing partially damaged homes (38.8%; Supplementary Table S2).

Interview participants generally appreciated humanitarian assistance but viewed it as short-term relief rather than a solution to housing insecurity. A key informant noted:

“Emergency assistance helps people survive, but it does not resolve the housing crisis. Families need safe shelter now and a realistic path toward recovery.” (KI, key informant)

A displaced participant similarly stated:

“Food and water are necessary, but we also need a place where our children can sleep safely and where we can live with dignity.” (HH, displaced household member)

Housing insecurity was not evenly distributed across the sample. Using the six-component descriptive index defined in the Methods, 283 of the 420 households (67.4%) were classified as experiencing severe housing insecurity. Prevalence was also high among households displaced four or more times (84.0%), but this partly reflects the inclusion of repeated displacement in the index.

Qualitative themes

The qualitative analysis generated six themes: loss of home as loss of identity and stability; displacement as repeated uncertainty; shelter without dignity; gendered and caregiving burdens; coping under constraint; and uncertain futures of return and reconstruction. Key informant interviews contributed mainly to Themes 3, 4, and 6, describing the gap between shelter coverage and shelter adequacy, the protection risks created by poorly lit and unorganized sites, and the limits of emergency assistance for housing recovery (see the key informant quotations in the Results above). Their accounts were used to corroborate and contextualize household narratives rather than to generate separate themes.

Theme 1: loss of home as loss of identity and stability

Participants consistently described the home as more than a physical structure: it represented memory, family unity, privacy, security, and social belonging, and its destruction or abandonment was experienced as the collapse of normal life.

“The home carried our memories. Every room had a meaning. Losing it was not only losing property; it was losing a part of ourselves.” (HH, displaced household member)

Theme 2: displacement as repeated uncertainty

Participants described displacement as a continuing process marked by instability and fear, and many families kept their belongings packed because they expected to move again.

“We never fully unpack. We always think we may need to leave again. This feeling destroys your sense of safety.” (HH, displaced household member)

Theme 3: Shelter without dignity

Participants emphasized that emergency shelter did not necessarily provide dignity; lack of privacy, poor sanitation, overcrowding, exposure to weather, and shared facilities made daily life difficult.

“A person needs more than a roof. We need privacy, cleanliness, safety, and a place where children can sleep without fear.” (HH, woman)

Theme 4: gendered and caregiving burdens

Women described specific challenges related to hygiene, menstruation, breastfeeding, childcare, privacy, and safety, while caregivers of older persons and persons with disabilities described additional difficulties with mobility, toileting, medication, and access to health services.

“The shelter is not designed for people who cannot move easily. Everything is far, crowded, and difficult.” (HH, caregiver)

Theme 5: coping under constraint

Households used multiple coping strategies, including sharing crowded spaces, borrowing money, reducing meals, using damaged buildings, relying on relatives, and improvising shelter materials, but described these strategies as temporary and inadequate.

“We survive day by day, but this is not a life. It is only waiting.” (HH, displaced household member)

Theme 6: uncertain futures of return and reconstruction

Participants expressed a strong desire to return home or rebuild but had little confidence about when or how this would happen, citing safety, lack of money, destroyed infrastructure, missing documents, unclear compensation mechanisms, and uncertainty about neighborhood reconstruction.

“We dream of returning, but we do not know if there is anything to return to, or whether we will have the right or ability to rebuild.” (HH, displaced household member)

Discussion

This mixed-methods study examined housing insecurity among war-affected households in the Gaza Strip, focusing on displacement, housing destruction, shelter conditions, access to services, protection risks, health consequences, and prospects for return or reconstruction. The findings show that housing insecurity is multidimensional and extends beyond the absence of a physical dwelling. It includes repeated displacement, overcrowded and unsafe shelter, inadequate water and sanitation, loss of privacy, livelihood disruption, documentation loss, psychosocial distress, and uncertainty about the future. This aligns with rights-based definitions of adequate housing, which include tenure security, services, affordability, habitability, accessibility, location, and cultural adequacy (CESCR, 1991; OHCHR and UN-Habitat, 2009).

The multidimensional pattern identified in this study is consistent with Boateng and Adams (2023), who conceptualized housing insecurity through overlapping dimensions of essential utilities, built-environment deficiencies, and tenancy challenges. However, the Gaza context suggests that housing insecurity under protracted conflict may involve additional dimensions, particularly housing destruction, repeated displacement, protection concerns, documentation loss, and uncertainty regarding return and reconstruction. These findings indicate that existing multidimensional housing-insecurity frameworks may require adaptation when applied to conflict-affected and repeatedly displaced populations.

A key finding was the scale and repeated nature of displacement. Nearly 9 in 10 sampled households had been displaced at least once, and one-third of displaced households had moved four or more times. Because households were recruited on the basis of housing-related adversity, these figures describe the study sample rather than the population of the Gaza Strip. This suggests that displacement is not a single event but an ongoing process of instability. Repeated movement can disrupt family life, weaken social support, increase exposure to unsafe environments, and limit access to services. Humanitarian guidance similarly recognizes that repeated displacement produces cumulative vulnerability, especially when families move without adequate protection, information, or support (IASC, 2010, 2015). Qualitative findings reinforced this, as participants described remaining prepared to move again and being unable to regain stability.

The high level of housing destruction highlights the central role of home loss in the crisis. More than two-thirds of households reported that their homes were completely destroyed or severely damaged. Housing loss affects not only shelter, but also safety, identity, privacy, family continuity, savings, and future recovery. This supports literature showing that home is both a material and social foundation for dignity and resilience (OHCHR and UN-Habitat, 2009; Rolnik, 2013). In conflict settings, housing destruction may also create long-term barriers to return, reconstruction, compensation, and restitution, particularly when documentation is lost or governance systems are disrupted (Pinheiro, 2005; Leckie, 2007).

Current shelter conditions were inadequate for many households. Collective shelters, tents, informal sites, damaged homes, and overcrowded host-family arrangements often provided only temporary refuge and failed to meet minimum standards for adequate shelter. The Sphere Handbook emphasizes that shelter assistance should support safety, dignity, privacy, protection from climate, access to services, and family life, rather than simply providing a covered space (Sphere Association, 2018). The findings show that many households had somewhere to sleep but lacked privacy, sanitation, ventilation, secure cooking arrangements, lighting, and protection from weather.

Overcrowding was one of the most frequently reported deprivations. More than three-quarters of households reported overcrowded conditions, often sharing sleeping areas with other families. Participants associated overcrowding with poor sleep, family tension, reduced privacy, and loss of dignity. These findings are consistent with evidence connecting overcrowding to infectious disease transmission, respiratory symptoms, stress, and reduced mental wellbeing (WHO, 2018). In humanitarian settings, overcrowding can also increase protection risks for women, children, older persons, and persons with disabilities (IASC, 2015; Sphere Association, 2018).

Limited access to water, sanitation, electricity, cooking facilities, and waste disposal was common and co-occurred with other forms of housing deprivation. Shelter support alone is insufficient when households lack safe water, toilets, bathing spaces, lighting, drainage, and waste management. Sphere standards emphasize that WASH services are essential for health, dignity, and protection (Sphere Association, 2018). Qualitative accounts, particularly from women and caregivers, showed that sanitation was one of the most difficult aspects of displacement due to long queues, lack of privacy, poor lighting, and fear at night.

The findings are also consistent with an association between housing conditions and health. Households reported respiratory symptoms, skin conditions, diarrheal illness, worsening chronic disease, poor sleep, and psychological distress. Respondents attributed these problems to overcrowding, poor ventilation, inadequate sanitation, insufficient water, and exposure to weather. WHO housing guidelines emphasize that housing conditions influence communicable disease, injury risks, thermal comfort, mental health, and chronic disease outcomes (WHO, 2018). Because health outcomes were self-reported at household level and measured at a single point in time, these findings reflect perceived associations rather than causal effects. The psychosocial burden was also substantial, with many households reporting severe stress, anxiety, sadness, fear, or sleep disturbance. This is consistent with evidence that displacement, home loss, violence, and uncertainty contribute to psychological distress and family strain (Porter and Haslam, 2005; Miller and Rasmussen, 2010, 2017; Zughbur et al., 2025).

Housing insecurity was also experienced as a loss of dignity. Participants emphasized that a roof alone was insufficient without privacy, cleanliness, safety, and the ability to care for family members. Dignity is central to humanitarian action and is embedded in major standards, including the Humanitarian Charter and Core Humanitarian Standard (CHS Alliance, 2014; Sphere Association, 2018). Practical measures such as privacy partitions, gender-sensitive sanitation, safe lighting, accessible toilets, and family-level space planning can therefore have significant effects on dignity, safety, and wellbeing.

The study suggests that housing insecurity is unevenly distributed. Severe housing insecurity was more common among female-headed households, households with persons with disabilities or chronic illness, households with older persons, repeatedly displaced households, and those living in tents or informal sites. These patterns suggest that vulnerability may be shaped not only by exposure to crisis but also by pre-existing and crisis-generated inequalities (IASC, 2010, 2015). This supports the need for targeted assistance based on vulnerability analysis rather than uniform distribution alone. This uneven distribution is a key finding: although housing damage and displacement were almost universal in the sample, the prevalence of severe housing insecurity was approximately 17–31% higher among female-headed households, households including a person with a disability or chronic illness, households with older persons, and households living in tents or informal sites than among the corresponding comparison groups (crude PRs 1.17–1.31). Because the study is cross-sectional and these estimates are unadjusted, they do not establish causal relationships, and the differences may partly reflect overlap between these characteristics, for example between female headship, income loss, and shelter type. Nevertheless, these characteristics are programmatically relevant because they may help identify households at greatest risk and guide prioritization for shelter upgrades, rental support, cash assistance, accessibility modifications, WASH improvements, and protection follow-up.

Livelihood loss was widespread and frequently co-occurred with housing insecurity. Most households had lost or reduced their main income source, limiting their ability to rent accommodation, repair homes, purchase materials, or meet basic needs. This highlights the relationship between housing and economic security. Shelter strategies should therefore be linked with cash assistance, livelihoods, market monitoring, and early recovery planning (UNHCR, 2023).

Tenure insecurity and documentation loss were also important. Many households had lost documents and feared being unable to prove ownership, tenancy, or eligibility for assistance. Housing recovery after conflict involves not only rebuilding structures but also addressing rights, claims, land records, compensation, inheritance, rental status, and inclusion of informal occupants. The Pinheiro Principles emphasize the right of displaced persons to housing and property restitution and the need for accessible claims procedures (Pinheiro, 2005). Recovery policies should therefore include owners, renters, informal occupants, female-headed households, and those who lost documentation.

The findings also suggest two exploratory concepts for understanding housing insecurity in protracted conflict. Housing recovery uncertainty refers to prolonged uncertainty about return, repair, reconstruction, compensation, and future housing stability. Documentary housing insecurity refers to housing vulnerability resulting from the loss or inaccessibility of documents needed to establish ownership, tenancy, occupancy, or eligibility for housing-related support. Together, these concepts highlight that housing insecurity may persist beyond immediate shelter conditions and extend into the prospects and mechanisms of long-term housing recovery. These concepts are proposed tentatively, as they rest on a small number of descriptive survey items and on qualitative accounts, and they extend rather than replace existing ideas. Housing recovery uncertainty relates to the “consistency” dimension of housing (Swope and Hernández, 2019), to residential instability in multidimensional measures of housing insecurity (Cox et al., 2019; Boateng and Adams, 2023), and to the notion of home as a source of ontological security, that is, a sense of continuity and constancy that displacement disrupts (Dupuis and Thorns, 1998). Documentary housing insecurity relates to perceived and legal tenure security (van Gelder, 2010) and to the housing, land, and property restitution literature, which identifies documentation loss as a barrier to claims (Pinheiro, 2005; Leckie, 2007; UNHCR, 2023). Future research should develop and validate measures of both concepts.

Although humanitarian assistance was widely reported, it was perceived as insufficient for shelter needs. Emergency support may reduce immediate suffering but cannot fully address large-scale housing destruction, service collapse, market disruption, and prolonged displacement. A phased approach is needed, combining immediate shelter support, shelter upgrades, rental or hosting assistance, WASH improvements, protection-sensitive site planning, documentation support, technical repair assistance, and long-term reconstruction.

The mixed-methods design strengthened the interpretation of findings. Quantitative data measured displacement, damage, overcrowding, service access, and vulnerability, while qualitative data explained how these conditions affected daily life. This integration showed that numerical indicators alone may not capture dignity, emotional loss, social relationships, and perceived future insecurity (Plano Clark, 2017).

Several programmatic implications emerge. Shelter response should assess habitability, safety, privacy, accessibility, and service availability, not only shelter coverage. It should be integrated with WASH, health, protection, mental health and psychosocial support, cash assistance, and livelihoods. Vulnerability-based targeting should prioritize households facing combined risks, including home destruction, repeated displacement, disability, chronic illness, female-headed household status, large household size, and residence in informal shelters. Documentation and tenure support should begin early, and affected communities should be involved in planning to ensure culturally appropriate, gender-sensitive, and accountable responses (CHS Alliance, 2014; Sphere Association, 2018).

The findings also reinforce the relevance of the right to adequate housing in conflict settings. In this study, nearly all dimensions of adequate housing were compromised, including tenure security, services, habitability, accessibility, affordability, cultural adequacy, and dignity (CESCR, 1991). Housing insecurity should therefore be understood not only as a humanitarian shelter problem but also as a rights-based recovery challenge.

Future research should use longitudinal designs to examine how housing insecurity changes over time and affects health, livelihoods, family structure, and recovery. Further studies should focus on renters, informal occupants, female-headed households, persons with disabilities, older persons, and households that lost documentation. Research is also needed on the effectiveness of shelter interventions such as cash-for-rent, host-family support, repair kits, transitional shelter, accessibility modifications, WASH upgrades, and community-based site planning. Finally, affected communities' own definitions of dignified and adequate housing should guide future programming and policy.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including the collection of primary data under extremely difficult conditions, the inclusion of diverse shelter settings across the Gaza Strip, and the integration of survey and interview data. It also has important limitations, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.

First, the pragmatic multi-stage sample was not fully probability-based, and sites were selected partly on the basis of accessibility and security. Households in inaccessible or unsafe areas, and those with little contact with site managers or community networks, may be under-represented, whereas households facing more severe conditions in displacement sites may be over-represented. Moreover, eligibility required exposure to housing-related adversity, so the sample was selected partly on the outcome. Percentages therefore describe the study sample and should not be generalized as population prevalence estimates. No formal sample size calculation was performed; the precision estimates were calculated after data collection, assume simple random sampling, and do not include a design effect, so the actual precision may be lower. In addition, non-response and household replacement were not recorded, so the extent of non-response bias cannot be assessed.

Second, the cross-sectional design does not allow temporal or causal relationships to be established. Associations between household characteristics, shelter conditions, and health outcomes should be interpreted accordingly; because these associations were not adjusted for other factors, confounding, including by unmeasured factors such as pre-war socioeconomic status or exposure to violence, is likely. Third, all measures were self-reported and may be affected by recall bias, particularly for displacement history and pre-war conditions, and by reporting bias, for example if respondents expected their answers to influence assistance. Health and psychosocial indicators were household-level, respondent-reported items rather than clinical assessments or validated screening tools. Fourth, some interviews were conducted remotely, and differences in rapport, privacy, and disclosure between remote and face-to-face interviews may have affected responses. Because the number of remote interviews was not recorded, this could not be examined.

Fifth, severe housing insecurity was measured using a study-specific index with equally weighted components and a threshold of at least three conditions. The index has not been externally validated, and other weightings or thresholds could classify households differently. A sensitivity analysis using alternative thresholds was not performed. Because two components of the index (home damage and repeated displacement) overlap with household exposures of interest, associations involving these components were not estimated, and no multivariable analysis was performed. Finally, the qualitative findings reflect the perspectives of the participants interviewed and the interpretive position of the researchers, and some nuances of meaning may have been lost in translating quotations from Arabic. In addition, the qualitative data were coded by a single analyst, without independent double-coding, which may have increased the influence of the author's interpretation on the themes.

Conclusion

This study suggests that housing insecurity among war-affected households in the Gaza Strip is a complex condition involving physical destruction, displacement, inadequate shelter, service deprivation, protection risks, economic hardship, documentation loss, and psychosocial distress. The findings emphasize that emergency shelter provision alone is insufficient when families lack privacy, safety, sanitation, tenure security, and realistic prospects for return or reconstruction. A comprehensive response should combine immediate shelter support with WASH, protection, health, mental health, cash, livelihoods, documentation assistance, and long-term rights-based housing recovery. The findings further suggest housing recovery uncertainty and documentary housing insecurity as potentially important dimensions of housing insecurity in protracted conflict that warrant further empirical investigation and conceptual development. The findings also support the future development and validation of a conflict-adapted multidimensional housing insecurity framework or scale that incorporates conflict-specific dimensions such as housing destruction, repeated displacement, protection risks, documentation loss, and uncertainty regarding housing recovery. Above all, housing interventions should be designed not only to keep people alive, but to restore safety, dignity, family life, and hope for the future.
