University of Dar es Salaam, Dar es Salaam, Tanzania See more Article metrics View details Abstract Introduction: Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) contains highly diverse aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems but remains underrepresented in biodiversity research. Environmental DNA (eDNA) offers a non-invasive approach for biodiversity assessment, yet its application across SSA has not been comprehensively synthesized. Methods: A systematic review following PRISMA 2020 was conducted using Google Scholar and Scopus for studies published from January 2018 to April 2026. Eligible peer-reviewed English-language studies used eDNA or DNA metabarcoding to assess aquatic or terrestrial biodiversity in SSA. Data were extracted on geographic distribution, ecosystems, target taxa, markers, sequencing platforms, sampling approaches, reference databases, and reported constraints. Results: Twenty-five studies met the inclusion criteria. Aquatic ecosystems accounted for 88% of studies, while fish were the dominant target taxon (48%). Southern Africa had the largest geographic representation. The most frequently used marker was 12S rRNA (52%), and Illumina was the dominant sequencing platform (72%). Incomplete reference databases were reported as a major limitation in 68% of studies, while terrestrial and arthropod-focused applications were markedly underrepresented. Discussion: eDNA has demonstrated substantial potential for biodiversity monitoring in SSA, particularly in aquatic systems. However, incomplete reference libraries, methodological heterogeneity, logistical constraints, contamination risks, limited infrastructure, and capacity gaps constrain broader application. Future work should strengthen African reference databases, standardize methods, expand terrestrial and arthropod applications, and support SSA-led capacity development. 1 Introduction Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is recognized as one of the final strongholds of global biodiversity (Chapman et al., 2022). Approximately 34 global biodiversity hotspots are located in eight regions of SSA, including Eastern Afromontane, the Coastal Forests of Eastern Africa, Guinean Forests of West Africa, and Succulent Karoo. SSA’s freshwater ecosystems alone support more than 3,000 fish species, including the iconic cichlid radiations of the African Great Lakes (Lake Victoria, Tanganyika, Malawi), which represent the most diverse vertebrate adaptive radiations on Earth (Santos et al., 2023). Terrestrial ecosystems harbour exceptional megafauna, endemic plant communities, and complex invertebrate assemblages, yet a large portion of these systems remain undescribed (Landmann et al., 2023). This biotic treasure is collapsing at an extraordinary rate. The 2019 Global Assessment Report by IPBES found that SSA has lost about 50% of its wetlands since 1900, and the populations of freshwater vertebrates have crashed by 83% between 1970 and 2016. This also represents the largest percentage loss among continents (Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES), 2019). The collective drivers for declines include overfishing, dam construction, agricultural intensification, mining, climate change, and invasive species (Bauknecht et al., 2025). The Rufiji River catchment ecosystem in Tanzania, sampled under this review, shows a supposed classical simplification: the power of Julius Nyerere Hydropower Station blocks migratory pathways for threatened anguillid eels, while expanding agricultural land use is fragmenting critical fisheries (Shechonge et al., 2024). Effective biodiversity conservation depends on accurate knowledge of species identity (taxonomic deficit), their spatial distribution (geographic deficit), and changes in their population dynamics over time (ecological snapshot deficit). However, sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) continues to experience substantial knowledge gaps across all three dimensions (Shechonge et al., 2024). Concerning freshwater fish, roughly 30–40% of species in most SSA catchments have been formally described or accurately inventoried (Message et al., 2026). For invertebrate taxa, the situation is worse, as less than 5% of insects on the continents are known (Christian et al., 2021). Apart from being labour-intensive and requiring higher taxonomic expertise, traditional biodiversity assessment methods, including sweep netting, gillnetting, trawling, electrofishing, visual transect studies, and pitfall trapping, are destructive (Ruppert et al., 2019). The challenge is exacerbated in SSA by limited infrastructure, security constraints, vast geographic scale, and a severe shortage of trained taxonomists (Heyden, 2023). Museum collections in the region are underfunded, and most specimen types are maintained by North American or European institutions, fostering knowledge gaps stemming from colonial times (Christian et al., 2021). Thus, the absence of baseline information on biodiversity hinders evidence-based conservation planning, impact assessment, and sustainable development in the SSA region. Environmental DNA (eDNA) analysis is a cutting-edge technology that has been utilized in a variety of ways to examine and understand biodiversity (Nørgaard et al., 2021; Zinger et al., 2025). Organisms shed DNA in the environment via their skin, scales, gills, mucus, faeces, urine, sexual fluids, gametes, and dead tissues. This genetic material can be retrieved from the environment by filtering water or soil samples, collecting leaf or flower surfaces and sediments, and identifying species present by amplifying and sequencing DNA using standard or species-specific primers (Ruppert et al., 2019). The eDNA methodology is rapidly gaining traction as a new tool for biodiversity assessment worldwide. This is mainly due to its ability to detect biodiversity from samples with unprecedented accuracy and speed (Beng and Corlett, 2020). According to Beng and Corlett (2020), eDNA has been successfully applied to detect freshwater fish, amphibians, marine mammals, and terrestrial vertebrates (Mynhardt et al., 2024). It has also been used to study insects (Deiner et al., 2017) and parasites (Sengupta et al., 2019). Additionally, eDNA technology provides multiple benefits over conventional methods. It is less invasive, more sensitive, and less expensive than traditional biodiversity surveys (Ruppert et al., 2019). Additionally, a single eDNA sample can yield biodiversity data about the species present in a certain area. Earlier systematic reviews of eDNA metabarcoding’s application to biodiversity monitoring at both global and regional levels identified eDNA as valuable for detecting biodiversity at various scales (Ruppert et al., 2019). The authors conducted a systematic review of the literature and produced numerous insights on eDNA’s relevance, application, and emerging trends. Similar to Ruppert et al. (2019), later reviews concentrated on eDNA’s potential to detect rare and elusive species, unlocking fresh biodiversity insights. Most of these reviews, however, were written from a global viewpoint, potentially overlooking critical regional variations in eDNA research and practice. For instance, von der Heyden (2023) evaluated eDNA surveys in Africa, noting key constraints and opportunities in adopting the technology on the continent. However, a more comprehensive systematic review of eDNA practice in SSA is required to respond to the research gaps identified in the works of von der Heyden (2023); Ruppert et al. (2019), and Beng and Corlett (2020). The systematic review will aid in identifying where eDNA has been utilized to examine aquatic and terrestrial biodiversity in SSA, as well as the methods and experiences. This review will also highlight the research and funding priorities based on the evidence. This is critical to achieving the goals of evidence-based policymaking and sustainable development in general, as well as effective conservation of the region’s rich biodiversity. Although eDNA has been presented as a major paradigm for characterizing and monitoring biodiversity, the evidence base for its use in SSA is disjointed and inadequate at this point. Heyden (2023) reported only 10 eDNA-related publications from SSA, compared with hundreds in Europe, America, and Asia at the time of study. Thus, there is a research gap that needs attention. However, the number of eDNA studies conducted across SSA countries tells only part of the story. There is currently no review that synthesizes what is known about the use of eDNA in the region or highlights research priorities based on the evidence. It remains unknown whether the evidence base is adequate to enable informed decision-making about the application of eDNA technology to monitor and manage the region’s biodiversity and natural resources. Equally relevant is the question of whether eDNA has been applied to study aquatic and other biodiversity in Africa, and, if so, what methods and experiences have been reported. Evidence on species detected using eDNA could also provide vital information about the state of biodiversity across SSA countries, since most of the region’s species have never been assessed or monitored using this groundbreaking technology. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to review the literature on the application of environmental DNA (eDNA) for biodiversity assessment across sub-Saharan African countries. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) characterize the extent and type of taxa studied across the region and identify geographic, ecosystem, and taxonomic biases; (2) describe and compare methodological approaches and their impact on detection; and (3) determine challenges and limitations regarding the application of eDNA for biodiversity assessment across sub-Saharan African countries. This review enhances the current understanding by providing a conceptual and empirical synthesis of the development of environmental DNA (eDNA) for biodiversity assessment across sub-Saharan African countries. Unlike reports that highlight the use of eDNA for specific taxa or at particular sites, this review collates the existing evidence to illustrate overarching themes, which would not be apparent otherwise. It provides the distribution of studies both geographically and taxonomically, the implications of different methodological approaches on detection and taxonomic resolution, highlights common technical, infrastructural, reference-database, logistical, and capacity challenges, and fills critical research evidence gaps. In particular, the review demonstrates the domination of aquatic studies over terrestrial arthropods and the lack of studies specifically targeting agricultural pests and vectors despite their immense significance to the region. By collating this information, the review offers a baseline understanding of the state of eDNA research across SSA countries and an evidence-based foundation for identifying future research needs. In addition, it helps identify research priorities, such as filling reference-barcoding gaps, enhancing methodological capacity, improving standardization and accessibility of data, and advancing terrestrial eDNA methods for biodiversity assessment and agricultural pest and vector surveillance in the region. 2 Methodology 2.1 Search strategy The systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines (Haddaway et al., 2017) to synthesize the literature on the use of eDNA methods for monitoring aquatic and terrestrial biodiversity across sub-Saharan African countries, covering the period from January 2018 to April 2026, and addressed four primary research questions: How is the taxonomic and geographic scope of eDNA biodiversity research in SSA distributed? What methodological approaches (markers, primers, sequencing platforms, sampling designs) have been used, and how have they affected detection outcomes? What are the main constraints limiting eDNA adoption across sub-Saharan African countries, and how have the studies addressed (or failed to address) reference database deficiencies, contamination risks, and logistical challenges? What recommendations are there for the integration of eDNA into biodiversity surveillance and conservation across sub-Saharan African countries? The study used the Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) framework to refine search criteria for identifying relevant studies. Population (P): biodiversity in sub-Saharan Africa; Intervention (I): environmental DNA (eDNA) in biodiversity monitoring; Comparator (C): not applicable, but comparisons were coded wherever a traditional method existed; Outcome (O): biodiversity measures, detection efficacy, and ecological information. Google Scholar and Scopus databases were chosen because of their extensive collections of peer-reviewed articles and accessibility through the Nelson Mandela African Institution of Science and Technology. 2.2 Inclusion and exclusion criteria This study included studies that utilized environmental DNA (eDNA) or DNA metabarcoding techniques or a combination of eDNA and traditional methods for the assessment of biodiversity; studies that were carried out across Sub-Saharan African countries for biodiversity assessment, which gave information about biodiversity evaluations and/or methodological factors that affect biodiversity monitoring; the studies that was done in terrestrial or aquatic environment, and studies that were in English and published as peer-reviewed articles. In regard to data availability, the articles analyzed were selected irrespective of whether the raw data (i.e., the sequencing reads) were publicly deposited or not (e.g., in the form of NCBI SRA, GenBank, or BOLD database entries). Nevertheless, this information was evaluated, and the data availability status was specified (see the results section, 3.9). The review excluded studies that addressed systems unrelated to aquatic or terrestrial biodiversity assessment/monitoring, did not include taxa associated with aquatic or terrestrial ecosystems, were published in a language other than English, did not include countries found in SSA, were reviewed without original data, or were not published between January 2018 and April 2026 (Table 2). There were zero arthropod-dedicated studies, meaning no study targeted insects (pests, pollinators, natural enemies), arachnids, or soil arthropods (Figure 6). Bálint et al. (2024) detected some arthropod ASVs in Lake Albert water, but these were just accidental detections (not intentional) and were not examined in an ecological way. 3.3 Methodological approaches For the sampling design, water was the main matrix (92%). Average water volume was 500 mL (range 50–1,500 mL). Shechonge et al. (2024) reported no relationship between filtered volume and species richness (P = 0.230). Median field replicates were 3 (range 1–6). Average PCR replicates: 3 (range 2–12). Negative controls were mentioned in 88% of studies. Seasonal replication is reported in only 16% (4/25) of studies (Keleman et al., 2025; Nneji et al., 2025; Burian et al., 2023, 2023; Courtaillac et al., 2024). 12S rRNA was the most frequently used marker (52%, 13 studies), often with MiFish (Thekiso et al., 2023) or Teleo (Schneider et al., 2016) primers. COI primers were used in 16% (4 studies). 16S appears in 8% (2 studies, Okello et al., 2025; Wambui et al., 2025). 18S was used in 1 study (Bálint et al., 2024). ITS was applied in 2 studies (Kupagme et al., 2026; Rossouw et al., 2024). Multi-marker strategies were uncommon (16%, 4 studies: Keleman et al., 2025; Bálint et al., 2024; de la Hoz Schilling et al., 2024; Rossouw et al., 2024) (Figure 7). Figure 7 Figure showing marker genes used in Sub-Saharan Africa eDNA studies. 3.4 Sequencing platforms Seventy-two per cent of the studies used Illumina for Sequencing (Figure 8). Oxford Nanopore MinION shows up in only 1 study (Nneji et al., 2025), and they described its portability, low cost of around $1,000, plus real-time sequencing, as practical benefits for resource-limited SSA contexts. Figure 8 Figure showing the sequencing platforms used in Sub-Saharan Africa eDNA studies. 3.5 Limitation in reference database Sixty-eight per cent of the identified studies said reference libraries were incomplete, which then constrained taxonomic reporting. Shechonge et al. (2024) found that 29 out of 91 expected fish species had no 12S references. Bálint et al. (2024) reported that 34% of the 18S ASVs and 67% of the COI ASVs could not be assigned past the super kingdom level. Mashaphu and his colleagues observed that 23% of South African fish were missing COI barcodes, while 41% lacked 12S barcodes (Mashaphu et al., 2024a). Nneji et al. (2025) stated that no comprehensive reference database exists for Nigerian fish species. Bauknecht et al. (2025) had to lean on MOTUs instead of species-level identifications (Bauknecht et al., 2025). 3.6 Biodiversity outcomes and detection success Shechonge et al. (2024) reported 66 fish species in the Kilombero River (50 samples, in 2 km per point). One sample held 23.1 species (41% of 56 total), while three samples captured 39.4 species (70%). Nneji et al. (2025) listed 25 taxa from Lagos Lagoon, 22 from Jebba Dam, and 8 from Asa River. de la Hoz Schilling et al. (2024) counted 27 elasmobranchs, with 67.9% categorized as IUCN threatened. Keleman et al. (2025) detected 49 taxa; the leading organisms were Coptodon guineensis (32.1% reads), Sarotherodon melanotheron (27.6%), and Pseudotolithus elongatus (20.6%). 3.7 Comparison with traditional methods Nneji et al. (2025) reported that eDNA detected 10 species that gillnetting missed in Lagos Lagoon; by contrast, traditional surveys recovered 108 species not observed in the eDNA, so eDNA should be used as a complement, not a replacement. Doble et al. (2020) observed a similar pattern in Lake Tanganyika (Doble et al., 2020). Shechonge et al. (2024) estimated that 93% of species could be detected when reference sequences were available, but they could not detect species without those references. 3.8 Reported constraints Gaps in the reference database (68%): This is the most severe constraint, already described earlier, but in brief, it basically leaves limited coverage. Logistical challenges (60%): Shechonge et al. (2024) mentioned that remote field sites needed a lot of coordination. Nneji et al. (2025) pointed to “security concerns” in Nigeria. Keleman et al. (2025) had to navigate tidal channels inside mangroves. There were also recurring problems with cold chain handling and filtration getting clogged. In fact, Shechonge et al. (2024) reported filtering only 202 mL on average because clogging kept disrupting the workflow. Contamination risks (32%): again. de la Hoz Schilling et al. (2024) found signs of contamination in extraction blanks for some species. For example, in blanks, it accounted for more than 90% of reads for Prionace glauca, so those species were excluded from downstream analyses. von der Heyden et al. (2023) showed best practices, where every negative control produced no amplification. Capacity gaps: about 76% of the studies had co-authors that were non-SSA; only 20% were led by SSA-based first authors (Shechonge-Tanzania; Nneji-Nigeria; Okello-Kenya; Kalalizi-DRC; Seemani-Zambia). Mashaphu et al. (2024b) also pointed out “limited local capacity for DNA barcoding.” Cost barriers: Nneji et al. (2025) chose MinION on purpose because it reduced expenses, and Shechonge et al. (2024) plus again. de la Hoz Schilling et al. (2024) admitted that outside financing was what enabled the work. 3.9 Data accessibility Raw sequence data were placed in public repositories in 72% (18/25). Code was uploaded in 32% (8/25). Shechonge et al. (2024) provided a strong example of good practice: NCBI SRA (PRJNA1155635), GenBank barcodes (PQ268652-PQ268849), GitHub code, and Zenodo data. Bálint et al. (2024) put their materials on FigShare. Bauknecht et al. (2025) deposited the dataset on Zenodo. 4 Discussion Southern Africa, and especially South Africa, with six studies, ends up leading the literature (48% of all studies). Meanwhile, Central Africa has almost zero eDNA studies, except for the two studies done in the DRC for pathogen assessment. The Congo Basin, which is the world’s second-largest rainforest and also a recognized biodiversity hotspot, shows zero published eDNA biodiversity studies as of April 2026. This result fits a larger theme seen in African biodiversity work, where older colonial-era research routes and the way post-independence research money was later allocated have tended to benefit South Africa, and, to a lesser extent, East Africa (Fry, 2023). Shechonge et al. (2024) even said that their work in Tanzania’s Rufiji catchment could only move forward because of international backing (Royal Society-Leverhulme Africa Awards). That detail really underscores how much SSA eDNA research depends on external support. There is a strong taxonomic bias, as 48% of all studies rely on fish. Bálint et al. (2024) successfully detected 4,527 eukaryotic ASVs from Lake Albert using 18S metabarcoding. Yet, their work still gave dominance to metazoans over other groups. Shechonge et al. (2024) detected 66 fish species from Tanzania’s Rufiji River, and they accounted for 73% of the estimated total, but they explicitly noted that six species that were present in their reference library but absent from their eDNA reads may be discovered with broader geographic sampling. This fish-centred focus is not accidental. The MiFish 12S primers (Thekiso et al., 2023) that Shechonge et al. (2024); Nneji et al. (2025), and Heyden et al. (2023) used are specifically tuned for teleost fishes, and the Teleo primers used by Bálint et al. (2024) and Bauknecht et al. (2025) also lean toward vertebrates. In practice, the ease of use of fish-targeted primers has narrowed the taxonomic range of SSA eDNA research. The almost complete lack of arthropod-centred studies is really concerning, especially since insects make up more than 80% of the undescribed animal species in SSA (Christian et al., 2021). Bálint et al. (2024) incidentally picked up arthropod DNA in Lake Albert water samples, which most likely reflects aquatic insects or terrestrial insects that were washed into the lake; however, no work in this review clearly aimed at arthropods. This mismatch is not really a limitation of eDNA technology itself, since broad efforts have already used COI metabarcoding to monitor pollinators (Thomsen and Sigsgaard, 2019), trace agricultural pests (Valentin et al., 2020; Kestel et al 2022), and also survey soil arthropods (Heyde et al., 2023). So, the shortage of studies in SSA points to a prioritization gap for research rather than a technical impossibility, or maybe an inability. 4.1 eDNA transition from aquatic to terrestrial environments Eighty-eight per cent of SSA eDNA studies centered on aquatic ecosystems, a skew that is three times more intense than what you typically see in the global average (around 70% aquatic overall). This kind of imbalance makes sense methodologically because water samples basically gather eDNA from aquatic life and can be processed with good efficiency. Terrestrial eDNA, though, runs into problems, like soil spatial patchiness, humic acid inhibition, plus relic DNA interference (Carini et al., 2016). Shechonge et al. (2024) worked with just an average of 202 mL per sample, and still they reported 66 fish species, so in practice, even a small water volume can work, provided replication is handled properly. Likewise, Nneji et al. (2025) managed to filter water samples from Nigerian lagoons and dams, even though turbidity was difficult. Still, the methodological convenience of aquatic eDNA should not set the research agenda. Terrestrial ecosystems take up 78% of SSA land area, feed most of SSA’s 1.1 billion people via agriculture, and are experiencing swift land use change. The three terrestrial studies in this review show both the benefits and the limitations of the work being done today. Okello et al. (2025) used soil eDNA to monitor plant diversity and prokaryote diversity in restoration sites around Kenya, and they confirmed that terrestrial eDNA indeed works in SSA conditions. Rossouw et al. (2024) picked up kelp-forest-associated metazoans, with some terrestrial arthropods among them, but the main focus still stayed marine. Kalalizi et al. (2026) applied eDNA to detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis at the human-great-ape interface in DRC, so that was more about pathogen detection rather than a broad biodiversity evaluation. Studying agricultural arthropods, despite their high economic impact, has been strangely overlooked, and that is a major missed chance. Shechonge et al. (2024) showed that eDNA can pick up fine-scale spatial structuring in fish communities across environmental gradients, like elevation, temperature and turbidity. In the same direction, that method could be used for arthropod assemblages running across agricultural intensification gradients, looking for tipping points where beneficial insects such as pollinators, natural enemies, and decomposers start to drop and where pest populations hit outbreak conditions. Nneji et al. (2025) also detected invasive Nile perch (Lates niloticus) in Nigerian waters, which basically proves eDNA can follow biological invasions; likewise, eDNA could help monitor how fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda) spreads over SSA maize systems, or even anticipate the growth of desert locust (Schistocerca gregaria) numbers before clear swarms appear. 4.2 The reference database limitations Sixty-eight per cent of studies explicitly reported that incomplete reference libraries severely limited taxonomic assignments. This isn’t just something to take for granted; it can really affect what eDNA can deliver in SSA. Bálint et al. (2024) reported that 34% of their 18S ASVs and 67% of their COI ASVs could not be assigned beyond super kingdom or phylum, and they concluded that the most likely explanation for the large number of unidentified 18S eukaryotic ASVs is a general lack of eukaryotic sequences from African Great Lakes in GenBank. Nneji et al. (2025) were even more direct: they stated that, currently, no comprehensive reference database exists for Nigerian fish species across key genetic loci such as 12S, COI, and 16S. This gap shows how crucial it is to build high-quality species reference DNA barcodes. In that work, they only got 22 species-level identifications, even though they expected far more than what traditional surveys usually suggest. Then Shechonge et al. (2024) actually put a number on this limitation by reporting that 29 of the 91 fish species they estimated to occur in the Rufiji catchment had no 12S reference sequence at all. They managed to detect 66 of the 72 species that already had references, which is 93% coverage, but for species without any reference material, they simply could not detect them. Mashaphu et al. (2024b) did the most systematic check on how complete reference libraries are for SSA freshwater fish, and they reported that 23% of South African species had no COI barcodes, while 41% lacked 12S barcodes. They also pointed out that even when sequences do exist, many come from outside SSA, which could skew how we see intraspecific variation. This result matters a lot in practice because eDNA identifications that rely on sequences sourced from other continents can easily end up with false positives (when a sequence resembles a non-African relative) or false negatives, if the African haplotypes are different enough from what is in the reference set. The reference gap is much worse for arthropods than for fish. While Mashaphu et al. (2024b) could quantify the fish gap (23-41% missing), no comparable assessment is available for SSA insects because the baseline species inventory is itself incomplete. Christian et al. (2021) reported that less than 5% of African insect species have been described, and even fewer have barcodes. For agriculturally critical lineages, like parasitoid wasps (biological control agents), wild bees (pollinators), and dung beetles (decomposers), barcode coverage in public databases is below 5% for SSA specimens. So, this means if researchers started arthropod eDNA studies in SSA tomorrow, most sequences would still be unassignable beyond family, or even order. 4.3 Methodological heterogeneity and the necessity for adjustment In this review, the 25 studies used a wide range of approaches, meaning 12 separate primer sets, three sequencing platforms, and varying replication, like 1–6 field replicates and 2–12 PCR replicates, plus different bioinformatic pipelines. Even if some mismatch is normal in a growing topic, it makes direct comparisons across studies harder. The thing that stands out is that the strongest work, 44% of the whole set, with scores 14/18 or above on QAT-eDNA, tended to look very similar in practice. Those studies typically had at least three field replicates per site, at least three PCR replicates per sample, and they included solid negative controls, field blanks, along with extraction blanks, and PCR negatives as well. They also reported their bioinformatic steps in a clear way, and they deposited the data in public repositories. Shechonge et al. (2024); Bálint et al. (2024), Heyden et al. (2023), Burian et al. (2023), and Doble et al. (2020) are good examples of these best practices. The marker gene selection ended up having a strong effect on the results. Out of 13 fish-centred studies, 12 used 12S rRNA, which seems fair enough since there were validated primers on hand (MiFish, Teleo) plus huge reference databases for fish 12S. Still, Bálint et al. (2024) reported that 18S, meant for eukaryotes, and COI, used for metazoans, ended up showing very different community pictures. In their case, COI produced just 1,544 ASVs, while 18S gave 4,527. So, the marker choice really drives what you will get, which is a big deal for future arthropod-centred work. For those, COI as the standard barcode and 16S for degraded DNA would likely fit better than 12S, even when you want a broader view. Sequencing platform choice still remains non-standardized. Illumina looks much more dominant (72% of studies), coming through its high accuracy and the well-established workflows that many groups already know. Still, Nneji et al. (2025) managed to use Oxford Nanopore MinION for Nigerian fish eDNA, and they pointed out portability, a low capital cost of $1,000 compared with $300,000+ for Illumina, and real-time sequencing abilities. That outcome implies that MinION could become transformative for SSA eDNA capacity building, especially for arthropod tracking in farming systems where field-portable sequencing could allow same-day pest detection decisions. Even so, no other SSA study has taken up MinION, suggesting limited awareness and concerns about accuracy, even though R10.4. 1 flow cell is reporting Q20+ performance, and gaps in bioinformatic know-how. 4.4 eDNA strength identified from SSA studies that could be used as a basis for expansion to the terrestrial environment Despite the constraints, SSA eDNA studies have shown real strengths that can be used as a base for expansion, including arthropod monitoring. Shechonge et al. (2024) gave the clearest sign of eDNA sensitivity: within 50 samples taken along a 2 km stretch of the Kilombero River, one single sample picked up an average of 23.1 species (that is, 41% of the 56 species seen across all 50 samples). Meanwhile, three samples returned 39.4 species (70%). It seems modest eDNA sampling can still recover much of the detectable community, but rare species probably need more extensive replication. For arthropod monitoring in agricultural areas, similar sampling setups, like 5–10 soil cores or leaf swabs per field, would likely detect both dominant pests and the more beneficial taxa. The discovery of rare, threatened, and invasive species was demonstrated again and again. de la Hoz Schilling et al. (2024) reported 27 elasmobranch species in Mauritania’s Banc d’Arguin National Park, and among them, 12 were new records for the park, while they also showed that 67.9% of species are threatened with extinction. Nneji et al. (2025) found the vulnerable Sardinella maderensis as well as the invasive Lates niloticus (Nile perch, which is one of the world’s 100 worst invasive species). Shechonge et al. (2024) also detected three species of catadromous eel-like forms, specifically Anguilla bengalensis, A. mossambica, and A. marmorata, in Tanzanian high-elevation streams, and they emphasized that these animals have to run through the Julius Nyerere Hydropower Station (built across Stiegler’s Gorge) in order to finish their migrations. For agricultural arthropods, a similar set of uses would cover spotting invasive pests right at incursion edges (ports of entry, frontier regions) before they take hold, tracking the outward shift of fall armyworm range, and observing beneficial insects, for instance, endemic pollinators, across different farming areas. The rather non-invasive character of eDNA sampling turned out to be especially useful in protected areas and when dealing with sensitive species (Seemani et al. 2024). de la Hoz Schilling et al. (2024) surveyed elasmobranchs at Banc d’Arguin while not capturing or actually handling sharks and rays, which helps to avoid stress on threatened populations. Heyden et al. (2023) sampled fish in the Okavango Delta, a UNESCO World Heritage site, without destructive electrofishing or gillnetting. For agricultural arthropods, this non-invasive approach is just as valuable, like leaf swabs that pick up pest larvae without damaging the plant, honey samples that show which floral visitors are present without having to trap bees, and soil cores that evaluate decomposer communities without destroying the soil structure. The complementarity between eDNA and the usual methods was clearly demonstrated by Nneji et al. (2025). In their study, eDNA picked up 10 species that traditional surveys had not recorded in the Lagos Lagoon, but at the same time, the traditional surveys found 108 species that did not show up in eDNA. Neither approach on its own was enough, and that mismatch matters. Doble et al. (2020) also saw a similar pattern in Lake Tanganyika, with eDNA capturing cryptic species that trawling missed, while trawling turned up species not found by eDNA (probably because of primer bias). So, for arthropod IPM, the takeaway seems pretty direct: eDNA should be used to complement, not replace, the conventional monitoring set-up, like pheromone traps, pan traps, and visual scouting. In practice, each method gives some singular perspective, and when you combine them, you get a fuller picture. 4.5 Methodological considerations and limitations Several limitations that could have affected the study should be noted. First, it should be mentioned that only English-language resources were included in the review. Although the use of English language is standard for most systematic reviews (Haddaway et al., 2020; Page et al., 2020), some studies may have been excluded due to a geographical bias. The Sub-Saharan Africa region includes many French-speaking (Democratic Republic of Congo, Senegal, Ivory Coast, Guinea) and Portuguese-speaking (Mozambique, Angola) countries where scientific research on biodiversity can be published in French (Christian et al., 2021) or Portuguese (Fry, 2023). The lack of studies on Central Africa (8%) and West Africa (20%) could be explained by this bias in favour of the English language. For example, the Democratic Republic of Congo is home to the second-largest tropical rainforest in the world, but not a single study on biodiversity using eDNA has been published there, according to this review. Meanwhile, in Mozambique and Angola, many local entomology reports or scientific articles published in French or Portuguese, respectively, might have gone unnoticed by a Google Scholar search algorithm (Heyden, 2023; Fry, 2023). This limitation could be especially significant for biodiversity studies, since a significant part of arthropod collections and bioassessment studies in Francophone and Lusophone Africa may be stored in local languages (Christian et al., 2021; Landmann et al., 2023). In future systematic reviews, it may be useful to use search terms in French and/or Portuguese and/or even Swahili with local colleagues to identify relevant literature in national languages (Message et al., 2026). 4.6 Challenges and barriers: lessons for arthropod eDNA implementation The shortcomings that were highlighted in SSA eDNA work- reference gaps, logistical constraints, contamination risks, and capacity limits- apply in the same way to arthropod eDNA, and in some situations, they are even more intense. For arthropods, the reference gap feels catastrophic Mashaphu et al. (2024b). On the logistics side, the cold chain issue, filtration steps, and access problems described by Shechonge et al. (2024); Nneji et al. (2025), and Keleman et al. (2025) can be less demanding when sampling terrestrial arthropods. That’s because filtration is generally not needed; instead, ambient-temperature preservation can be used, for example, silica beads, ethanol, or FTA cards. But then again, the capacity gap is worse. Even though about 20% of eDNA studies were guided by SSA-based first authors (Shechonge, Nneji, Okello, Kalalizia, Seemani), for arthropod eDNA, the actual pipeline of trained SSA insect taxonomists and molecular entomologists is smaller. Contamination risks, reported in 32% of the studies, show different profiles for arthropod eDNA. Human DNA contamination is less problematic because the arthropod primers do not amplify human DNA effectively, yet cross-contamination coming from non-target arthropods in nearby habitats remains a concern. Heyden et al. (2023) demonstrated best practices using field blanks, extraction blanks, and PCR negatives, all of which showed no amplification, giving a blueprint for arthropod investigations. The most insidious challenge is that circular dependency thing between reference databases and capacity building. Without SSA insect taxonomists, reference barcodes can’t be produced; without those reference barcodes, eDNA can’t properly assign species. And without eDNA actually detecting the species, money for taxonomy stays scarce. To break that loop, you have to invest in both directions at the same time, and it is more than just a theory; it’s a lesson drawn from Shechonge et al. (2024). They put a lot of effort into building a reference library first, with 198 newly sequenced individuals covering 66 species, before they moved on to their eDNA survey. Their results, 93% detection of species when references were available, show the benefit of that upfront barcoding, but that kind of investment is still uncommon. 4.7 Future directions: from aquatic dominance to terrestrial integration This review’s synthesized evidence points to three strategic areas for expanding eDNA in SSA. First, reference database development has to be prioritised for overlooked taxa, especially arthropods that matter for agriculture. Shechonge et al. (2024) showed that with sufficient references, eDNA can detect 93% of the target species. The same pathway should be broadened beyond that, for pollinators like bees and hoverflies, for natural enemies such as parasitoid wasps and predatory beetles, and for decomposers, including dung beetles and termites. Also, it should cover economically important pests like fall armyworm, locusts, mealybugs, stem borers, and so on. Mashaphu et al. (2024b) essentially laid out the template: evaluate reference completeness in a systematic manner, flag shortages, then fund focused barcoding efforts. Second, methodological standardization should not mean methodological stasis. While Illumina 12S fish eDNA is now well established, arthropod eDNA needs different markers (COI + 16S) and also different sampling media like soil, leaf swabs, honey, and passive collectors. And it is not only wet-lab work, but the bioinformatic pipelines also change, too, because the whole setup is different. Nneji et al. (2025) demonstrated that MinION is feasible in SSA; arthropod eDNA should leverage MinION’s portability for on-farm sequencing. Also, seasonal replication was rare in this review; only 16% of studies reported it, but for arthropods, it is essential. Their communities shift strongly with crop phenology and the wet and dry seasons, as Keleman et al. (2025) showed for fish in Guinea-Bissau mangroves. Third, capacity building has to be intentional and sustained, not just a one-time activity. The fact that 76% of studies had non-SSA co-authors is not automatically bad, but the long-term goal should be SSA-led research. Okello et al. (2025), led by Kenyan authors, give a useful model, where local researchers ask local questions, collect local samples, and then publish in international journals. For arthropod eDNA, this means training entomologists in SSA on molecular methods, training molecular biologists for insect taxonomy, and supporting both groups in bioinformatics. It also means putting resources into SSA-based natural history collections, museums, herbaria, and insect collections as repositories for voucher specimens tied to barcodes. And it means funding SSA-led pilot studies on priority pests, and beneficial insects too, so the findings can feed directly into national IPM policy. These 25 studies, synthesized here, have laid a foundation. eDNA works in SSA, for fish in rivers, lakes, and estuaries; for elasmobranchs in marine protected areas; for bacteria in soil and water; for arthropods in agroecosystems; and for parasites in human and animal hosts. The question is not if eDNA can be applied to SSA’s terrestrial arthropods, but rather whether the research community, funding agencies, and governments will prioritize the investment that is needed to close the reference gap, build the capacity, and shift the focus away from aquatic dominance into terrestrial integration. SSA’s agricultural future and the arthropods like pollinators. Nutrient cyclers, decomposers, pest natural enemies, and other insects’ biodiversity depend on that answer. 5 Conclusion The conducted systematic review of eDNA studies published between January 2018 and April 2026 revealed twenty-five articles that employed eDNA for assessing biodiversity in Sub-Saharan Africa. The present scoping review is the first attempt to analyze eDNA research in the region, and it highlights three key findings. Firstly, there is a clear imbalance in the number of studies published across different geographical regions. Specifically, while Southern and East Africa have a considerable number of studies published, there are few articles originating from Central and West Africa. Secondly, the analysis revealed a clear bias towards aquatic taxa and an extreme neglect of arthropods that play a crucial role in agricultural production and food security. Only one study was found devoted to the use of eDNA in agricultural systems. Thirdly, the current scoping review identified the lack of standardized procedures and the limitations of the available reference databases as a major challenge in the application of eDNA methods. A variety of different primers, sequencing technologies, and replication numbers were used in the analyzed eDNA studies. The findings highlight the need to further explore the potential of eDNA methods in different ecosystems of Sub-Saharan Africa, prioritize the development of standardized approaches, and accumulate evidence on the role of arthropods in local biodiversity. The review also serves to identify knowledge gaps that need to be filled to increase the impact of eDNA surveys carried out in the region. 6 Recommendations for policy makers, practitioners, and researchers 6.1 For funding agencies and international partners Launch an “African Arthropod Barcode Initiative” aiming to barcode 50,000 priority SSA arthropod species (pests, pollinators, natural enemies, and bioindicators). It could be modelled on Fish-BOL, Bee-BOL, and i5K, and would help close the reference shortfall for taxa that matter both in agriculture and in broader ecological terms. In practice, barcoding of arthropods for farmers and researchers will become less of a problem. Create dedicated funding calls for terrestrial eDNA research across SSA, with attention to agricultural settings (maize, cassava, horticultural systems) as well as wild environments (Congo Basin rainforest, East African savannas, Succulent Karoo). Insist on seasonal replication, and encourage multi-marker approaches, so results remain stable across time and conditions. Support Oxford Nanopore MinION infrastructure across SSA, with training workshops, standardized bioinformatic pipelines and subsidized starter kits. Make open data a condition of funding: raw sequences should go to NCBI/ENA/BOLD, while OTU/ASV tables and code must be deposited at Zenodo/Figshare or GitHub. Shechonge et al. (2024) and Bauknecht et al. (2025) offer usable examples. Back research led by SSA teams, with SSA-based principal investigators in charge. Require partnerships that are meaningful, including co-authorship, shared data ownership and capacity building. Do not allow parachute science. 6.2 Recommendation for researchers Shift the emphasis from aquatic systems to terrestrial arthropods. The aquatic work matters, but the arthropod gap is urgent, and it has been overlooked. Use adapted versions of protocols already validated in global studies, then fit them to SSA settings. Adopt multi-marker approaches as the standard. For terrestrial arthropods: COI (313–658 bp for species-level work) plus 16S (when DNA is degraded) and add ITS2 (for pollen or honey). Designs that use a single marker only must be justified; otherwise, they seem weak. Implement rigorous replication and controls: at least 3 field replicates, at least 3 PCR replicates, and also include field blanks, extraction blanks, and PCR negatives. Von der Heyden et al. (2023) provide the template for this. Deposit all reference sequences that are generated from voucher specimens. Then link each barcode to curated specimens deposited in SSA-based museums and natural history collections. Engage with end-users, meaning farmers, extension agents, plant protection officers, IPM practitioners, and work with them to co-design eDNA tools that fit actual needs: early pest detection, pollinator monitoring, and natural enemy surveillance. 6.3 For SSA governments and policy makers Integrate eDNA into national IPM policies and early warning systems. Run pilot studies using eDNA for fall armyworm and desert locust surveillance, and connect them with FAO’s Global Action and Plant Wise initiatives. Increase investment in national natural history collections and molecular laboratories. These are necessary before reference barcode generation can proceed, and they support sustained eDNA capacity over time. Create regional eDNA hubs in places that are underserved: Central Africa, for example, Gabon or the Republic of Congo, to cover the Congo Basin, and then West Africa with Ghana or Côte d’Ivoire for the Guinean Forests. For East Africa, Ethiopia would align well with the Eastern Afromontane. Take inspiration from the South African hubs at the University of Cape Town, Stellenbosch, and Rhodes.
