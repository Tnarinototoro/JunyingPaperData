Department of Medical Biochemistry and Biophysics, Umeå University, Umeå, Sweden Article metrics View details Abstract Most of the user-interface of toilets is associated with in-situ, decentralised wastewater treatment systems. Specifically, on-site sanitation systems (OSS) and decentralised wastewater treatment systems (DEWATS) are mainly used to treat toilet blackwater (faeces, urine, and flush water). Though ideal, malodour generated within these systems can hinder their adoption, especially where sanitation infrastructure is limited. Perceived desirability and social acceptability of toilets are negatively influenced by malodour and is a major contributor to its non-use and disuse. Obnoxious odours emanate largely from bacterial activity under anaerobic conditions, through the production of volatile organic sulphur compounds, nitrogenous compounds, and volatile fatty acids. Conventional odour-abatement strategies are plagued by substantial operational inputs and generation of secondary waste streams. Against this backdrop, bacteriophages are being investigated as a precision biological option, due to their host-specificity, and ability to self-amplify. This review examines bacteriophage-based strategies alongside established approaches for managing odour-causing microorganisms, with emphasis on emission points, biochemical processes, toxicity, translational bottlenecks, and research priorities. Graphical Abstract 1 Introduction Access to safe and clean sanitation is a fundamental public health priority. Nevertheless, approximately 3.6 billion people worldwide still lack reliable sanitation services, with a significant proportion relying on on-site sanitation systems (OSS) such as pit latrines and septic tanks and on decentralised wastewater treatment systems (DEWATS) (Varma et al., 2022). In India, many households depend on OSS, while DEWATS adoption remains more limited (National Institution for Transforming India, 2022). Despite their widespread use, these decentralised facilities may be underused or abandoned in the event of persistent malodour. The ‘aspiration quotient of a toilet, which is the perceptual measure of its desirability and social acceptability, can therefore be impaired by malodour. This is particularly prominent in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where provision of toilet infrastructure does not always translate into sustained use (compliance) (Sinharoy et al., 2019). Malodour has also been identified as one important factor contributing to avoidance or abandonment of sanitation facilities (Rheinländer et al., 2013). The psychological and social implications of malodour outweigh mere discomfort; they contribute to open defecation practices, compromised dignity, negative health outcomes, and associated economic burden (Caruso et al., 2017; Saleem et al., 2019). Malodour emanating from OSS and DEWATS is biogenic, a result of microbial metabolism, especially that of bacteria. Under anaerobic conditions, diverse microbial populations metabolise organic matter in black water-a mixture of faeces, urine and flush water-generating a spectrum of volatile compounds. Key malodourants include hydrogen sulphide (H2S), methanethiol (CH3SH) and ammonia (NH3), which have low odour thresholds and pose toxicity concerns at elevated concentrations (Senatore et al., 2021; Czarnota et al., 2023). Moreover, volatile fatty acids (VFAs), indole, skatole, and other nitrogenous volatiles further contribute to the heterogeneous and complex odour profile of sanitation systems (Lin et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2014). Conventional odour-abatement strategies viz., chemical dosing, activated-carbon filtration, biological scrubbing, and physical ventilation, can be effective under specific operating conditions, but suffer from various limitations. For example, chemical approaches may require repeated dosing and generate harmful residuals, whereas biological and physical systems require elaborate infrastructure, monitoring, and maintenance that are difficult to sustain in dispersed OSS settings (Barbusinski et al., 2017). Therefore, alternatives should be evaluated not only for removal efficiency, but also for operational burden, selectivity, sustainability, and compatibility with the existing sanitation infrastructure. Viruses that specifically infect bacteria, called bacteriophages, are being explored in targeted biocontrol approaches. Their host specificity allows removal of selected malodour-producing bacterial populations while leaving non-target bacteria unaffected. Recent reviews describe increasing interest in the application of phages in wastewater treatment, while also highlighting challenges related to host range, resistance, effects on microbial community, environmental persistence, and horizontal gene transfer (Reisoglu and Aydin, 2023; Shivaram et al., 2023; Baud et al., 2026). The presented review synthesises current knowledge on the biochemistry and microbiology of toilet malodour, evaluates established mitigation strategies, and examines evidence for bacteriophage-mediated odour control. The review compares phage-based interventions with biological and biotechnological alternatives, discusses evidence from wastewater and related environmental applications, and identifies methodological, ecological, regulatory, and engineering knowledge gaps that need to be addressed before translation from laboratory studies to field deployment. 1.1 Literature search strategy This review adopted a structured literature search. The search words used to identify relevant peer-reviewed articles through Scopus were ‘wastewater odour’, ‘toilet odour’, wastewater treatment’, and ‘sanitation’. The articles included in the data were restricted to those published between 1986 and 2026 (Supplementary Datasheet S1). Studies were included if they reported original experimental data or synthesized evidence on phage biology, malodourant biochemistry and toxicity, or conventional and biological odour-control technologies relevant to sanitation, wastewater treatment, microbe-induced corrosion, manure management etc. Wastewater malodour remains a neglected issue, even though it has a pronounced implication for sanitation, health and hygiene. But an increase in relevant publications (2,623) over the years (1986–2026) is indicative of enhanced interest in malodour, its importance, and the need for its abatement (Figure 1). The upward trend demonstrates the increasing global research interest in odour generation, monitoring, and mitigation strategies in sanitation systems. 2 Malodour emission points in decentralized sanitation systems 2.1 Pit latrines The most basic form of OSS is pit latrines, which are rampantly adopted in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. Malodour emission points include the toilet superstructure (drop hole), the pit vent pipe, and the surrounding soil. Amongst these, the primary emission point relevant to user perception is the drop hole, where gases generated by the active anaerobic fermentation in the pit, directly perfuse into the superstructure. Headspace measurement consistently reveals elevated concentrations of H2S, NH3 and various, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), with concentrations often exceeding occupational health guidelines during periods of peak fermentation (Nakagiri et al., 2016). The drivers that influence the composition of the emission include the moisture of the pit content, temperature, loading rate, and the composition of the material co-deposited with the pit content. Moreover, the anaerobic condition in the pit is highly conducive to sulphidogenic and ammonifying bacterial activity, converting it into a persistent source of malodour. Pit latrines fitted with vent pipes, though can redirect the odour from the user, but does not eliminate the emission (Torondel et al., 2016). 2.2 Septic tanks The quantitative estimation of odour sources from the pretreatment system of OSS revealed that septic tanks account for more than 90% of sulphide emissions and are attributed to two major factors such as the anaerobic conditions and the volatilisation induced by turbulence during effluent discharge (Yoo et al., 2026). Anaerobic treatment units have a long hydraulic retention time with sludge accumulation and high organic loading which promotes sulphides particularly H2S generation. A proper engineering of septic tank systems can significantly reduce malodour complaints by approximately 80%–90%. Advanced forms of the conventional septic tanks such as the baffled septic tank with up-flow anaerobic filter (BASTAF), Johkasou, etc., have demonstrated improved performance but have raised significant concerns on methane emissions, nutrient removal limitations, sludge accumulation, etc (Ventura et al., 2024). 2.3 Bremen Overseas Research and Development Association (BORDA) DEWATS BORDA DEWATS is a modular decentralised treatment configuration that may incorporate a biogas settler, baffled reactor (ABR), anaerobic filter (AF), and post-treatment polishing steps. Odour management remains an operational challenge in it, particularly at gas–liquid interfaces, and during effluent or biogas handling. Reported H2S concentrations can be high in anaerobic treatment compartments, emphasising the importance of gas capture, ventilation, and appropriate occupational monitoring (Vu et al., 2022). The liquid-gas interface in the DEWATS compartments represents the primary site of the transfer of odorous compounds. Efficient biogas capture can reduce atmospheric emission but demands sealed infrastructure and careful management. By treating the biogas with iron-oxide scrubbers, the H2S can be removed, but at a high cost and complexity. 3 Biochemistry of malodour generation 3.1 Sulphur metabolism and key compounds The biogenesis of malodour in anaerobic environments is closely linked to biogeochemical cycling of sulphur and nitrogen. H2S can arise through dissimilatory sulphate reduction and through degradation of sulphur-containing amino acids such as methionine and cysteine. Sulphate-reducing bacteria (SRB), including members of Desulfovibrio, Desulfobacter, and Desulfotomaculum, can contribute to H2S generation and to microbially influenced corrosion of concrete and steel (Wang et al., 2022; Anusha and Mulky, 2023). Methanethiol (CH3SH, methyl mercaptan) is generated through anaerobic methionine degradation, including reactions involving methionine γ-lyase (Vermeij and Kertesz, 1999) (Figure 2). Its low odour threshold of 0.0014 ppb makes it a potent malodourant (Ren et al., 2018; Schäfer and Eyice, 2019). Pseudomonas putida can produce methanethiol in the presence of methionine (Kahnert et al., 2000). Dimethyl sulphide (DMS) and dimethyl disulphide (DMDS) can also arise from methanethiol-related reactions and contribute to odour (Lomans et al., 2002). FIGURE 2 Microbial pathways involved in the biogenesis and transformation of malodorous sulfur compounds viz., H2S and methanethiol. 3.2 Nitrogen metabolism and ammonia biogenesis Ammonia in blackwater is generated through multiple pathways, principally urea hydrolysis and amino-acid deamination. Urease-producing bacteria include Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus saprophyticus, which hydrolyse urea to ammonia and carbonic acid (Udert et al., 2003; Ray et al., 2018; Dheyab et al., 2018). Because urine is a major source of urea in blackwater, urease activity is an important driver of ammonia production in sanitation systems. In addition to urea hydrolysis, microbial degradation of amino acids can contribute to ammonia generation through deamination and fermentation pathways. During anaerobic degradation, amino acids can serve as substrates for microbial catabolism, with ammonia released as a nitrogen-containing product and organic acids such as volatile fatty acids generated as co-products (Park et al., 2013). Complete hydrolysis of urine can increase the pH to approximately 9, at which point the fraction of free ammonia becomes substantial and ammonia loss to the gas phase is promoted (Tao et al., 2019). Consequently, in poorly ventilated or poorly contained sanitation systems, continued urea hydrolysis and the associated accumulation of alkalinity and ammonia can promote further NH3 volatilisation, contributing to ammonia-associated odour. Furthermore, a laboratory-scale study demonstrated that urease activity is maximum at a temperature of 37 °C and at pH ranging from 6.7 to 8.1, conditions that are relevant to urea hydrolysis in urine and other sanitation environments (Rao and Mogili, 2021). A study on the odour and irritation thresholds of ammonia demonstrated that the mean odour detection threshold is ∼6 parts-per-million (ppm) (Smeets et al., 2007). Several other reports also indicate low detection threshold levels of 1.1–1.5 ppm in ambient field studies and up to 5 ppm in broader sensory surveys (Cawthon et al., 2009) (Figure 3). FIGURE 3 An overview of the biochemistry of the biogenesis of ammonia. 3.3 Volatile fatty acids and other compounds The fermentation of carbohydrates and proteins under anaerobic conditions leads to the generation of a range of short-chain fatty acids (SCVFAs), such as acetic, propionic, and butyric acids. Though less potential than sulphurous and nitrogenous compounds, VFAs contribute synergistically to the malodour profile and may modulate the growth and activity of sulfidogenic and ammonifying bacteria. Amongst all, butyric acid with its unique ‘rancid butter’ odour, is a significant contributor to the faecal odour profile in toilets (Njalam’mano et al., 2020). Indole and skatole (3-methylindole), resultant of the anaerobic degradation of tryptophan by bacteria including Clostridium spp., and certain strains of Escherichia coli, are responsible for rendering ‘faecal’ and ‘putrid’ malodour at low concentrations. These compounds are difficult to remove through conventional strategies due to their chemical stability and recalcitrance to standard biological treatment, where the biodegradation pathway is largely unknown (Wang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025). The odour threshold of indole in water is 0.1–14 ug/mL, whereas skatole has the lowest odour detection threshold in air of less than 0.00309 mg/m3 (Lasaridi et al., 2010; Ye and Fu, 2023). The enzymatic conversion of L-tryptophan to indole, by E. coli, and P. vulgaris catalysed by tryptophanase (encoded by the tnaA gene), is the predominant route of indole biosynthesis in the gut and in faecal wastewater (Li and Young, 2013). Skatole also arises from skatole synthase-mediated methylation of indole or directly from tryptophan via alternative pathways in organisms including Lactobacillus and certain Clostridium species (Whitehead et al., 2008). Both compounds are subject to aerobic biodegradation but accumulate under anaerobic conditions to concentrations sufficient to drive persistent malodour (Figure 4). FIGURE 4 Metabolic pathway of volatile fatty acids and indole. 4 Toxicity of key malodourants The exposure to malodorous compounds causes potential health hazards to humans as well as the environment. It is a major occupational health hazard for workers in sewers and manholes, and exposure to elevated concentrations can cause reversible sudden unconsciousness known as the knockdown effect, leading to asphyxiation (Hallam et al., 2012). H2S is one of the most dangerous malodorous compounds, and toxicity occurs mainly through inhalation with acute fatalities having been reported. Toxicity is not limited to workers alone; rescuers without respiratory protection are also at equal risk of fatality. Other clinical manifestations at higher concentrations include cellular damage and apoptosis, leading to seizures and cardiopulmonary arrest, while lower concentrations cause skin irritation (Guidotti, 2010). The severity of hydrogen sulphide toxicity is strongly influenced by exposure concentration as well as exposure time. Saeedi et al. (2015), reported that prolonged exposure to even low concentrations affects human red blood cells. At relatively higher concentrations, of ∼100 ppm exposure to H2S can rapidly paralyse the olfactory nerves, a condition called hyposmia (Hirsch and Zavala, 1999). The routes of MT toxicity are either through inhalation, ingestion, or direct contact with skin. MT is dangerous upon inhalation, causing respiratory paralysis and sudden death. When MT combines with water, steam, or acids, it produces toxic fumes that can cause pneumoedema, renal impairment, etc (Faris et al., 2022). There were also incidences of 4 deaths and 5 injuries reported in 2014 in the US due to MT exposure (Czarnota et al., 2023). It was also reported that MT can cause cytotoxicity in mammalian cells, leading to necrosis and neurotoxicity (Fang et al., 2019; Lei et al., 2022). Ammonia, due to the low odour sensory threshold, even a moderate increase in ammonia concentrations can cause suffocation and irritation. Whereas higher concentrations can cause pain behind the sternum, nausea, respiratory arrest, and fibrosis of lung tissues (Schauberger et al., 2018). These are also harmful to the environment, causing increased nitrogen deposition, degradation of sensitive ecosystems, formation of atmospheric aerosols, eutrophication, and even contributes to global warming (Krupa, 2003). In municipal waste management facilities, ammonia is one of the significant malodourants, contributing 37% of the odour during the hygienisation process. As a result, the highest levels of ammonia emissions are often observed during sludge stabilisation, compost turning, etc (Jin et al., 2025). Prolonged exposure to indole and skatole has adverse effects on human health, causing pulmonary and hepatic toxicity (Fu et al., 2022). 5 Conventional odour-abatement strategies and their limitations 5.1 Physical and chemical approaches Broadly, odour abatement methods are classified into physical, chemical, and biological methods, each with distinct mechanisms, advantages, and limitations. Physical methods involve blocking emissions or diluting the emitted gas, which involves the use of natural barriers such as planting trees or shrubs and artificial barriers such as aeration tanks or even installing high chimneys (Wysocka et al., 2019; Maurer et al., 2016; Estrada et al., 2011; Hwang et al., 1994; Wu et al., 2023). Physical adsorption is another simple and easy-to-apply method in which malodorous substances are retained on fixed beds of porous granular adsorbents. However, despite their operational simplicity, the relatively high operating costs and the generation and disposal of spent consumables associated with these methods pose challenges to their integration within a circular-economy framework (Senatore et al., 2021). Reported odour removal efficiencies range from approximately 72%–100% at low ppm concentrations, depending on the adsorbent, target odorant, and operating conditions (Li et al., 2024). Formal wastewater management widely adopts chemical odour control strategies owing to its scalability and reproducibility. Iron salts (ferrous chloride, ferric sulphate) are commonly dosed to precipitate sulphides as insoluble iron sulphides, thereby effectively removing dissolved H2S and abating gas-phase emissions (Cen et al., 2023). While effective in implementation and operation, adding iron salts is challenged by factors such as sewage pH; moreover, their handling, storage, and transport require additional safety measures (Firer et al., 2008). In some systems, nitrate dosing is employed, which functions by shifting the microbial community from sulphate-reducing activity towards nitrate-reducing activity, which leads to the abatement of H2S from the source (Garcia-de-Lomas et al., 2007). However, prolonged nitrate dosing results in increased biofilm formation thereby limiting the penetration of chemical oxidants (Liang et al., 2022). On the other hand, magnesium hydroxide and chemicals can suppress H2S volatilisation by maintaining an alkaline pH (Li et al., 2025). But this strategy can inadvertently promote the volatilisation of ammonia, shifting one malodour problem to another. Oxidising biocides viz., chlorine, hydrogen peroxide, ozone etc., are used for contingency treatment, but are non-selective, disrupt beneficial microbial processes, which are essential to treatment, and generate recalcitrant disinfection byproducts of environmental concern (Maillard, 2018). In wastewater treatment plants WWTPs, chemical scrubbers are among the most used abatement techniques because of their high robustness and short gas retention times. Still, many chemical reagents and higher quality potable water are required for their operation, which are major drawbacks of the system. Moreover, oxidants used in chemical scrubbers form chlorinated byproducts, which again produce a pervasive smell and are harmful to humans (Wu et al., 2014). 5.2 Biological approaches Biological odour control technologies make use of microorganisms capable of biodegrading or transforming malodorous compounds into less or non-odorous end products and therefore represent an environmentally friendly approach to odour abatement (Eaktasang et al., 2013). Among these, the most widely implemented full scale technologies for wastewater odour control are biotrickling filters (BTFs) and biofilters. In biofilters, contaminated air is passed through a porous organic or inorganic packing material colonized by microbial biofilms, where the odorants are transferred from the gas phase to the biofilm and subsequently biodegraded. BTFs work on a similar principle but employ inert packing material with continuous recirculation of a nutrient-containing liquid. This enables better control over moisture and nutrient availability and the treatment of relatively high pollutant loads (Cho, 2024; Ho et al., 2007). BTF and biofilters exhibit high removal efficiency, with the highest reported H2S removal efficiencies being 91% under halo-alkaline conditions and 99% at a different inlet H2S concentration (Omri et al., 2013; Rouhollahi et al., 2024). A more optimized biofilter system considers several factors like, empty-bed residence time, packing-media characteristics, moisture content, pH, nutrient availability and microbial diversity for improving odour management (De Falco et al., 2025). Similarly, biofilters with lava rock media have shown a removal efficiency of 98% in methanethiol removal (Vitko et al., 2022). Even though these systems have low operating and investment costs, their disadvantages are, maintenance of the operational conditions and lower treatment efficiency at higher concentrations of the odourants (Barbusiński et al., 2021). Bio-scrubbers on the other hand, uses immobilised cells that can remove odorous compounds, but its use is limited because of the low capacity of treatment in the removal of less soluble volatile compounds such as methanethiol and hydrogen sulfide (Talaiekhozani et al., 2014). Inoculation with microbial agents like bacteria or fungi, and addition of bulking agents such as biochar, zeolite, etc. are some of the low-cost strategies adopted for integration into (WWTPs). Nonetheless, microbial augmentation requires careful selection of effective strains and optimisation of inoculum composition, while the odour removal performance of bulking agents strongly depends on the physico-chemical properties (Toledo and Muñoz, 2025; Shu et al., 2025). Specialised microbial consortia have been investigated as low-cost biological approaches for mitigating malodour from pit-latrine systems. Njalam’mano et al. (2020) demonstrated complete degradation of butyric acid by an indigenous consortium of Serratia marcescens and Bacillus cereus isolated from pit-latrine fecal sludge, while Xu et al. (2025) reviewed microbial strategies for the degradation of skatole (3-methylindole), an important fecal malodourant. Biological stabilisation provides another route for controlling ammonia emissions by manipulating the microbial processes governing nitrogen transformation. Strategies including partial and complete nitrification, and suppression of ureolysis can reduce the accumulation or volatilisation of ammonia, offering an upstream approach particularly relevant to urine and faecal-sludge systems. But major challenges include the production of acid-tolerant bacteria, which thereby release hazardous gases into the environment (Larsen et al., 2021). In comparison, biological technologies offer the advantages of relatively low chemical consumption, low energy requirements, and biological destruction or mitigation of malodourants, rather than transfer to another waste stream, making them attractive for sustainable wastewater odour management. However, their effectiveness remains highly dependent on reactor configuration, microbial ecology, and operating conditions. 6 Bacteriophages: biology, diversity, and mechanism of action 6.1 Overview and classification Bacteriophages are viruses that specifically infect bacteria, and the most abundant biological entity of the biosphere, with a population amounting to 1031 phage particles, and represented with a diversity to the magnitude, of a cognate phage to every bacterium (Clokie et al., 2011; Dion et al., 2020). Phages operate two life cycles: lytic and lysogenic lifestyles. Lytic phages replicate in susceptible bacterial hosts, hijack their cellular machinery, replicate and translate its genome, and produce progeny phages (typically 50–200), which can subsequently amplify through successive rounds of infection (Hobbs and Abedon, 2016). This property motivates the description of phages as ‘self-amplifying’ or ‘auto-dosing’ an advantageous feature of phage-based malodour abatement (Cairns et al., 2009). Unlike the lytic phages, lysogenic counterpart following specific infection, integrate their genome into the host chromosome as a prophage, potentially conferring new traits to the host, called lysogenic conversion. Host recognition, a prerequisite for specific infection, is mediated by receptor-binding structures and is followed by genome entry. In the case of lysogeny, the phage genome may subsequently integrate into the bacterial chromosome, whereas in the lytic cycle, it undergoes replication, transcription and translation, followed by virion assembly and host-cell lysis (Nobrega et al., 2018). The lysogenic cycle is reversible and is induced (through environmental stressors) into an operating lytic cycle (Rostøl et al., 2026). The ecological outcomes resulting from phage activity depend on host range, adsorption efficiency, phage persistence, environmental conditions, and interactions with the surrounding microbial communities (Silva et al., 2016). Since phage amplification is conditional, and therefore auto-dosing should not be interpreted as a substitute for monitoring and replenishment. Moreover, lysogenic conversion demands, screening of the host genome for lysogeny, virulence-associated genes, and antimicrobial-resistance genes as an important pre-deployment requirement (Meaden and Koskella, 2013). 6.2 Host specificity and its implications Host specificity is the central feature of phage-based control. Unlike broad-spectrum antibiotics and some chemical biocides, many phages infect only a restricted set of strains or species (Labrie et al., 2010; Shivaram et al., 2023). This may permit targeted suppression of odour-producing populations, while limiting disruption of other treatment functions. However, specificity can also be disadvantageous when the target population is genetically diverse or when receptors are heterogenous. Thus, host-range mapping and community-level testing are of paramount importance prior to field deployment. Usage of phage cocktails, with complementary host ranges or receptor specificities can broaden coverage and may reduce the probability that a single mutation can thwart infection. However, cocktail design must consider interactions among phages, cross-resistance, stability, and the possibility that treatment selects for resistant or ecologically altered bacterial populations (Hegarty, 2025; Alseth et al., 2024). 6.3 Resistance and co-evolution Phage resistance can arise through receptor modification or effacement, restriction-modification systems, CRISPR-Cas immunity, abortive infection, and other defence mechanisms (Wright et al., 2019; Labrie et al., 2010). Resistance may modify bacterial fitness or other phenotypes, but the magnitude and directions of such trade-offs are context dependent. Phages can also evolve, through changes in receptor-binding proteins and other determinants of host range (Markwitz et al., 2022). In heterogenous environmental communities, these processes can produce dynamic phage-host flux rather than permanent elimination of the target. Long-term trials are therefore needed to determine whether suppression is sustained and whether resistance alters treatment performance or microbial-community functions. 7 Bacteriophage targets in malodour-producing microbial communities 7.1 SRBs SRBs are important biological drivers of H2S and sulphide production in anaerobic blackwater environments. However, independent studies and reviews on wastewater indicate that phages can influence bacterial populations and their interactions in treatment systems, but broader ecological consequences of targeted phage addition remain incompletely understood (Reisoglu and Aydin, 2023; Shivaram et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2015; Scarascia et al., 2021). In the sanitation-relevant work, Salim et al. (2021) isolated two lytic bacteriophages (φPh_Se01 and φPh_Se02) active against Salmonella enterica, a facultative sulphidogen and evaluated their ability to suppress biogenic H2S production in synthetic sewage. The experimental design combined complementary measurements of H2S reduction in headspace and liquid along with the effect of phages on host cell viability. In a proof-of-concept study involving these phages, treatment in synthetic sewage achieved approximately 70% reduction in biogenic H2S within 6 h, accompanied by 4-log reduction in population density. The use of a resazurin-based viability assay to confirm the bacteriolytic activity in this study introduced a novel methodological approach that improves the sensitivity and interpretability of phage biocontrol experiments in complex environmental settings. Resazurin-based assay, also known as the alamar blue assay is a quantitative measurement in which metabolically active cells enzymatically reduce resazurin to resorufin and this was further complemented by bacterial cell count reduction using a plate count assay (Porayath et al., 2017; Meppoyilam et al., 2025; Subhash et al., 2022). Dissemination of phages into wastewater presents a challenge, as phage lysates may contain viable bacterial hosts that could be inadvertently discharged along with the phages. Lytic broadcasting system (LBS) is a passive phage-dissemination device that employs a 0.22-µm cellulose membrane to permit phage passage due to its smaller size while retaining bacterial cells. The phage transfer was experimentally verified by measuring the phage titres in the receiving medium and reported an average diffusion rate per hour of approximately 9.3 × 106 PFU/mL and no detectable bacterial infiltration through the membrane over 24 h, which was confirmed by both culture-based enumeration and a resazurin-based assay. The bacteriophage amplification reactor (BAR-LBS) extends this principle by retaining phage and host cells within the device so the phage replication can support sustained release. In a 1-L augmented synthetic-sewage experiment, BAR-LBS operation produced a 3–4 log reduction in bacterial load and 64%–74% H2S reduction over 48 h (Salim et al., 2022a). These results demonstrate engineering feasibility at bench scale, but they do not establish performance in full-scale OSS or DEWATS systems, where fouling, hydraulic variability, suspended solids, non-specific phage adsorption, and maintenance could affect performance. Phages infecting SRBs have been recovered from marine sediments, deep groundwater and by induction from laboratory strains, but reports from sewage-derived systems such as OSS and DEWATS are, to our knowledge, lacking (Kamimura and Araki, 1989; Eydal et al., 2009; Walker et al., 2006; Kushkevych et al., 2021). The removal of SRB population by specific phages would reduce the H2S production at the source. More importantly, targeted SRB suppression would not impair methanogenesis, as it can compete for other substrates or other essential anaerobic processes. 7.2 Urease-producing bacteria Urease-producing bacterial population need to be targeted to reduce the biogenesis of ammonia from blackwater. The population is represented by phylogenetically diverse species namely, P. mirabilis, K. pneumoniae, S. saprophyticus, and numerous other species (Flores et al., 2011). Proteus mirabilis phages have been extensively studied in the clinical settings, but this knowledge could serve as a foundation directly applicable to sanitation contexts, since P. mirabilis and Acinetobacter junii is dominant urease producer in blackwater environment (Milo et al., 2017; Sarat et al., 2025). Constituting an appropriate phage cocktail would be required, to achieve meaningful ammonia reduction, since urease-positive bacterial species are phylogenetically diverse. To achieve even greater results, phages could be used along with urease inhibitors. 7.3 Methanethiol-producing bacteria Methanethiol abatement was demonstrated by Sarat et al. (2023), through the application of two phages (φPh_PP01 and φPh_PP02) active against P. putida. In augmented synthetic wastewater, the phages reduced methanethiol production by 52% and 47%, respectively, with a concomitant 3 log reduction in P. putida within 6 h. In real sewage spiked with P. putida, methanethiol reduction of 49% and 48% were observed. The difference in matrices is important; the synthetic systems provided controlled conditions, in contrast to the complex conditions of the real sewage, which is reflected by the difference in efficiency of the treatment. These results though support feasibility but do not establish long-term field efficacy. Moreover, these efforts should be supported by better understanding of the ecological diversity of methanethiol producers in pit latrines and septic tanks. For example, organisms such as Fusobacterium nucleatum and some Clostridium species can also contribute to methyl-mercaptan production through methionine metabolism (Hara et al., 2024), indicating the importance of broader host-range mapping and environmental surveillance. Across the available studies, several limitations constrain quantitative comparison. Experiments used different sewage compositions, target inoculum, phage titres, reactor volumes, sampling intervals, and analytical endpoints. The H2S and methanethiol studies were also short-term, and long-term rebound, resistance, seasonal variability, and repeated dosing were not comprehensively assessed. Therefore, percentage reductions should be viewed as study specific outcomes rather than universal performance benchmarks. Future experiments should use biological replicates, predefined controls, time-resolved phage and bacterial measurements, and parallel measurements of odorant concentration in both liquid and headspace. 8 Evidence for phage-based biocontrol in relevant contexts Evidence for phage-based control of malodour-producing bacteria in sanitation is still emerging, and direct evidence remains concentrated in small number of laboratory studies. Independent work in wastewater supports the broader feasibility of phage biocontrol. For example, Azzam et al. (2024) reported polyvalent phage mixture that reduced several bacterial populations in drainage water and improved water-quality parameters. Azzam et al. (2025) also described a polyvalent phage system with activity against different multidrug-resistant bacteria and associated improvements in wastewater quality. Though these studies are not malodour trials, but they provide independent evidence related to phage delivery, host-range breath, and wastewater matrices. Recent reviews likewise stress on the potential, and ecological and operational bottlenecks of phage applications in wastewater (Reisoglu and Aydin, 2023; Baud et al., 2026). Therefore, H2S and methanethiol studies should be interpreted as proof-of-concept evidence rather than definitive performance benchmarks and cannot be extrapolated to field performance or compared directly with established physical or chemical dosing in sewers (Zhang et al., 2008). Differences between synthetic and real sewage, inoculum composition, reactor volume, residence time, and background removal processes can influence measured reductions. Reductions in dissolved or headspace H2S may reflect a combination of microbial suppression, volatilisation, adsorption, oxidation, and matrix effects. Future studies should report matched controls that quantify these processes separately. The relationship between microbial suppression and perceived odour should be interpreted quantitatively where possible. The reported H2S odour threshold is approximately 0.0005–0.3 ppm, depending on the method and observer, whereas OSHA’s enforceable general-industry ceiling is 20 ppm, with a 50 ppm peak, permitted for up to 10 min under specific conditions; NIOSH recommends a 10 ppm, 10 min threshold (OSHA, 2024; ATSDR, 2016). Therefore, a 50%–70% reduction can substantially reduce exposure if the starting concentrations are close to an occupational limit, but it does not guarantee that concentrations fall below either health-based limits or detection/annoyance thresholds. For example, a reduction of 70% from 1 ppm will still leave approximately 0.3 ppm, which can remain within the reported odour detection ranges. Conversely, a 70% reduction from 20 ppm would leave a residual 6 ppm, which is below the OSHA general-industry ceiling, but still above typical odour thresholds, and above the NIOSH 10 min ceiling. This highlights why future trials should report starting and ending headspace concentrations, not only percentage reduction, and distinguish odour acceptability from occupational safety. Azzam et al. (2024) and co-workers demonstrated the potential of bacteriophages as targeted biological tools for wastewater disinfection. Three newly isolated phages viz. MCn10 (Siphovirus), MCn11 (Myovirus), and MCn12 (Podovirus) were used as a polyvalent cocktail and achieved reductions of 95.2% in E. coli, 91% in C. freundii, 73.3% in Proteus vulgaris, 98% in Salmonella sp., 50% in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, 80% in P. fluorescens, and 96.1% in Enterococcus faecalis. Building on this work, another study characterised Malk1, a lytic phage active against 92% of the bacterial strains tested, including multidrug-resistant bacteria. Importantly, immobilisation of Malk1 on plastic sheets enhanced bacterial removal, achieving 78%–91% reduction in Citrobacter freundii, 74%–85% in E. coli, 60%–76% in S. enterica, and 63%–72% in Shigella flexneri within 6–8 h, when compared with usage of free phages. Collectively, these studies highlight the potential of phage cocktails and immobilised-phage systems as rapid, targeted approaches for bacterial pathogen control and wastewater-quality improvement (Azzam et al., 2025). Phage applications have also been explored outside the context of sanitation. In petroleum and pipeline systems, phages targeting sulphate-reducing bacteria have been investigated for control of microbially influenced corrosion; one recent study reported significant sulphide reduction associated with a Desulfovibrio vulgaris-targeting phage (Zhang et al., 2025). In food systems, phage biocontrol has been investigated against spoilage-causing and pathogenic bacteria, furnishing a formulation and regulatory precedence for targeted phage-based biocontrol (Hernández, 2017). In manure management, phage targeting H2S-producing bacteria have been evaluated for reducing odour emissions (Gong et al., 2014). These applications are mechanistically related but should not be assumed to predict performance in OSS and DEWATS without rigorous field validation. An emerging dimension of phage-based odour mitigation involves the activation of endogenous prophages within malodour-producing bacteria, rather than the external application of lytic phages. Zhen et al. (2026) recently demonstrated that low-dose free nitrous acid (FNA; 0.2–4.0 mg N/L), a chemical biocide used for sewer control, can induce prophage activation in the sulphate-reducing bacterium D. vulgaris, an important contributor to biogenic hydrogen sulphide (H2S) formation in sewer systems. The authors showed that reactive nitrogen species-mediated oxidative stress was associated with prophage activation, resulting in prophage-associated bacterial lysis in addition to direct chemical oxidation. Following induction, the released phages were capable of infecting susceptible D. vulgaris cells, resulting in a 25.7% reduction in the viability of freshly cultured cells and a 15.2-fold increase in total phage production. The mechanism was further supported in real sewage biofilms, where increased phage production and decreased bacterial viability were observed following exposure to only 0.2 mg N/L FNA, a concentration below that required for substantial bacterial destruction through direct chemical oxidation. Importantly, metagenomics analysis of 896 sewage samples indicated that 71.9% of recovered sewage-derived microbial genomes harboured prophage sequences, suggesting that prophage-mediated bacterial control may represent a widespread, but largely overlooked, mechanism in sewer microbial communities. Because D. vulgaris contributes to biogenic H2S production, suppression of this population through prophage-mediated lysis could potentially reduce H2S generation and associated odour; however, the study did not directly quantify H2S or odour reduction, and therefore this application should currently be regarded as an emerging source-control mechanism rather than a demonstrated phage-based odour-removal technology. Ecological risk is of paramount importance and needs to be considered when translating these findings to environmental applications. Phages, being natural entities of wastewater microbial communities, can alter host abundance, competition, nutrient flows, and community structures. Targeted removal of sulphidogenic or ammonifying populations could therefore have indirect effects on other organisms through altered competition or cross-feeding, even when the phages have a narrow host range (Reisoglu and Aydin, 2023; Baud et al., 2026). Additionally, horizontal gene transfer is a concern, particularly with temperate phages. Genomic screening can reduce the risk of excluding candidates with detectable lysogeny, virulence, or antimicrobial-resistance determinants, but it cannot eliminate uncertainty associated with uncharacterised genes or rare transfer events. These considerations support a sequential approach in which laboratory efficacy is followed by contained pilot trials with longitudinal microbiome, resistome, phage-persistence, and monitoring of the treatment-performance. Another concept that is advanced as a theoretical framework for phage-based environmental biocontrol is called ‘phage shepherding’, which uses phages to steer microbial community composition towards a preferred state. In this proposition the phages need not achieve complete eradication of the target bacteria (which would be thermodynamically impossible and ecologically undesirable), but rather sustained suppression of the target population below thresholds at which their metabolic activities generate a perceptual level of malodourant compounds. The experimental results from the H2S and methanethiol abatement studies are entirely consistent with this concept, demonstrating substantial but partial reduction target bacterial load will nonetheless translate to meaningful abatement of malodour output (Table 1). Sewage sampling, plaque assay, one-step growth analyses, stability testing, host-range mapping, and genome analysis provide a useful workflow for identifying candidate phages. However, culture-based isolation alone may miss non-culturable hosts and underestimate phage diversity. Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic surveys of OSS and DEWATS can complement isolation by identifying phage populations, host associations, and ecological dynamics. Candidate phages should be screened for host range, obligate lytic life cycle, absence of virulence and antimicrobial-resistance determinants, and stability under sanitation-relevant pH, temperature, ammonia, suspended solids, and organic-loading conditions. Safe production hosts are also required when devices depend on in situ amplification (Salim et al., 2022b). 9.2 Formulation, delivery, and scale-up For field application, formulations should show stability and retain infectivity under changing pH, temperature, ammonia concentration, ionic strength, and interactions with suspended solids and biofilms. In the present evidence base, phages φPh_PP01 and φPh_PP02 exhibited stability under selected stress conditions, while LBS/BAR-LBS experiments demonstrated short-term delivery in augmented synthetic sewage. However, measured long-term storage survival, performance under sustained high NH3, and device performance under repeated fouling and cleaning cycles remain insufficiently characterised for deployment in sanitation settings. Spray-drying, lyophilisation, and encapsulation are therefore promising formulation strategies, but their suitability should be demonstrated using defined storage-stability endpoints (titre, activity after rehydration, and shelf-life) before claims on deployment readiness are made (Leung et al., 2018). LBS and BAR-LBS should similarly be subjected to evaluation for membrane fouling, phage adsorption, hydraulic resistance, service intervals, and containment. 9.3 Regulatory, ecological, and biosafety considerations Environmental applications demand a precautionary safety framework. Phage specificity can reduce direct effects on non-host bacteria, but its effect at the community level cannot be inferred solely from host range. Target suppression could alter cross-feeding, competition, sulphur cycling, nitrogen transformation, methanogenesis, or biofilm structure require for example, longitudinal 16S rRNA gene sequencing or metagenomics coupled with functional measurements. Horizontal gene transfer or phage conversion is another consideration because temperate or transducing phages can mobilise bacterial DNA across the population. Genome screening can exclude lysogeny, virulence, and antimicrobial-resistance determinants, but it cannot eliminate all with certainty because gene-function annotation is incomplete and rare transfer events may be difficult to predict (Reisoglu and Aydin, 2023; Baud et al., 2026). Regulatory frameworks for deliberate environmental phage release in sanitation remain less established than those for food or therapeutic applications (Singh et al., 2025). Though a benign alternative to conventional antimicrobials, their environmental application remains associated with considerable regulatory ambiguity. Unlike conventional chemical disinfectants and biocides, phages lack a uniform defined regulatory pathway for environmental application, particularly in wastewater treatment and sanitation. Regulatory concerns include safety and genetic characterisation of phage formulations, the possibility of horizontal gene transfer, inadvertent effects on non-target microbial communities, and the persistence and ecological consequences of releasing high titre of phage into the environment. Consequently, regulatory framework remains fragmented across jurisdictions and application sectors, highlighting the need for standardised safety assessment, genomic screening, environmental risk evaluation, and application specific regulatory guidelines before rampant environmental deployment of phage-based strategies (Meaden and Koskella, 2013). 9.4 Integration with existing treatment systems Plugging in with the existing treatment systems should be designed as a compatible modular unit rather than as a replacement for basic sanitation engineering. For example, not yet field-validated, scenario for a septic tank, an LBS could be positioned at a controlled liquid-flow or recirculation point where contact with the target bacterial population is maximised, while the device remains accessible for inspection. A BAR-LBS could be considered where continuous phage local phage production is demonstrated to be safe and controllable. The appropriate phage titre, dosing interval, and device residence time should be determined experimentally from hydraulic retention time, target bacterial abundance, and measured phage decay rather than assumed from laboratory MOI values. Routine ventilation and a simple biofilter or gas-treatment step could continue to manage residual emissions. Device servicing would need defined responsibilities, for example, trained sanitation operators or scheduled desludging/maintenance teams with inspection teams, with inspection intervals based on fouling, titre loss, and hydraulic performance. This integrated model should be tested first in pilot OSS/DEWATS units before community-scale implementations. 9.5 Priority methodological agenda Four connected priorities can move field from proof-of-concept to robust environmental validation: (1) standardise laboratory and field protocols that combine facile spectrometric techniques, GC-MS/olfactometry with phage treatment and matched abiotic controls; (2) in situ monitoring that couples 16S rRNA gene sequencing or metagenomics with odorant concentration and flux measurement; (3) long-term trials that track phage persistence, resistance, microbial-community recovery, and seasonal effects of temperature, pH, loading and hydraulic conditions; and (4) quantitative formulation and dosing studies informing infective titre during storage, delivery efficiency, membrane fouling, and performance under elevated NH3 and variable pH. These datasets should be reported with biological replicates, uncertainty estimates, and appropriate untreated and process controls so that phage-mediated effects can be separated from physical, chemical, and matrix-dependent removal. 10 Conclusion Malodour is an important barrier to the acceptability (aspirational quotient), and sustained use of on-site sanitation in many settings. Its biogenesis reflects complex microbial and physicochemical processes involving sulphur and nitrogen containing compounds, volatile fatty acids and other odorous metabolites. Conventional mitigation can be effective, but operational requirements, maintenance, non-target effects, and residual management can constrain deployment in sanitation systems. Bacteriophages provide a potentially selective means of suppressing defined malodour-producing bacterial population. However, the current evidence should be interpreted as an early-stage proof-of-concept. Reported reduction in H2S and methanethiol have largely been obtained in laboratory-scale systems, and field-scale efficacy, cost, long-term stability, resistance dynamics, microbiome effects, and environmental safety remain to be established. Moreover, phage-based mitigation should be cautiously described as inherently low-cost or environmentally benign; these attributes require validation for each formulation and operating context (Skliros et al., 2025). Actionable future research should prioritise standardised odorant measurements, long-term OSS/DEWATS trials, quantitative phage persistence and resistance monitoring, community-level microbiome analysis, and engineering studies of LBS/BAR-LBS deployment. Regulatory and biosafety frameworks should be considered with these experiments, including genome-based screening for lysogeny, virulence and antimicrobial-resistance determinants and controlled-release protocols. Comparative studies with chemical, physical and biological odour-control approaches will be required to assess whether phage-based interventions would provide a measurable operational or sustainability advantage. In conclusion, phage-mediated malodour mitigation is a promising research direction, rather than a field-ready technology. Progress will depend on integrating environmental microbiology, phage ecology, sanitation engineering, analytical odour science, community co-design, and regulatory assessment. Moreover, a balanced evidence base that includes successful outcomes, failures, non-target effects, and long-term performance will be essential for determining whether and where phage-based systems can contribute to acceptable, safe, and sustainable sanitation.
