Abstract 35 
Breeding Bird Monitoring Schemes (BMS) provide the backbone of large-scale 36 
biodiversity assessment and are widely used to inform conservation policy and 37 
management. However, for species with low or variable detectability, abundance-based 38 
indicators derived from general monitoring schemes may not accurately reflect 39 
population dynamics. This issue is particularly relevant for species with highly variable 40 
detectability. Using the Common Quail (Coturnix coturnix), a widespread farmland 41 
game bird, we evaluated how detectability bias influences abundance estimates and 42 
long-term population indicators derived from volunteer-based monitoring. 43 
We compared data from a general breeding bird monitoring scheme and a species-44 
specific survey designed to maximize quail detectability. The species-specific survey 45 
frequently detected quails in surveys classified as absences by the general monitoring 46 
scheme and consistently recorded higher abundances when both methods detected the 47 
species. A calibration model incorporating vegetation greenness (NDVI) translated 48 
general survey counts into species-specific abundance indicators and showed that 49 
differences between monitoring approaches depended on habitat conditions. 50 
Although overall long-term trends were broadly similar between monitoring 51 
approaches, substantial differences emerged in high-quality habitats, where detectability 52 
bias was strongest and uncorrected indicators suggested population declines that were 53 
not supported by calibrated estimates. These results indicate that detectability bias can 54 
influence population trend indicators and potentially affect conservation assessments. 55 
Integrating species-specific and volunteer-based monitoring provides a practical 56 
framework for improving biodiversity indicators without sacrificing the spatial and 57 
temporal coverage of large-scale monitoring programs. 58
