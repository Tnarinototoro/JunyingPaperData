Using the “soul and body” framework of urban attractiveness aligned with UN Sustainable Development Goal 11, we conducted an interactive survey with 120 participants during Malta’s Science in the City festival. The research employed a novel visual installation where participants used colour-coded yarn to indicate their perceived satisfaction across seven outcome targets: housing satisfaction, mobility, participatory governance, cultural identity, safety, cleanliness, and access to green spaces. Results reveal significant regional disparities, with Malta’s densely populated Central region showing notably lower satisfaction in walkability, cleanliness, and green space access compared to other regions. A striking finding emerged regarding generational differences: children consistently rated their urban environment more critically than adults, particularly concerning walkability and safety. Additionally, participants across all demographics expressed minimal involvement in local decision-making processes, highlighting a disconnect between formal governance structures and genuine public participation. The study, though exploratory, demonstrates that perceived liveability extends beyond technical urban indicators to encompass emotional connections to place and meaningful community engagement. Our findings suggest three key intervention areas: enhancing pedestrian infrastructure and green connectivity in dense urban areas, developing child-centric urban design approaches, and establishing more authentic participatory governance mechanisms. This citizen-centred methodology offers a replicable model for other high-density urban contexts seeking to align urban development with resident needs and experiences. In doing so, this study makes an original contribution by operationalising the Soul and Body framework within an SDG 11 context through a participatory instrument — an approach that remains largely absent from perceived liveability research in Mediterranean small-island settings. Community Well-Being Quality of Life Research Urban Ethnography Sociology of the Body Social Indicators Urban Sociology Urban Livability Assessment and Planning 1 Introduction Living in densely populated urban areas often creates a curious paradox: while constantly immersed in the built environment, residents can exhibit a surprising lack of awareness regarding its overall quality. This observation fuelled our desire to delve into public perceptions of liveability within Malta’s urban centres. Understanding how people truly experience and value their surroundings, rather than relying solely on technical assessments, is crucial for fostering sustainable and appealing urban spaces. The Republic of Malta, a Mediterranean Small Island State, presents a unique case study that can, however, yield useful recommendations for other high-density urban areas. Geographically, it’s divided into six regions—Northern, Central, Southern, South Eastern, Western, and Gozo—each with its own Regional Council. Despite this regional structure, governance remains largely centralised at the national level, influencing how urban development policies are conceived and implemented across the Maltese Islands. This centralised control, coupled with the inherent challenges of a young state grappling with rapid urbanisation, makes Malta an ideal setting to explore the nuances of urban liveability. This research aims to understand indicators of liveability arising from a theoretical framework in the light of the lived experiences of citizens. Our primary objective was to carry out an exploratory study that serves as a pilot piece of research into designing a replicable methodology for gaining an overview of urban attractiveness as perceived by the general public in Malta. It is not the scope of the research to provide generalisable conclusions on the subject. Rather, it contributes a participatory instrument that bridges an established theoretical framework with lived citizen experience; an approach that is notably absent from existing liveability research in Malta and the wider Mediterranean small-island context. By collecting and analysing these public perceptions however, we can juxtapose them with existing technical indicators of liveability to identify potential correlations or discrepancies that can eventually inspire placemaking initiatives nation-wide. Ultimately, this leads to our central research question: Which indicators of liveability are prioritised by the general public, and how does this relate to potential placemaking initiatives in Malta? The paper will first establish a theoretical framework, contextualising the concepts of liveability and placemaking, and addressing the inherent challenges in quantifying the intangible aspects that shape public perceptions of the built environment. Following this, we will detail our data collection methodology and present a thorough discussion of the results. This discussion will cover key themes including demographic influences, perceptions of urban attractiveness, walkability, and the role of public participation in urban planning. The paper will conclude with a set of actionable recommendations aimed at informing future placemaking initiatives in Malta, emphasising a more people-centric approach to urban development. 2 Literature review The built environment in Malta has been subject to rapid change, densification and car-centric urbanisation [ 1 ]. As a result, one of the many ways this is impacting our socio-geographical reality is by shaping our urban areas into nondescript spaces [ 2 ], whilst users lose a sense of place [ 3 ], and the capacity for individual agency [ 4 ]. Against this backdrop, it becomes imperative to understand how the qualities of ‘place’ give rise to urban liveability. In other words, liveability can be viewed not as an independent goal but as the fruit of well‑nurtured places. In urban theory, place is not defined simply in geographical terms; as a physical position or location on earth, however, ‘place’ is also the term used to describe a space with meaning [ 5 ]. Yi-Fu Tuan’s key publication, ‘Space and Place’ [ 6 ] provides valuable insight into the intricate relationship between individuals and their environment [ 3 ]. Therefore, a space becomes a place in the attachment of meaning by users to a physical location [ 3 ]. 2.1 Liveability as a result of place Building on this foundation, scholars have demonstrated how place qualities directly influence human behaviour and social relations [ 7 ]. Places are the loci of social life; in the words of [ 7 ], “places are seen as the sites of social relations” (p. 3) constructed through the daily routines and interactions of inhabitants. In this view, building a livable city means crafting places with distinctive character and identity, not just managing empty space. Classic theorists from Lefebvre to Tuan and Relph similarly argue that cities only become meaningful through such human practices and attachments. Simply put, the character of place itself serves as the engine driving social vitality [ 3 ]. To be able to use and apply these theoretical insights, the concept of quality of place, or urban attractiveness, may be used. This concept integrates various tangible and intangible indicators to be able to measure the liveability, attraction or satisfaction of a built environment [ 8 ]. Jane Jacobs [ 9 ] famously illustrated how mixed‑use streetscapes generate spontaneous social interaction, or “sidewalk ballet” (p. 50), laying the groundwork for resilient communities. Gehl [ 10 ] extended this by documenting how human‑scaled architecture, street furniture, and greenery increase pedestrian activity and perceived safety. Empirical studies by [ 11 ] further confirm that districts scoring high on quality‑of‑place indicators report elevated residential satisfaction and stronger place attachment. These findings collectively reveal that place quality is not an abstract aesthetic but a measurable determinant of liveability. It is also worth noting that this brief look at various approaches also reveals the difficulty in finding a unified approach to measuring such indicators. Liveability, accordingly, emerges as users relate to place, and more specifically, the degree to which places and spaces satisfy the needs of their users [ 12 ]. Crucially, liveability manifests only when place qualities foster positive experiences [ 3 , 11 ], for instance, when a park is not just green space but a venue for recreation and social exchange, or, when a neighbourhood street is not only a traffic artery, but a platform for users’ vibrant daily life [ 13 , 14 ]. In this sense, liveability can be conceptualised as the product of place: the more a location fosters meaning and agency, the more liveable it becomes [ 8 ]. Framing liveability as a result of place prepares the way for placemaking strategies aimed at cultivating these outcomes [ 3 , 15 ]. Placemaking translates theoretical constructs into actionable processes by intentionally shaping public spaces around community identities and needs. 2.2 Placemaking and co-creation Placemaking translates place theory into practice. Emerging as a response to 1960s car-centric planning, placemaking emphasises people-focused design, drawing on thinkers like Jacobs, Whyte, and Gehl. In broad terms, placemaking refers to the act of the creation or changing of place. The term is often used in the context of the adaptation of spaces to be more user-centric and designing for more liveable and resilient urban environments [ 4 ]. This is often understood as explicit placemaking; in other words, intentional, systematic efforts that seek to shape the environment, so as to meet pre-defined objectives [ 16 ]. There is, however, another approach to placemaking through implicit actions. This can be described as a range of changes that result from many distinct interactions between people and place. Notably, the National Research Council report, ‘Community and the Quality of Life’ [ 17 ], draws attention to the interchangeability of the terms “community” and “place” in relevant literature. This linguistic convergence points to the intrinsic connection between physical spaces and society, highlighting the bidirectional relationship between place and people [ 2 , 3 , 16 ]. We can therefore understand how, in the process of placemaking, community involvement has the potential to be a driving force [ 5 ]. Placemaking at the community scale is an intentional and systematic endeavour to achieve specific objectives [ 16 ]. This involves the active participation of community members in shaping the physical and social dimensions of their surroundings. It is through this collaborative effort that urban spaces transform into meaningful places [ 5 ]. Studies bear this out: In one case, [ 18 ] found that applying placemaking interventions to an underused commercial street in Erbil yielded measurable gains in liveability. They concluded that placemaking elements were “essential for activating liveability” (p. 1) and that as more placemaking initiatives were enacted, so did the perception of the urban environment’s liveability [ 19 ]. 2.3 The challenge of measuring The field of measurement of comprehensive reference data for issues of liveability, quality-of-place and other similar fields has received increased attention in recent years. However, it is still relatively under-researched, without a consistently applied method for measurement [ 20 ]. One exception to this reality exists in the Netherlands, where the Dutch Government has consistently given attention to this area of research, with the goal of applicability in policy making. The Netherlands’ Leefbarometer project offers a rare example of integrating subjective perceptions with objective indicators for policy-making [ 20 ]. In doing so, the measurement encapsulates how liveable an area is and the factors that contribute to this liveability – the result and the ingredients. Other organisations have created different frameworks to measure the liveability of a city. Widely used tools like The Economist’s and Mercer’s liveability indices offer broad comparisons but focus heavily on economic metrics, often overlooking lived experience [ 21 , 22 ]. A notable discrepancy in definition is between organisations that acknowledge only tangible characteristics of cities, and others that consider the intangible ones also [ 15 ]. The challenge in creating a comprehensive system of measurement for liveability lies in the subjective and intangible elements of place, which, as discussed above, carry significant weight in informing people’s decisions to stay in, and create, place [ 5 , 8 ]. An approach that seeks to gain a comprehensive understanding of the overall ‘health’ of an urban environment is citizen-centred. This citizen-centred approach of, not only building quality place in the community [ 3 ], but also defining the quality of a place through stakeholder perception [ 23 ], is outlined in ESPON’s report, ‘Quality of Life Measurements and Methodology’. Outlining two conceptual streams that are to be used both when defining and assessing the quality of life of stakeholders, deliberately and directly involving residents is “perceived as useful by the stakeholders interviewed in all case studies” [ 3 , 24 ]] referenced in the report. A public perception approach may be applied to assess the impact of physical characteristics on perceived liveability [ 25 ]. Understanding the various indicators and factors that may inform public perception is necessary to give value to the results obtained. 2.3.1 Soul and body One such framework that attempts to take into account both the intangible and tangible aspects of quality-of-place through a citizen-centred approach is that outlined by [ 15 ] in their paper, ‘How to make cities the home of people – a ‘soul and body’ analysis of urban attractiveness’. This idea of ‘body’ and ‘soul’ of a place is an attempt at making a more comprehensive measure of liveability, understanding the value of the lived experience of the stakeholders [ 15 ]. In this model, the body of a city refers to its physical and infrastructural characteristics, such as housing, public amenities, aesthetics, and accessibility. The soul represents the less tangible, often subjective experiences of place, including identity, pride, belonging, and community cohesion [ 15 ]. This is in line with research in the fields of user satisfaction and liveability, and has the potential to deal more directly with the impacts of the built environment on its stakeholders [ 12 , 24 ]. Observing a shift in urban theory literature, we can consider the city to be a complex organism, dynamic and alive. The question that this notion poses is how one is to assess the health of such an organism [ 15 ]. Whilst most of the literature concerned with assessing the quality, or ‘health’, of a city deals with tangible indicators, the question of why residents choose to stay where they do does not always correlate [ 21 , 22 ]. This is the question that is sought to be answered through the division of a city’s characteristics into two principal elements – its body, and its soul [ 15 ]. In defining what elements of a city make up its body, and which make up the soul, one can assess more comprehensively the overall health and appeal of a place, and in turn, its perceived liveability, allowing policymakers to address gaps that would otherwise not have been addressed [ 8 ]. Cities display fascinating dynamics resulting from the interaction between humans and their environment. Over time, as cities evolve and decline over time, this interaction remains central, as Jacobs (1961) highlights. The essence of this interaction is rooted in two key components: the physical body and the intangible essence of human existence. [ 15 ] define the body of a city as consisting of the material elements that make up the urban environment, “the visible and tangible part of a human settlement” [ 15 ]. Resultantly, the body impacts the soul, and vice versa. It is therefore important for one to be able to assess both elements of the urban area. In their empirical research across four Swedish cities, [ 15 ] developed a City Love Index that aggregates residents’ subjective assessments of their cities across multiple indicators. These indicators are grouped into relational dimensions - the soul - and environmental or functional dimensions - the body. Below are some of the elements that make up both soul and body indicators [ 15 ]. 2.3.2 Soul Identity: Whether residents feel the city has a strong, recognisable character. Belonging and attachment: The degree to which people feel part of the place. Pride and satisfaction: Emotional investment in the city’s image and lived experience. Loyalty and recommendation: Willingness to remain in or promote the city to others. Identification with fellow residents: The social fabric that binds communities. 2.3.3 Body Housing: Quality, affordability, and diversity of residential stock. Public services and governance: Efficiency, transparency, and accessibility of local administration. Aesthetics: Visual coherence, architectural beauty, and overall physical attractiveness. Walkability and accessibility: The ease of navigating urban space on foot or via public transport. Green and open spaces: Presence and proximity of parks, nature, and recreational areas. Meeting places and commercial services: Spaces that support everyday interaction and economic vitality. Diversity and culture: Representation, inclusion, and vibrancy across social groups. Whilst this outlook is accredited in contemporary literature, it is still not widely applied in practice. The implication of this is that numerous organisations apply their own frameworks and methods of measuring, often tangible, aspects in order to assess the liveability, or ‘health’ of a city. ESPON (European Observation Network for Territorial Development and Cohesion) has, in recent years, shifted towards an approach that takes into account the users’ perspective and lived experience. The report titled ‘Quality of Life Measurements and Methodology’, published by ESPON in 2020, aims to formulate a standard method for measuring “territorial quality of life” [ 24 ]. The goal here, in line with much of the other literature on the topic, is not to simply meet a bare minimum of ‘liveability’ [ 12 ], but rather to research and benchmark the quality of life intending to create conditions that enable stakeholders to flourish [ 24 ]. 2.3.4 The maltese context Attempting to apply this to the local context, one must note that Malta’s constitutional framework explicitly enshrines sustainability as a guiding principle, requiring the State to “protect and conserve the environment and its resources for the benefit of the present and future generations” [ 26 ]. This foundational commitment is echoed in the Development Planning Act (2015), which mandates land-use planning to enhance quality of life through sustainable development. However, the country’s rapid urban expansion raises pressing questions about whether these commitments are being meaningfully upheld, particularly in light of high-density development and growing public concern over environmental degradation [ 1 , 27 ]. In this context, the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal 11 Sustainable Cities and Communities (SDG 11), offers a useful lens for evaluating sustainable urban development. SDG 11 articulates a series of targets that directly intersect with the core themes of this study—liveability, placemaking, and citizen experience. Safe housing, access to nature, cultural identity, and community participation are all core ingredients in cultivating a strong “sense of place” and fostering urban environments that support wellbeing and attachment. In this way, SDG 11 acts not merely as a set of policy benchmarks, but as a bridge between macro-level sustainability goals and micro-level lived experiences. Applying it to the Maltese context helps highlight where urban development supports placemaking—and where it may be undermining it. This approach is particularly apt for Malta, where urban density, limited green space, and rapid development often obscure how people feel about their surroundings. By aligning the indicators used in this framework with SDG 11 outcome targets and local policy priorities, this study operationalises a citizen-centred model of urban assessment. It enables a more nuanced understanding of how demographics (e.g., children, older adults, residents of specific regions) influence perceived liveability and how placemaking initiatives can respond to these differentiated needs. 3 Methodology This research used an exploratory quantitative approach to gain insights into public perceptions of Malta’s urban environment. Designed as a pilot study, a quantitative survey was formulated, consisting of 7 Likert scale questions, each representing a specific indicator of perceived liveability. The process of choosing relevant indicators and creating the survey format, which took the form of an interactive installation, is further discussed in the chapter below. Data collection and processing followed ethics approval from the University of Malta Faculty for the Built Environment’s Research Ethics Committee. 3.1 Data collection As mentioned above, the data collection occurred in person, as part of a science showcase event in Valletta, Malta. ‘Science in the City’ (SitC) is Malta’s national Science & Arts Festival, organised by the Malta Chamber of Scientists in collaboration with the University of Malta and other research and cultural institutions [ 18 ]. Launched in 2012 as part of the European Researchers’ Night initiative, SitC has, over more than a decade, built bridges between researchers, artists and the wider community [ 29 ]. 3.1.1 Participants Participants of all ages residing in Malta were invited to take part in the survey, with no restrictions beyond permanent residency. The event’s family-oriented setting facilitated engagement with a diverse demographic, including a significant number of children, offering an opportunity to explore generational differences in perceptions of liveability. While the festival enabled a broad reach, its family-friendly outlook likely skewed the sample toward young families, older adults, and individuals from higher educational backgrounds. 40% of the participants were indeed children, which affected the results of certain thematics, as discussed below. Also, the central location just outside Malta’s capital city, Valletta, prompted regional diversity among respondents.Recruitment was conducted using convenience sampling over two evenings at the festival. We acknowledge that this type of sampling has a high selection bias. It is however appropriate for the exploratory nature of the study, whereby the methodology can be interpreted as a pilot, to be used in studies which employ other sampling techniques that are more representative and that can lead to generalisable results. 3.1.2 Instrument The survey was conducted using a visual and interactive installation (Fig. 2 ), designed to encourage participation. The installation featured a PVC panel displaying a seven-spoke wheel, each spoke representing a liveability indicator (Figs. 1 and 2 ) selected through the process outlined below. Wooden dowels along each spoke acted as Likert scale markers, around which participants wound yarn to create a closed star shape (Fig. 3 ). Participants used yarn, colour-coded by region of residence, to indicate their perceived value for each indicator. This approach allowed for a visually engaging and real-time display of collective perceptions (Fig. 4 ), while also enabling spatial trends to emerge across regions. Firstly, indicators from [ 15 ] paper, ‘How to make cities the home of people – a ‘soul and body’ analysis of urban attractiveness’ were chosen, and these were then correlated through the UN’s SDG 11 Outcome Targets to create overarching questions for the purpose of this study. This paper adopts the Soul and Body framework, as detailed in the theoretical framework above. While this dual lens is gaining traction internationally, it remains relatively underexplored in the context of Malta, where studies on perceived liveability and satisfaction with the built environment are still limited. Existing data, such as that published by ESPON, provides a structural overview of liveability in Malta, but offers little insight into how urban environments are experienced by residents. This study, therefore, aims to fill that gap by applying the Soul and Body model in a participatory context, while also refining it through the lens of SDG 11. Integrating SDG 11 allows the research to align global sustainability benchmarks with local perspectives, enabling both greater public engagement and a critical comparison between top-down data and bottom-up lived experience. Therefore, the indicators selected to be used in the final survey were the result of factors extracted from literature deemed relevant, which were funnelled and synthesised through the outcome targets that make up SDG 11. During the survey event, once people were engaged to participate, they were asked to confirm their consent to particiapte, allowing us to process and publish the data gathered. Once the survey began, and they answered each question using the yarn as described above, and a researcher would input each response into a Google Form used to record each response individually and digitally, allowing it to be stored most efficiently. Following the completion of the data collection, photos were taken of the final result of the board. A Google Sheet was automatically created with the data from the responses, and descriptive statistical techniques were implemented through univariate analysis. This revealed trends that are further analysed and discussed below. 4 Results and discussion This section summarises the findings from the survey described above, gathering insights from 120 participants. The interactive survey explored perceptions of urban attractiveness, walkability, and public participation, considering aspects related to cleanliness, satisfaction with one’s home, walkable environments, access to green open spaces, safety, participation in traditional activities and involvement in decision-making, as shall be discussed hereunder. 4.1 Demography 4.1.1 Adult to child ratio of the participants Of the 120 participants, 73 were adults and 47 were children. Though the Science in the City festival is targeted primarily towards children, these were always accompanied by their parents. The festival was held at the Mall in Floriana, just outside the capital city, Valletta. Though this tree-lined space was closed off to vehicular traffic throughout the event, this was not the case for a heavily trafficked nearby road, and parents did not generally allow their children to roam freely around the festival site. The 60:40 adult-to-child response rate also suggests that the topic is perceived as being more relevant to adults, and therefore, that children sense that they have less agency in commenting upon factors relating to urban attractiveness and walkability in their region of residence. There were also instances where adults were consulting their children prior to indicating their response, but they preferred to remain in control of the overall participation. Their responses ultimately reflected their own views when this did not align with those of their children. 4.1.2 Region of origin percentage of the participants, and by age The Central region was most represented with 37% of participants hailing from Attard, Balzan, Birkirkara, Gzira, Iklin, Lija, Msida, Pieta, St Julian’s, San Gwann, Santa Venera, Sliema and Ta’ Xbiex. The Northern and Southern regions were equally represented, with 23% of participants hailing from each of these regions, namely from Dingli, Għargħur, Mdina, Mellieħa, Mġarr, Mosta, Mtarfa, Naxxar, Pembroke, Rabat, St Paul’s Bay and Swieqi, and from Birzebbuġa, Għaxaq, Gurja, Ħamrun, Kirkop, Luqa, Mqabba, Qormi, Qrendi, Safi, Santa Luċija, Siġġiewi, Żebbuġ and Żurrieq respectively. 16% of the participants hailed from the South Eastern region, thereby from Birgu, Bormla, Fgura, Floriana, Kalkara, Marsa, Marsaskala, Marsaxlokk, Paola, Senglea, Tarxien, Valletta, Xagħjra, Żabbar and Żejtun. Gozo, Malta’s largest sister island, was the least represented, with only 1% of the participants hailing from there, possibly due to the fact that the journey to the festival would have taken much longer than that of the other participants, since it requires a ferry crossing. 4.2 Urban attractiveness 4.2.1 How clean do you feel your locality is? This question, whilst emerging from various ‘body’ and ‘soul’ indicators as defined in Table 8 , results in an assessment of general cleanliness that can be considered to be a ‘body’ metric. The results described below clearly show the impact of such a metric on the perceived ‘soul’ of an urban space. Across all participants, regardless of age or region of origin, the results show a preference for the midpoint of the 5-point Likert scale, ranging from Very Dirty to Very Clean, corresponding to a Moderately Clean environment. A second preference was shown towards the next point of the scale, that is, a Clean environment. This suggests a predominant ambivalence towards cleanliness, with evidence of being generally satisfied with the state of cleanliness in Malta. Cleanliness is a core physical attribute of liveability and one that is often used to measure the quality of a place and how users perceive that same place [ 4 , 19 ]. Indeed, cleanliness is also a motivataion for community-led placemaking [ 5 ], making the participants’ ambivalnce significant in the light of the lack of community-led placemaking initiatives in Malta. Delving deeper into the participants’ views, there is a significant divergence between the regions Footnote 1 . People from the Northern, South Eastern and Southern regions contributed to the general view, with all three regions scoring equally on the Very Dirty point of the scale and the South Eastern region scoring lowest on the Very Clean point of the scale. The Central region differed in that most participants stated that their region was Dirty, and had a higher relative number of Dirty scores than any of the other regions. This region has Malta’s highest population density and is a tourism hub while also being home to the country’s principal entertainment district, attracting large numbers of visitors daily [ 30 ]; a factor that probably contributed to this result. Considering age, adults generally tended to be more critical of the state of cleanliness, with relatively less adult participants than children stating that their region was Clean or Very Clean. 4.2.2 How satisfied are you with your current home? Whilst this question is rooted primarily in ‘body’ indicators as outlined in Table 3 , it refers to overall personal satisfaction with a particularly subjective element of one’s urban environment, which can be defined as a primarily ‘soul’ indicator. Participants across all regions and ages showed a preference for being satisfied, with 63% stating that they were Very Satisfied and 26% stating that they were Satisfied. None stated that they were Very Dissatisfied, though participants from the Northern and Southern regions had a slightly more negative perception of their home, with two participants from each of these regions stating that they were Dissatisfied, as opposed to the Central and South Eastern regions, whereby none chose this score. Thus, there is a somewhat more positive perception of the home in the less peripheral regions of the country, though this result would require further research to investigate the phenomenon. 4.3 Walkability 4.3.1 How easy is it to walk around within your locality? As defined in Table 4 , this question is evidently a ‘body’ indicator. Walkability is recognised as a primary indicator of liveability, since active mobility in a high quality environment that provides safe and interesting routes is essential to prompt community-led placemaking [ 4 , 13 , 19 ]. From the survey, there was a general perception towards good walkability, with half the participants stating that their region is Extremely Walkable and another 24% stating that their region was Very Walkable. This response is split in a 60:40 ratio among adults and children, showing that children had a significantly more critical view of how walkable their environment is. Such a result is of concern considering the deep-seated culture of car use in Malta and the importance of having walkable routes that are safe and pleasant in order to change this mentality and shift towards active mobility in the future [ 31 , 32 ]. On a regional level, once again only the Central region deviated from the trend, with 36% stating that their region ranged from Not at all Walkable to Moderately Walkable, 68% of these participants being adults. This is significant when compared to other regions, whereby only an average of 7% of participants from these regions chose such low scores when discussing walkability, showing a strong variance in the type of environments that people experience across Malta. The critical views on walkability, particularly among children, highlight a deficit in the ‘Body’ of the urban fabric - specifically the lack of pedestrian-priority infrastructure - which as described above directly inhibits active engagement with the urban environment. 4.3.2 How easy is it to get to a green open space to, from or within your locality? The question defined in Table 9 is the result of a body and a soul indicator, and the nuanced results explained below point to the dual nature of the question, fitting both into the ‘body’ and ‘soul’ camps. When asked how easy it is for each of the participants to access a green open space, 42% stated that it is Very Easy, equally divided amongst adults and children; while the remaining participants were overall equally divided across the scale, showing a strong variance in opinion. Considering the regions separately, however, gives a more nuanced narrative, with the Central region once again bucking the trend that the other regions show. Here, 32% of participants from the Central region stated that access to a green open space was Very Difficult, compared to the 20% that found such spaces Very Easy to access. This supports the need to connect available green open spaces to residential areas by means of walkable routes, making the most of valleys that pierce Malta’s urban conurbation in the direction of the northern harbours [ 33 ]. Such an intervention will drive place attachment and significantly improve the liveability of the region [ 14 , 19 ]. In the Northern region the results for Very Difficult to access and Very Easy to access are 3% and 68% respectively, reflecting the semi-rural nature of the region as well as the awareness that residents have of the opportunity to access green areas not only as formal spaces within the locality but also natural landscapes just beyond. Participants from the Southern region were equally divided amongst those who found access Very Easy and those who chose lesser points on the scale. This region has several industrial sites but also retains a strong element of rural landscape, thus echoing the experience of those in the Northern region. 4.3.3 How safe do you feel in your locality? As defined in Table 7 , this question is a primarily ‘soul’ indicator, revealing people’s perceptions relating to overall feelings of security and safety. There is a strong predominance of feeling safe amongst the participants, with 41% stating that they feel Very Safe and 33% stating that they feel Safe in their locality of residence. This is reflected at a regional scale, with only the South Eastern region having more participants who chose Safe over Very Safe, scoring 53% and 32% respectively. Amongst those who felt Safe and Very Safe, 61% were adults while 39% were children, thus showing a lesser feeling of safety among children that correlates with how walkable they perceive their environment to be. This points towards a general lack of adequate urban infrastructure, and pedestrian amenity more specifically, which is crucial for building liveability through place-attachment [ 4 , 19 ]. Correlating this with the total offences registered in Malta, the localities of Floriana and Valletta, located within this region, experience a crime rate between 2 and 5 times the national rate, with five other localities within this region having a rate between the national up to twice that rate [ 34 ]. 4.4 Public participation 4.4.1 To what extent do you participate in traditional activities within your locality? When considering this ‘soul’ indicator, as defined in Table 6 , there is a wide variance in how often people participate in organised activities that are traditional, such as the local festa Footnote 2 or that support an aspect of traditional culture, such as agrarian festivals, artisan markets and so on. It is, however, significant that children seem to participate more often than adults, with adults comprising 78% of the participants who stated that they Never or Rarely participate in such activities. Conversely, 57% of participants who stated that they Often or Very Often attend such activities were children. Considering the regional aspect of the data, a strong preference for participation is observed in the Southern region, where indeed such cultural activities are regularly held and the local festa remains an important aspect of the annual calendar [ 30 ]. 4.4.2 How involved do you feel in taking decisions concerning your locality? Across all regions and ages, participants showed a strong lack of involvement in decision-making related to their locality, with children mirroring the perception of the adults. This is result is concerning since long term social sustaianbility, a foundation of creating a sense of place that leads to community-led placemaking, requires residents’ involvement in issues that impact their environment [ 3 , 14 ]. 52% of participants replied that they are Not at all involved, while 19% replied that they are Slightly Involved. Of these, 35% are children. Only 10% of all participants feel Very Involved, and 8% are Completely Involved. These are people who form part of a Local or Regional Council, and half are children. Thus, though each locality in Malta has a Local Council and forms part of a Regional Council, a few localities have Children’s Councils and only one region has a Children’s Regional Council. There is therefore still much to be done to motivate people to form part of these Councils and to participate in outreach processes that encourage involvement in decision-making [ 35 ]. This ‘body’ indicator as defined in Table 5 is revealing of the tokenistic approach to public involvement, evident in the responses. This could be a call to introduce methods that lead to more genuine involvement at a local and regional level. 4.5 Considering liveability The analysis of participant responses provides critical insights into Malta’s liveability, pinpointing three key areas of investigation, namely that. 1. The Central region seems to be an outlier in matters related to urban attractiveness and walkability. 2. Children’s perceptions of liveability often differ significantly from that of adults. 3. The tokenistic approach to participatory governance is deeply felt. Considering the first point, the observations about the Central region strongly correlate with the pervasive urban growth in Malta [ 1 ], with intense densification in this region demonstrating the profound challenge of maintaining aesthetic appeal and pedestrian-friendly environments in such contexts. Achieving genuine liveability in these highly trafficked, dense urban areas necessitates a fundamental shift in planning, prioritising strategic vision and human-centric design over unchecked and barely regulated expansion. As established in the theoretical framework, liveability is the product of place quality: where place fosters meaning and agency, liveability follows [ 8 ]. Secondly, the fact that there is a significant divergence between children’s and adults’ perceptions of liveability is very much in line with research on child-friendly urban spaces in Malta [ 36 , 37 ]. Children have a somewhat conradictory relationship with their built environment, viewing it more favourably in terms of cleanliness but far more critcally regarding walkability. Thus, they are concerned about the lack of pedestrian infrastructure but feel that they are adequate proximity to green spaces. What does this say about the quality of the route to get to these green space then? Also, they feel engaged in community events, but not in local decision-making. Once again, what does this say about forming responsible future politicians? This underscores the need to create safe, stimulating, and accessible environments that are targeted towards the needs of younger generations through decision-making process that include children. True liveability in Malta must move beyond adult-centric designs, actively integrating children’s perspectives to cultivate spaces where they can thrive, play, and navigate independently. This will not only foster a healthier society but also has the potential to build a much-needed appreciation for high-quality spaces within Malta’s urban landscape. Thirdly, the deeply felt tokenistic approach to participatory governance represents a significant impediment to liveability. While formal civic engagement mechanisms exist, people are aware of the lack of real power at a local scale, which fosters cynicism and hinders genuine public participation [ 38 ]. This challenge is compounded by a post-colonial trend where formal engagement in local governance may be weak, yet robust participation in non-governmental decision-making structures mainly related to traditional cultural events prevails. Bridging this gap by fostering more impactful avenues for citizen involvement in decisions shaping their environments is crucial to creating liveable places that are responsive to the needs of those who live there. 5 Concluding recommendations The results suggest concrete targets for placemaking interventions - spatially, socially and politically: Spatially , in focusing future placemaking efforts on enhancing the pedestrian experience and green infrastructure, with a focus on the dense Central region. Socially , in creating targeted initiatives that focus specifically on children’s perceptions and experiences of the built environment. Politically , in prompting debate on the nature of good governance and the role of citizen’s participation at a local and regional level. Considering the above through the lens of integrated spatial planning, there are many areas of intervention that can be suggested as a result of the research. A primary intervention may be to connect green open spaces to residential areas via walkable routes in Malta’s urban conurbation. These routes can pass through the valleys, some of which as yet unbuilt, that meander through the densely urbanised Central region, thereby simultaneously improving connectivity and providing access to nature. In valleys that have been entirely urbanised, a strategy for green infrastructure can be implemented to reintroduce a network of green open spaces and biodiversity corridors. Providing such important connectivity opportunities ties in directly with children’s need for more walkable places that prioritise both their physical safety and psychological wellbeing. A network of child-friendly public spaces and routes can be designed through a series of tactical placemaking initiatives that showcase how children perceive the built environment and visualise their aspirations for living in attractive urban areas. There are several methods in the global placemaking toolkit that can be mobilised to undertake such an initiative, all of which have been piloted and can be adapted for the Maltese context. Let us recognise the role that political support can play in taking socio-spatial interventions forward, from the tactical to the permanent. Infrastructural interventions require strategic planning policy direction that in turn requires a political mandate which supports such a direction. Such interventions cannot happen in a political lacuna, and neither should then happen without genuine efforts towards participatory governance. Co-creation of public space has been proven to foster a sense of ownership and belonging; considering different voices, whether of children, marginalised groups or advocacy organisations has great capacity-building potential that can have positive multiplier effects on creating a future political class that prioritises good governance based on inclusivity and co-creation. 5.1 Further research Since the scope of this research was to obtain a broad-brush understanding of perceived liveability in Malta, there are many avenues for further research that the current study has inspired. To broaden the applicability of the study, the methodology can be standardised into a Tactical Participatory Mapping framework, replicable during participatory initiatives and deployed across different cities worldwide, especially where issues related to high-density manifest themselves. By capturing real-time, cross-generational sentiment on universal urban stressors that are aligned with SDG 11 indicators, the tool functions as a rigorous qualitative instrument that can bridge the gap between technical urban metrics and the lived experience of residents worldwide. From a spatial perspective, factors contributing to the negative perception of cleanliness in dense urban environments can be analysed in depth, drawing upon worldwide best practice examples of waste management, tourism impact mitigation measures, and others. Simultaneously, people’s preferred types of green open spaces and modes of access can be better understood, with the potential to focus on how regional similarities can inform the creation of a toolkit that is specifically targeted towards regional specificities. Inferential statitical techniques can be applied to the data obtained from wider application of the methodology to gain further insights into the subject. Interdisciplinary social research is needed to better understand liveability, potentially conducting mixed-methods, locality-level research to understand how liveability indicators intersect and influence residents’ daily experiences. Citizen science initiatives are particularly relevant here, as a means of understanding unique challenges in the built environment. With children, further research can focus on conducting qualitative studies to investigate the specific barriers and concerns they face regarding walkability and safety. Finally, further research can focus on investigating the motivations and barriers to civic engagement. Though some work has already been carried out in this regard, a specific lacuna in knowledge concerns how the skill-set that is gained through participation in traditional activities can be translated into more formal participation mechanisms related to local, regional and national governance.
