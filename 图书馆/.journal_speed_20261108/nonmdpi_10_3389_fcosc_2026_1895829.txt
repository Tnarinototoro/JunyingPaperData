Human-Wildlife Interactions Volume 7 - 2026 | https://doi.org/10.3389/fcosc.2026.1895829 The night shift: temporal activity and local use respond unevenly to human infrastructure in an Amazonian mammal community FL Finn Laidlaw * SZ Samantha Zwicker Hoja Nueva, Puerto Maldonado, Peru Article metrics View details Abstract Human infrastructure can affect wildlife by altering habitat access, human encounter risk, hunting pressure, and the spatial distribution of resources, but species may respond through different behavioral pathways. Some species may avoid infrastructure locally, others may shift the timing of activity, and many may show weak or context-dependent responses. We used camera-trap data from 483 stations across the Las Piedras region of Madre de Dios, Peru, to evaluate how distance from human infrastructure was associated with temporal activity and local use across 30 Neotropical mammal taxa. We modeled two complementary binomial responses: nocturnality, defined as the probability that independent detections occurred at night rather than during the day, and detection-night probability, defined as the probability that a species was detected on a given active camera night. Hierarchical generalized additive models estimated nonlinear species-specific and trophic-group responses to infrastructure distance while accounting for species identity, trail placement, spatial structure, survey grid, camera station, and relevant environmental covariates. The best-supported final models combined species-specific and trophic-group distance smooths, while trophic-group-only models remained strongly unsupported. Across the primary near–far contrast from 0.85 to 7.20 km from infrastructure, responses were uneven across both species and response dimensions. Lowland tapir showed the clearest temporal response, with predicted nocturnality higher near infrastructure than farther away. Detection-night responses were generally smaller in absolute magnitude, but small rodents showed higher predicted detection-night probability nearer infrastructure and collared peccary showed higher predicted detection-night probability farther from infrastructure. Brocket deer showed a similar positive but less certain detection-night trend farther from infrastructure, and tayra showed a small, supported increase in detection-night probability nearer infrastructure. Many carnivores showed weak, nonlinear, or uncertain near–far detection-night responses. Overall, Amazonian mammals did not respond to human infrastructure through a single behavioral pathway. Instead, temporal and local-use responses were concentrated in particular taxa and ecological contexts, highlighting the value of evaluating when species are active alongside where they are detected. Introduction Human infrastructure can reshape wildlife communities by changing both the physical structure of landscapes and the risks animals associate with particular places and times. Roads, settlements, logging routes, farms, river-access points, artificial light, noise, dogs, and hunting can alter movement, local habitat use, encounter rates, and activity schedules even where forest cover remains largely intact (Benítez-López et al., 2010; Forman and Alexander, 1998; Espinosa et al., 2014). In Neotropical forests, these effects can include both direct access-related pressure and a broader landscape of human fear, while indirect cues associated with infrastructure, including anthropogenic noise and artificial light, can alter foraging, sensory environments, and species interactions (Luo et al., 2015; Davies et al., 2013; Mendes et al., 2020). These effects are often interpreted through the “landscape of fear,” in which animals adjust behavior in response to environmental cues associated with risk (Laundré et al., 2010). In human-modified landscapes, people may function as “super-predators,” eliciting avoidance not only through direct mortality but also through non-lethal cues associated with human presence and access (Clinchy et al., 2016; Gaynor et al., 2018). However, human-dominated landscapes do not affect all species in the same direction. Human presence can repel large predators more strongly than prey, creating apparent refuges known as human shields (Berger, 2007). Recent synthesis suggests that human shields are context-dependent rather than universal, varying with species traits, predator–prey relationships, disturbance type, and whether human presence is associated with lethal risk such as hunting (Gaynor et al., 2025). Infrastructure may therefore displace some species, attract or shelter others, and produce weak or undetectable responses in many cases. Body size and trophic role provide useful, though incomplete, context for anticipating how mammals may respond to infrastructure. Large-bodied mammals generally require larger areas, occur at lower densities, reproduce more slowly, and are less able to compensate for added mortality than smaller species. Global mammal analyses have shown that extinction vulnerability rises sharply above body-mass thresholds of roughly 3–5.5 kg, reflecting the combined effects of life history, ecology, and human pressure (Cardillo et al., 2005; Davidson et al., 2009). In Neotropical forests, hunting pressure is also strongly size-biased: hunters often target large-bodied mammals, and roads, rivers, and logging routes can extend hunting access into forest interiors (Jerozolimski and Peres, 2003; Espinosa et al., 2014). These patterns suggest that large prey and persecuted carnivores may be especially likely to show infrastructure-associated changes in local use or activity timing, whereas smaller prey may respond more strongly to microhabitat, resources, predator distributions, or other local conditions. However, these expectations are not deterministic. Responses may differ among apex predators, mesopredators, large prey, and small prey, and trophic categories alone are unlikely to capture species-level differences in behavior, detectability, hunting desirability, movement ecology, and tolerance of disturbance. Human pressure can affect wildlife demographically and behaviorally, and these responses must be distinguished. Demographic responses include changes in abundance, survival, reproduction, or population persistence, whereas behavioral responses include changes in where animals move, when they are active, and how they use habitats or travel routes (Gaynor et al., 2018; Burton et al., 2015). Camera traps are well suited to detecting patterns in local occurrence, detection, and activity timing, but they do not automatically identify the mechanism behind those patterns (MacKenzie et al., 2002; Rowcliffe et al., 2014). A reduction in detections near infrastructure could reflect fewer animals, avoidance of those locations, reduced movement through camera views, altered activity timing, changes in detectability, or some combination of these processes (Burton et al., 2015). Similarly, increased nocturnality may represent behavioral adjustment to human disturbance, but it does not necessarily mean that night is a safe refuge, especially in Amazonian landscapes where roads, rivers, and other access routes can facilitate hunting and travel into forest interiors (Gaynor et al., 2018; Espinosa et al., 2014). In this study, we therefore interpret detection-night probability as an index of relative local detection or use, not as direct abundance, and we interpret nocturnality as a temporal activity pattern conditional on detections. Spatial and temporal responses should also be treated as complementary behavioral dimensions rather than competing responses. Animals may respond to people by using different places, changing the timing of activity, combining both strategies, or showing little detectable response. They may also shift where they are during particular parts of the day without changing their overall level of nocturnality. Péron et al. (2017), for example, showed that anthropogenic gradients can alter circadian patterns of space use, highlighting that animals may reorganize space use across the daily cycle rather than simply becoming more or less nocturnal. Camera-trap work in other systems similarly shows that some mammals respond to anthropogenic stressors through spatial redistribution, whereas others show temporal shifts that could be missed by distribution-only analyses (Diao et al., 2021). This distinction is important for camera-trap studies because a station-level detection-night response summarizes local detections across trap nights, whereas a nocturnality response summarizes the timing of detections once a species is present. Together, these responses sample different aspects of a broader spatiotemporal response, but they cannot fully resolve fine-scale circadian shifts in location. Evaluating both responses in parallel can therefore reveal whether species primarily differ in where they are detected, when they are active, or whether neither pattern is strongly expressed. Temporal activity is ecologically important because diel schedules structure foraging, predator–prey interactions, interspecific competition, thermoregulation, and access to resources (Kronfeld-Schor and Dayan, 2003; Gallo et al., 2022). This focus is important because nighttime ecology has historically received less attention than daytime ecology, even though many species depend on nocturnal conditions and the nighttime environment is increasingly altered by human activity (Gaston, 2019). Behavioral responses to human-induced rapid environmental change also vary widely among species, depending on whether animals can recognize novel risks and adjust behavior without incurring excessive costs (Sih et al., 2011). Mammals often increase nocturnal activity in response to human disturbance, and a global meta-analysis found an average 1.36-fold increase in nocturnality under human disturbance (Gaynor et al., 2018). In agricultural landscapes, increased nocturnality has also been linked to fragmentation and cascading community effects, emphasizing that temporal shifts can have consequences beyond simple avoidance of people (Shamoon et al., 2018). However, this pattern is not universal. Carnivore responses to disturbance can be variable, and some species show weak, nonlinear, or context-specific changes in diel activity (Frey et al., 2020). Temporal flexibility is also constrained by morphology, sensory capacity, physiology, diet, thermal environment, and baseline activity pattern. For some species, shifting activity into the night may reduce encounters with people; for others, it may increase energetic costs, reduce foraging efficiency, alter predator exposure, or fail to reduce risk where hunting and travel also occur at night. Thus, increased nocturnality should be interpreted as one possible behavioral response to human pressure, not as a universal solution to disturbance. These questions are especially relevant in Amazonian forest frontiers, where roads, logging, mining, agriculture, settlement expansion, and river access are increasing human presence in landscapes that still retain substantial forest cover (Laurance et al., 2000; Asner et al., 2005; Sonter et al., 2017). In Madre de Dios, migration and settlement dynamics associated with the Interoceanic Highway have further altered human access and community structure around forest-frontier communities (Jensen et al., 2018; Zwicker et al., 2025). The Las Piedras region of Madre de Dios, Peru, provides a useful setting for studying these dynamics because it contains a diverse mammal community distributed across conservation concessions, logging roads, river corridors, farms, settlements, and remote forest. Previous work in this landscape suggests that mammal responses to human disturbance may be expressed along both spatial and temporal dimensions. Ocelots (Leopardus pardalis), for example, showed altered temporal niche patterns without strong evidence of broad spatial displacement, while occupancy work from the same region found that moderate anthropogenic disturbance can structure site use by species such as jaguar (Panthera onca) and lowland tapir (Tapirus terrestris) (Zwicker and Gardner, 2024; Zwicker et al., 2025). The region therefore offers an opportunity to test whether infrastructure-associated responses are broadly shared across the mammal community or concentrated in particular taxa and behavioral dimensions. Here, we use camera-trap data from 483 stations across the Las Piedras region to examine how distance from human infrastructure is associated with two complementary responses across a Neotropical mammal community. We use nocturnality to evaluate temporal activity, defined as the probability that an independent detection occurred at night rather than during the day after excluding crepuscular records. We use detection-night probability to evaluate relative local detection or use, defined as the probability that a species was detected at a camera station on a given active trap night. We ask whether mammals respond to infrastructure primarily through changes in activity timing, local detection/use, both, or neither, and whether responses are better explained by species identity or by broader trophic grouping. We predicted that responses to infrastructure would vary among species and response dimensions rather than following a single community-wide pattern. We expected broad ecological differences associated with trophic position, movement ecology, and disturbance vulnerability to provide partial structure, with large prey and persecuted apex predators potentially showing stronger avoidance or activity shifts than smaller species. We also expected mesopredators to show heterogeneous responses because they may simultaneously respond to humans, prey distributions, resource availability, dogs, and changes in apex predator activity. Finally, because species within trophic groups differ in behavior, baseline activity, detectability, hunting vulnerability, and tolerance of disturbance, we expected species-specific responses to remain important even if trophic grouping provided additional broad structure. Materials and methods Study area The study was conducted along the Las Piedras River in Madre de Dios, southeastern Peru, within lowland Amazonian forest. The camera-trap network spanned approximately 11.84–12.16°S and 69.27–70.22°W and included four broad study areas collectively covering approximately 450 km2. Camera-trap surveys Camera-trap surveys were conducted across multiple years and survey grids with cameras active between 2015 and 2026. Survey grids differed in station number, station spacing, area covered, and survey period because they were originally designed for several related monitoring and research objectives across the Las Piedras landscape. Earlier surveys and several later grids used camera spacing informed by the home ranges of medium-sized felids, particularly ocelots, with stations spaced approximately 1.5 km apart on average. Two 2022 surveys and one 2024 survey used smaller spacing of approximately 350–450 m to better sample smaller felids such as margay (Leopardus wiedii) and jaguarundi (Herpailurus yagouaroundi). Survey duration and timing varied among grids because of logistical constraints and field conditions. Camera-trap stations were established by overlaying a grid on each study area. When a grid point fell within approximately 100 m of a wildlife trail or pre-existing road, cameras were deployed along that feature to increase detection probability. Otherwise, cameras were placed as close as possible to the intended grid point while maintaining field practicality and suitable camera placement. Each station consisted of one or two Browning Strike Force trail cameras placed 40–50 cm above the ground. Paired cameras were used during select seasons when enough working cameras were available and when individual identification was part of the objectives for specific grids. When paired, cameras were positioned opposite one another, approximately 2–3 m apart and angled to maximize detection and assist species identification while minimizing the chance that paired cameras would trigger one another. Cameras were set to infrared video mode. Because cameras were deployed at ground level, the survey was designed primarily for terrestrial and ground-using mammals. Primates and other primarily arboreal mammals were not included because they were not reliably sampled by this design; evaluating arboreal taxa would require complementary methods such as canopy camera traps, line transects, or acoustic/visual surveys (Bowler et al., 2017; Whitworth et al., 2016). The final combined analysis included 483 camera stations distributed across settlements, river corridors, logging-road networks, cocoa farms, conservation concessions, and more remote forest sites. The spatial distribution of camera stations across survey years and grids is shown in Figure 2. Data processing and species classifications All camera-trap videos were manually reviewed and assigned a species identification, date, time, camera station, and associated survey information. Humans, domestic animals, non-mammalian taxa, and non-target records were removed from the data set. Repeated detections of the same species at the same camera station within 30 minutes were classified as recaptures and removed to reduce temporal dependence among records. Some taxa were grouped because species-level identification was not always reliable from camera-trap videos. These included red and grey brocket deer (Mazama spp.), mouse opossums (Marmosa spp.), small armadillos (Dasypus spp.), and small rodents, including Proechimys, Oecomys, Hylaeamys, Nectomys, Rhipidomys, and Neacomys spp. After filtering and applying the 30-minute independence threshold, the data set contained 26,655 independent mammal detections across 30 species or species groups. Each species or species group was assigned to one of four broad functional groups: apex predators, mesopredators, large prey or large non-carnivores, and small prey or small non-carnivores. Large prey included larger-bodied non-carnivores, generally those approaching or exceeding approximately 6 kg or commonly targeted by hunters, whereas small prey included smaller-bodied non-carnivores. Apex predators included jaguar and puma (Puma concolor). Mesopredators included ocelot, margay, jaguarundi, tayra (Eira barbara), short-eared dog (Atelocynus microtis), bush dog (Speothos venaticus), greater grison (Galictis vittata), crab-eating raccoon (Procyon cancrivorus), coati (Nasua nasua), and weasel (Neogale africana) where data were available. Large prey included lowland tapir, brocket deer, collared peccary (Pecari tajacu), white-lipped peccary (Tayassu pecari), paca (Cuniculus paca), capybara (Hydrochoerus hydrochaeris), giant anteater (Myrmecophaga tridactyla), tamandua (Tamandua tetradactyla), giant armadillo (Priodontes maximus), nine-banded armadillo (Dasypus novemcinctus), and greater long-nosed armadillo (Dasypus pastasae). Small prey included brown agouti (Dasyprocta variegata), green acouchi (Myoprocta pratti), Brazilian rabbit (Sylvilagus brasiliensis), common opossum (Didelphis marsupialis), mouse opossums, small rodents, squirrels, and porcupine (Coendou spp.). Response variables We modeled two complementary binomial response variables: nocturnality and detection-night probability. Nocturnality was used to evaluate temporal activity. Each independent detection was classified as nocturnal, diurnal, or crepuscular using the date, time, and camera-station coordinates. Sunrise and sunset times were calculated using the R package suncalc. Detections within one hour before sunrise and one hour after sunset were classified as crepuscular and excluded from the nocturnality analysis to avoid assigning twilight records to either day or night. For each species at each camera station, we counted the number of nocturnal detections and the number of daytime detections. Nocturnality was then modeled as a two-column binomial response: nocturnal detections versus daytime detections, with crepuscular detections excluded. The nocturnality data set contained 4,507 species-by-station observations. Detection-night probability was used to evaluate relative local detection or use. For each species at each camera station, we counted the number of active trap nights with at least one independent detection and the number of active trap nights without a detection. Detection-night probability was then modeled as a two-column binomial response: nights with detection versus nights without detection. This response is interpreted as an index of relative local detection or use, not as a direct estimate of abundance. A lower detection-night probability near infrastructure could reflect reduced local abundance, spatial avoidance, reduced movement through camera views, lower detectability, or some combination of these processes. This approach was selected because it retains the binomial structure of the data and avoids problems associated with raw capture rates, which can exceed one when multiple independent detections occur on a single trap night. The detection-night data set contained 14,490 species-by-station observations. Distance from human infrastructure and additional human-pressure covariates Distance from human infrastructure was used as the primary predictor of human pressure. Infrastructure included the two main settlements in the study area, Puerto Lucerna and Puerto Nuevo, and mapped roads or logging roads connecting Puerto Lucerna toward the Interoceanic Highway. Distance was calculated from each camera station to the nearest mapped infrastructure feature. This distance metric combined proximity to settlements and roads because these features can jointly represent human presence, access, fragmentation, hunting access, vehicle movement, noise, light, dogs, and other disturbance mechanisms. For model fitting, distance from infrastructure was standardized. For interpretation, predictions were transformed back to kilometers. We used a primary near–far contrast from 0.85 km to 7.20 km from infrastructure, corresponding to the 25th and 75th percentiles of the camera-station distance distribution. Because human population in Puerto Lucerna changed over the study period, we evaluated estimated annual human population as an additional sensitivity covariate rather than incorporating population into the infrastructure-distance layer itself. Annual population estimates were approximate because no formal yearly census was available and some residents are seasonal. We therefore treated population as a coarse year-level estimate and used linear interpolation between approximate values early and late in the study period. We also evaluated a population-by-distance model to test whether the effect of distance from infrastructure changed with estimated human population size. Distance to the Las Piedras River was calculated for each camera station and evaluated as an additional covariate. River distance was included because river corridors may represent both ecological gradients and human-access routes in Amazonian landscapes. Survey year was also evaluated as a sensitivity covariate to account for possible interannual differences in species activity, detection, human pressure, and survey context. A complete mapped network of smaller wildlife trails, hunting paths, and informal access routes was not available consistently across all years and study areas, so we did not calculate a continuous distance-to-trail covariate. Instead, camera placement relative to trails was represented by the binary trail-placement covariate described below, which accounts for detection differences between trail-based and off-trail cameras but should not be interpreted as a measure of distance to all possible hunting paths in the landscape. Environmental and sampling covariates We considered vegetation productivity, elevation, trail placement, spatial structure, survey grid, camera station, and species identity as covariates or random-effect smooths. Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) was included in candidate models because it can represent vegetation productivity and habitat quality in high-biomass Amazonian forests and performs well in dense vegetation environments (Matsushita et al., 2007; Maeda et al., 2016). EVI values were extracted from the MOD13Q1.061 Terra Vegetation Indices data set at 250 m resolution (Didan, 2021). Elevation was included in candidate models because topography can influence mammal distributions and activity patterns (Hof et al., 2012). Elevation data were extracted from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission CGIAR-CSI 90 m Digital Elevation data set (Jarvis et al., 2008). Trail placement was included as a binary station-level covariate indicating whether cameras were placed on a wildlife trail or pre-existing road/trail feature. This covariate was included to account for differences in detection probability between trail-based and off-trail camera placement. Survey grid was included to account for differences in year, location, spacing, survey design, and broader landscape context. Camera station was included to account for repeated observations at the same station. Spatial structure was modeled using UTM coordinates to account for broad spatial autocorrelation not captured by measured covariates. Statistical analysis We analyzed mammal responses to distance from human infrastructure using hierarchical generalized additive models fitted with the R package mgcv (Pedersen et al., 2019). Generalized additive models were selected because they allow flexible, nonlinear relationships between predictors and responses, while hierarchical factor-smooth terms allow response curves to vary among species within a partial-pooling structure. We refer to these as hierarchical models because all species were modeled together, but each species was allowed to have its own smooth response to distance from infrastructure. This approach retains species-level inference while reducing instability associated with fitting completely separate models for species with sparse detections. Results Sampling effort and analytical data sets The final analytical data set contained 26,655 independent mammal detections from 483 camera stations and 30 species or species groups. Camera stations were distributed across 12 survey grids assigned to nine survey years in the final analysis, spanning 2015–2026. Annual sampling effort varied among years, with 27–91 camera stations per year and mean active nights per station ranging from 61.1 to 134.5. Mean annual distance from infrastructure ranged from 0.46 km to 6.93 km and mean annual distance to the Las Piedras River ranged from 1.06 km to 8.40 km. After filtering detections for the nocturnality analysis, the temporal data set contained 4,507 species-by-station observations. The detection-night data set contained 14,490 species-by-station observations, representing each species or species group at each camera station. Distance from human infrastructure ranged from 0 to 13.37 km across camera stations, with a median of 3.13 km. The primary near–far contrast used for species-level interpretation compared predicted responses at 0.85 km and 7.20 km from infrastructure, corresponding to the 25th and 75th percentiles of the observed camera-station distance distribution. Model comparison and candidate-model evaluation Model comparisons supported models that retained both species-specific response structure and trophic-group distance responses to the final models. Trophic-group-only models remained strongly unsupported. For nocturnality, the lowest-AIC candidate was the species-specific + trophic-group river model (AIC = 8263.22). The simpler species-specific river model was nearly equivalent (ΔAIC = 0.30), but we retained the lowest-AIC trophic-augmented structure because it provided biologically interpretable shared trophic-group structure while preserving species-level responses. The core full species-specific + trophic-group model without the river-distance covariate ranked third (ΔAIC = 2.66), whereas the trophic-group-only model was much less supported (ΔAIC = 160.15). For detection-night probability, the full species-specific + trophic-group model with EVI and elevation had the lowest AIC (AIC = 51715.69). The reduced species-specific + trophic-group no-environment model was essentially tied (ΔAIC = 0.07) and was more parsimonious, so it was retained as the final detection-night model. The corresponding species-specific no-environment model without trophic-group smooths was outside the ΔAIC< 2 threshold (ΔAIC = 2.69), and the trophic-group-only model was strongly unsupported (ΔAIC = 738.32). Models including distance to river, estimated human population, population-by-distance interactions, survey year, or reduced spatial structure were less supported. Linear species-specific distance models were strongly unsupported for both nocturnality (ΔAIC = 138.26) and detection-night probability (ΔAIC = 910.61), indicating that the nonlinear HGAM structures better represented the observed responses. The final nocturnality model therefore included species-specific and trophic-group distance smooths, distance to river, EVI, elevation, trail placement, species identity, spatial structure, survey grid, and camera station. The final detection-night model included species-specific and trophic-group distance smooths, trail placement, species identity, spatial structure, survey grid, and camera station, but excluded EVI and elevation. The nocturnality model explained 81.5% of deviance, and the detection-night model explained 60.8% of deviance; these fit statistics are reported descriptively only and were not interpreted as evidence that one response type was more strongly structured than the other. Model diagnostics indicated convergence and adequate smooth basis dimensions for both final models. Pearson dispersion was 1.56 for the nocturnality model and 3.76 for the detection-night model, so prediction uncertainty was inflated using model-specific overdispersion factors when generating response curves and near–far contrast intervals. Quasibinomial sensitivity fits produced similar broad summaries and did not change the main inferential patterns. Summary of response patterns Overall, responses to infrastructure were uneven across both species and response dimensions. The strongest temporal response was observed in lowland tapir, which was predicted to be more nocturnal nearer infrastructure. Detection-night responses were concentrated in a smaller subset of species: small rodents showed higher detection-night probability nearer infrastructure, collared peccary showed higher detection-night probability farther from infrastructure, and brocket deer showed a similar positive but less certain trend farther from infrastructure. Tayra also showed a small, supported increase in detection-night probability nearer infrastructure. Many carnivores, including jaguar, puma, ocelot, and several mesopredators, did not show strong monotonic detection-night responses across the primary near–far contrast, although some showed nonlinear temporal patterns across the full distance gradient. These results indicate that infrastructure-associated responses were not uniformly strong across the community but instead were concentrated in particular taxa and expressed differently through temporal activity and local detection/use. Discussion Uneven responses to infrastructure across species and response dimensions Human infrastructure was associated with changes in temporal activity and local detection for some mammals in the Las Piedras landscape, but responses were not uniform across the community. The strongest temporal response was observed in lowland tapir, which was predicted to be more nocturnal nearer infrastructure. The clearest detection-night responses were higher predicted detection-night probability nearer infrastructure for small rodents and farther from infrastructure for collared peccary. Brocket deer showed a similar positive but less certain detection-night trend farther from infrastructure, while tayra showed a small, supported increase in detection-night probability nearer infrastructure. In contrast, many carnivores, including jaguar, puma, ocelot, and several mesopredators, showed weak, nonlinear, or uncertain near–far detection-night responses. This pattern supports a more nuanced interpretation than a simple community-wide shift toward nocturnality or spatial avoidance. Human infrastructure can alter perceived risk, human access, habitat structure, movement routes, and resource distribution, but species differ in their exposure to those pressures and in the behavioral options available to them. Some species may respond primarily by shifting activity timing, others by changing local use, others through both pathways, and many may show little detectable response at the scale sampled here. Directional but uncertain trends, such as brocket deer farther from infrastructure or coati slightly farther from infrastructure, are also informative because they identify responses or trends that may strengthen with additional sampling, even when current intervals overlap zero. This agrees with broader evidence that mammals often increase nocturnal activity in response to human disturbance, but that diel and local-use responses vary by species, disturbance type, and ecological context rather than following a single rule (Gaynor et al., 2018; Frey et al., 2020; Gallo et al., 2022). Model comparisons pointed to the same conclusion. Trophic-group-only models were strongly unsupported, indicating that broad guild labels alone were insufficient to explain the response structure. However, the best-supported final models combined species-specific and trophic-group distance smooths, suggesting that broad trophic grouping captured some shared ecological structure once species-level heterogeneity was retained. This distinction is important: trophic group helped summarize patterns among large prey, small prey, mesopredators, and apex predators, but species identity remained necessary because the community contained a mixture of strong, weak, nonlinear, and near-zero responses. More broadly, because species interaction networks can shift along environmental gradients, infrastructure-associated responses may reflect changes in community context as well as direct responses to infrastructure alone (Pellissier et al., 2018). Large prey showed the clearest infrastructure-associated responses Large prey produced some of the strongest and most interpretable responses in the study. Collared peccary showed a supported positive detection-night contrast, indicating higher predicted detection or local use farther from infrastructure. Brocket deer showed a similar positive detection-night trend, although its uncertainty interval overlapped zero. Lowland tapir showed the strongest temporal response in the community and a weaker positive detection-night contrast. Paca and giant anteater also showed positive but uncertain detection-night trends. Taken together, these prey responses fit the expectation that large-bodied prey may be especially sensitive to human access, hunting, and repeated disturbance near roads, settlements, rivers, and logging routes. Body size offers one explanation. Larger mammals generally occur at lower densities, reproduce more slowly, require larger areas, and are less able to compensate for added mortality than smaller-bodied taxa (Cardillo et al., 2005; Davidson et al., 2009). In Neotropical forests, hunting pressure is also strongly size-biased, with hunters often targeting large-bodied mammals, and access routes can extend hunting pressure into forest interiors (Jerozolimski and Peres, 2003; Espinosa et al., 2014). Higher detection-night probability farther from infrastructure for collared peccary, and the similar positive trend in brocket deer, may therefore reflect behavioral avoidance, local depletion, altered movement, or some combination of these processes. Because detection-night probability is not a direct measure of abundance, these mechanisms cannot be separated here, but the pattern suggests that large prey are among the most informative taxa for monitoring infrastructure-associated disturbance. This interpretation is consistent with biome-wide evidence that large Amazonian mammals respond to human-induced habitat change in species-specific ways, with site-use patterns differing among white-lipped peccary, tapir, giant armadillo, and jaguar (Quintero et al., 2023). Lowland tapir is particularly important because its strongest response was temporal rather than strictly local-use based. Tapirs were still detected near infrastructure, but their predicted activity was more nocturnal there. Presence alone may therefore understate the response. A tapir using areas close to human access routes may not be using them in the same way as a tapir farther from infrastructure; it may instead be compressing activity into times with lower perceived risk. This is consistent with the broader idea that behavioral adjustment can occur before, or without, obvious local disappearance, and that persistence in human-modified habitat does not necessarily mean absence of disturbance effects (Gaynor et al., 2018; Medici et al., 2022). For a large herbivore and seed disperser, such shifts could influence movement, foraging, predator overlap, and ecological function (Vidal et al., 2013). Not all large prey responded similarly. White-lipped peccary, giant anteater, giant armadillo, capybara, tamandua, and small armadillos showed weaker or more uncertain near–far contrasts. Some of this may reflect lower sample size or uneven representation across the distance gradient, but it also likely reflects species-specific ecology. For example, peccaries can show broad-scale movement and local population fluctuations that may not be captured by a single infrastructure-distance gradient, while anteaters and tamanduas may be large or medium-bodied non-carnivores but less desirable to meat hunters than ungulates, pacas, or peccaries. These exceptions make body size and hunting vulnerability useful background context for interpreting species differences, but not deterministic explanations for the patterns observed here. Carnivore and small-prey responses were weaker, nonlinear, or context-dependent Carnivores did not show the strong monotonic detection-night avoidance that might be expected under a simple infrastructure-risk hypothesis. Jaguar and puma both had near-zero detection-night contrasts across the primary near–far interval. That result does not mean apex predators were unaffected by infrastructure. Large carnivores move over broad areas, may respond to disturbance at scales larger than individual camera stations, and can use roads, trails, river edges, or other linear features for travel while still avoiding people temporally or at finer spatial scales (Jędrzejewski et al., 2017; Quiroga et al., 2016; Boron et al., 2019). Their responses may also depend on prey distributions, hunting pressure, vegetation cover, and human activity schedules rather than distance from mapped infrastructure alone. Recent work from the same broader Las Piedras landscape reinforces this caution. In a puma-focused camera-trap analysis, puma space use was better explained by prey activity, jaguar activity, and terrain structure than by measured anthropogenic covariates, and pumas showed high temporal overlap with both prey and jaguars (Lukasser and Zwicker, 2026). Together with our near-zero puma detection-night contrast, this suggests that broad distance-to-infrastructure metrics may miss the biotic and terrain-mediated processes structuring large-carnivore activity at camera-station scales. The jaguar and puma curves point in this direction. Their activity did not change as a simple linear function of infrastructure distance, but neither species showed a strong local-use contrast in the detection-night model. A weak near–far detection-night contrast therefore does not rule out behavioral sensitivity. Large carnivores may respond through movement routes, diel timing, human overlap, or prey-mediated pathways that are not fully resolved by station-level detection-night probability. Future analyses combining camera traps with human detections, prey distributions, telemetry, or day–night-specific space-use models would be better suited to testing these mechanisms. Mesopredators were also heterogeneous. This heterogeneity is consistent with studies of medium and small felids and mesocarnivores showing that human impacts can alter habitat use, activity patterns, and temporal guild interactions in species-specific ways rather than producing uniform avoidance (Cruz et al., 2018; Gálvez et al., 2021). Ocelot showed a weak pattern consistent with greater nocturnality nearer infrastructure and slightly higher detection-night probability farther from infrastructure, broadly aligning with previous work in Las Piedras showing temporal sensitivity to human activity even when broad spatial displacement is less evident (Zwicker et al, 2024). Tayra showed higher detection-night probability nearer infrastructure, although the absolute effect was small; this pattern could reflect behavioral flexibility, altered resources, movement along trails or edges, or changes in predator activity. Coati and short-eared dog showed weak positive detection-night trends, while margay, bush dog, crab-eating raccoon, and other mesopredators showed weaker or more uncertain responses. This variation is consistent with work showing that mesopredators may respond idiosyncratically to human disturbance, depending on the balance among human risk, prey availability, resource subsidies, dogs, and release from larger carnivores (Gehrt and Clark, 2003; Frey et al., 2020; Monterroso et al., 2013). Small prey also did not respond as a single group, although the group-level tendency was toward higher detection-night probability nearer infrastructure. Small rodents showed the strongest supported small-prey response, with higher detection-night probability nearer infrastructure while remaining strongly nocturnal across the gradient. Brown agouti, green acouchi, squirrels, Brazilian rabbit, mouse opossum, and common opossum showed negative but weaker or more uncertain detection-night contrasts. These patterns could reflect altered resources, edge conditions, habitat structure, detectability, or reduced mesopredator activity near human-influenced areas. They are also consistent with one possible human-shield mechanism, in which prey use areas avoided by predators, but this interpretation should remain cautious. Human-shield effects are context-dependent and can be weakened or reversed when human presence is associated with hunting, dogs, roads, or other lethal and nonlethal risks, which are all variable across this study area (Berger, 2007; Gaynor et al., 2025). In Amazonian forest frontiers, areas near people are not automatically refuges. The higher local detection of small rodents near infrastructure should therefore be treated as a pattern requiring further testing, not as evidence that infrastructure benefits small mammals. Interpreting temporal shifts: behavioral flexibility, circadian space use, and limits of inference The paired analysis of nocturnality and detection-night probability shows why spatial and temporal responses should be interpreted as complementary rather than competing dimensions. Nocturnality describes when detections occurred, whereas detection-night probability describes whether a species was detected at a station on a given active trap night. These responses answer different questions. Tapir combined a strong temporal shift with a weaker local-use contrast; collared peccary and the positive brocket deer trend were expressed more clearly through detection-night probability than through temporal shifts; small rodents showed higher local detection near infrastructure but little temporal change because they were already highly nocturnal. A single response variable would have missed some of this structure. At the same time, the two-response framework still simplifies the full spatiotemporal behavior of animals. Species may use different places at different times of day without changing their overall level of nocturnality or their overall detection-night probability. Péron et al. (2017) showed that periodic behaviors can translate into periodic patterns of space use along anthropization gradients, highlighting that animals may reorganize where they are during the daily cycle rather than simply becoming more or less nocturnal. In our study, a carnivore with a weak near–far detection-night contrast could still avoid infrastructure during the day and use it at night, while a prey species could shift both activity and location in ways not fully captured by station-level contrasts. Temporal shifts should also not be interpreted as cost-free. Increased nocturnality may reduce encounters with people in some systems, but it can also alter foraging efficiency, thermoregulation, predator–prey overlap, competition, and access to resources (Kronfeld-Schor and Dayan, 2003; Shamoon et al., 2018; Gallo et al., 2022). This is especially relevant in Amazonian landscapes where rivers, roads, and logging tracks can facilitate human access and where human activity, including hunting, may occur after dark (Jerozolimski and Peres, 2003; Espinosa et al., 2014). Behavioral flexibility may therefore help some mammals persist near infrastructure, but it may also indicate that animals are using suboptimal times or places. The conservation question is not only whether species remain present, but whether they can maintain normal movement, activity, and ecological interactions in human-influenced habitat. Several limitations are important. Detection-night probability is an index of relative local detection or use, not abundance. It can be influenced by abundance, movement rate, activity level, detectability, camera placement, and habitat structure. This is a general challenge in camera-trap studies, where detection and nondetection cannot be interpreted directly as presence, absence, or abundance without additional assumptions (MacKenzie et al., 2002; Burton et al., 2015; Rovero and Zimmermann, 2016). Our precision analysis helped separate poorly estimated species from well-sampled species with weak near–far contrasts, but it does not resolve the mechanisms behind detection-night responses. Occupancy, abundance, spatial capture–recapture, telemetry, or integrated models would be needed to distinguish behavioral avoidance from demographic depletion. Conservation implications and limitations The results have practical implications for conservation in Las Piedras and similar Amazonian forest frontiers. First, maintaining large, connected, low-disturbance forest remains essential. Collared peccary had higher predicted detection-night probability farther from infrastructure, brocket deer showed a similar positive trend, and tapir showed a strong temporal shift near infrastructure. These species are ecologically important as herbivores, seed dispersers, and prey for large carnivores, and their responses suggest that forest cover alone may not be enough if access routes, hunting, dogs, and settlement activity alter how animals use otherwise intact habitat. Second, management should consider both spatial and temporal refuges. Camera-trap studies commonly measure diel activity, but conservation practice has fewer established tools for applying temporal information directly. In this landscape, reducing hunting pressure and unnecessary night travel, managing logging-road access, limiting dog movement, and maintaining corridors away from major access routes could help protect both where animals move and when they can use those areas. These actions are especially relevant in Amazonian settings where roads and rivers can extend human access into forest interiors (Laurance et al., 2000; Espinosa et al., 2014). Third, monitoring programs should treat behavioral change as a useful early-warning signal, while avoiding overinterpretation. Tapir illustrates this point clearly: presence near infrastructure would not reveal the magnitude of its activity shift. At the same time, jaguar, puma, and many mesopredators did not show strong monotonic detection-night responses, meaning that no single species or response variable can represent the entire community. Monitoring should therefore include multiple focal taxa with different trophic roles, body sizes, and vulnerability profiles, and should evaluate both local detection and activity timing. Directional but uncertain results, such as the brocket deer detection-night trend, can also be useful for prioritizing future monitoring because they identify patterns that may strengthen with additional data. The study also has important limitations. Distance from infrastructure is an index of human pressure, not a direct measure of human activity. It does not directly measure hunting intensity, dog presence, road traffic, river travel, seasonal population changes, or nocturnal human activity. Estimated human population and population-by-distance interactions were evaluated but were not retained in final models, likely because resident population is only a coarse proxy for the forms of human pressure most relevant to wildlife. Distance to river improved the nocturnality model, suggesting that river corridors are important to temporal activity in this landscape, but river distance may represent both natural ecological gradients and human access. Finally, we could not model distance to smaller hunting trails directly because hunters often follow wildlife trails and informal paths that can shift over time, and a complete trail-distance layer was not available across all grids and years. Instead, we included trail placement as a binary station-level covariate to account for detection differences between trail-based and off-trail cameras. Some taxonomic and sampling limitations also remain. Several taxa were grouped because species-level identification was not always reliable from infrared camera-trap videos, including brocket deer, small rodents, mouse opossums, and small armadillos. The brocket deer result is therefore important but should be interpreted with caution, because sympatric brocket deer species can differ in baseline activity (de Pinho et al., 2021). Survey grids also differed in year, location, spacing, duration, and original sampling objective. The models accounted for grid, camera station, trail placement, spatial structure, and species-level partial pooling, but this heterogeneity remains part of the study design. Scale and sample size also affect how these results should be interpreted. The primary 0.85–7.20 km near–far contrast summarizes station-level responses across heterogeneous survey grids, but it does not capture home-range-scale movement, fine-scale avoidance of people, or day–night shifts in where animals occur within the landscape. Rare species and species unevenly represented near one end of the distance gradient had wider intervals, meaning that nonsignificant contrasts for these taxa should not be interpreted as evidence of no response. Conversely, near-zero detection-night contrasts for better-sampled taxa such as jaguar and puma suggest an absence of strong monotonic station-level responses, but they do not rule out responses at broader spatial scales, through movement routes, diel timing, prey-mediated pathways, or human-overlap dynamics. Additional data, targeted sampling at distance-gradient extremes, repeated surveys through time, telemetry, occupancy models, or spatial capture–recapture would be needed to separate scale-dependent behavioral responses from demographic effects. Importantly, these responses were detected around relatively small and recently established riverine communities. Puerto Lucerna was established only approximately a decade ago, and Puerto Nuevo approximately two decades ago, and both remain small compared with many Amazonian river settlements, with resident populations roughly in the hundreds and seasonal fluctuations in occupancy. Even under these conditions, we detected clear behavioral and local-use signals in several taxa. This suggests that measurable changes in mammal activity and local use can emerge early in the development of forest-frontier settlements, before communities become large urban centers or before deforestation becomes extensive. In landscapes with larger settlements, heavier hunting pressure, more roads, greater agricultural expansion, logging, dogs, and more persistent river or road traffic, infrastructure-associated effects on mammal communities are likely to be stronger and more widespread. Continued monitoring in Las Piedras can therefore provide an early-warning baseline for understanding how Amazonian mammal communities respond as human access intensifies. Overall, this study shows that mammal responses to infrastructure in Las Piedras are already detectable but uneven across both species and behavioral dimensions. The strongest supported temporal response was increased lowland tapir nocturnality near infrastructure. The clearest detection-night responses were higher predicted local use nearer infrastructure for small rodents and farther from infrastructure for collared peccary, with brocket deer showing a similar positive but less certain trend. Tayra showed a small, supported increase in local detection nearer infrastructure, while many carnivores did not show strong monotonic detection-night responses. The main contribution of this study is therefore not that all mammals become more nocturnal or avoid infrastructure, but that infrastructure-associated responses are concentrated in particular taxa and expressed through different combinations of time and local use. In Amazonian forest frontiers, conservation monitoring should ask not only whether mammals remain present, but how, when, and where they continue to use human-influenced landscapes.
