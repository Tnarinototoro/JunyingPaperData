Local perceptions on ostrich conservation in farming areas around Lake Chivero Recreational Park, Zimbabwe

Abstract

The common ostrich (Struthio camelus), despite its global classification as Least Concern, has experienced severe population declines in Zimbabwe, particularly within Lake Chivero Recreational Park (LCRP). This study assessed community perceptions, legal knowledge, and traditional uses of ostriches among 79 households in Maine, Mwashita, and Caroline villages, using a stratified random sampling design covering 28% of the household frame. Weighted estimates indicated that 59.0% of households could identify ostriches and 69.2% considered them important for conservation, yet only 55.7% reported willingness to notify authorities of sightings, revealing a perception–behaviour gap. Correct legal knowledge of wildlife law was critically low (16.9%), with village-level disparities highlighting localized enforcement gaps. Ethnobiological analysis showed moderate diversity of ostrich-related uses (UV = 1.39) and strong consensus on medicinal applications (IAR = 0.905), dominated by bone use (UAV = 68.2%). These findings demonstrate that ostrich conservation in LCRP is constrained by limited legal literacy, persistent traditional practices, socio-economic pressures, and the disconnect between positive attitudes and conservation action. Effective interventions must therefore move beyond awareness-building to strengthen procedural knowledge, institutional trust, and culturally sensitive alternatives, while extending benefit-sharing mechanisms to remote communities. Integrated strategies of this nature are essential to secure the long-term persistence of ostrich populations in Zimbabwe.

Introduction

The ostrich is the largest extant avian taxon and serves as a critical mega-herbivore within the arid and semi-arid ecosystems of Africa (Cooper et al., 2010). Historically, these ratites have been exploited throughout their range for both subsistence and commercial purposes, including the trade of feathers, skin, and meat (Kummrow, 2015; SECRETARIAT, C. I. T. E. S. 2023). However, beyond their economic value, ostriches are ecosystem engineers that facilitate seed dispersal and maintain plant community structures through grazing and trampling (Rugemalila, 2021). The existing ornithological classification identifies two distinct species, the common ostrich (Struthio camelus) and the Somali ostrich (Struthio molybdophanes), which were elevated to species status due to significant reproductive and genetic isolation (Freitag and Robinson, 1993; BirdLife International, 2014; Folch et al., 2020). Within S. camelus, distinct subspecies exist, often categorized by skin pigmentation, the red-necked varieties (S. camelus camelus and S. camelus massaicus) and the blue-necked (S. camelus australis), each occupying specific ecological niches across the African continent (Cooper et al., 2010; BirdLife International, 2014). The South African black ostrich (S. camelus var. domesticus) remains a commercially selected hybrid, distinct from wild lineages (Brand, 2006).

Despite their iconic status, local poaching, international trafficking, and rapid habitat degradation have placed increasing pressure on wild ostrich populations in Africa (African Wildlife Foundation, 2013; SECRETARIAT, C. I. T. E. S. 2023; BirdLife International, 2018). The common ostrich is an important part of Zimbabwe’s highveld and lowveld wildlife, supporting the nation’s trophy hunting and photographic tourist sector as well as the ecological integrity of protected areas (Cooper et al., 2010). Zimbabwe used to have healthy wild populations in national parks, recreational parks and private conservancies, but because of land-use pressure and past changes in agricultural policy, these populations are now increasingly restricted to protected enclaves (Wolmer et al., 2004).

The ostrich’s protection is under the main provisions of the Parks and Wildlife Act [Chapter 20:14] and not under a specific protected designation in Zimbabwe (ZPWMA, 2024). After the 2020 amendment to the part related to Mammalia in the Sixth Schedule of the Specially Protected Animals, ostriches are no longer listed in this schedule (ZPWMA, 2024). Rather, their exploitation is regulated by Part XII of the Act governing the hunting, removal and sale of animals and animal products. This refers specifically to the offences of hunting any animal on any land without a permit [Section 59(2)(a)] and the removal of any animal or part of any animal from any land without authorization [Section 59(2)(b)]. Section 63 also provides a reporting requirement for the killing or harm of animals, and outlines who is responsible for reporting. In the context of Lake Chivero, these provisions are of direct relevance as it is a boundary town between the recreational park and the adjoining farms, thus providing a conducive environment for illegal hunting and removal (ZPWMA, 2024). It remains unclear, however, whether communities near the park know about, and respect, these separate provisions, given this legal structure (ZPWMA, 2024).

Lake Chivero Recreational Park (LCRP) is a peri-urban protected area in Zimbabwe and ostriches were historically stocked in the park to preserve its reputation as a top sentinel environment for avian biodiversity (Chiweshe and Caron, 2012; Mwale et al., 2025). Nevertheless, their population has experienced severe localized challenges (Chiweshe and Caron, 2012; ZPWMA, 2024). The park’s close vicinity to growing agricultural farms and communal areas like Maine, Mwashita, and Caroline has resulted in a complicated human-wildlife interface where the birds are susceptible to habitat encroachment and household disturbances (Chiweshe and Caron, 2012; ZPWMA, 2024). Crucially, these farms have established communities with permanent residents, whose households uphold long-term livelihoods and cultural customs, rather than transitory worker compounds (Chiweshe and Caron, 2012; Mwale et al., 2025).

Although published population estimates are lacking, observations by park management indicate that the Common ostrich (Struthio camelus) population in Lake Chivero Recreational Park has declined in recent years (Mwale et al., 2025). This apparent decline coincides with increasing habitat degradation, bush encroachment, anthropogenic disturbance, and other ecological pressures documented within the park (Mwale et al., 2025; Tendaupenyu et al., 2025). However, there is inadequate empirical evidence to determine if conservation behaviour, compliance with wildlife legislation, and community awareness, attitudes, and sociodemographic characteristics influence ostrich conservation in LCRP (SECRETARIAT, C. I. T. E. S. 2023; Cooper, 2025). Consequently, understanding the social dimensions of conservation is essential, as effective ostrich conservation requires an integrated approach that considers both ecological processes and human behaviour (Bennett et al., 2017). Addressing these knowledge gaps is critical for informing evidence-based conservation strategies and promoting the long-term persistence of ostrich populations in LCRP. Without this information, conservation efforts run the danger of failing because of limited legal understanding, minimal community involvement, and ongoing reliance on wildlife resources for livelihood and cultural purposes (Muboko and Murindagomo, 2014; Matseketsa et al., 2019).

While community attitudes towards ostrich conservation have been studied elsewhere (e.g., Magige and Røskaft, 2017; Mamadou et al., 2020), it is crucial to examine public legislation about ostriches in Zimbabwe to direct future efforts to raise awareness. This study i) assessed community perceptions and attitudes, ii) evaluated the level of legal knowledge towards ostrich conservation, and iii) quantified traditional cultural uses and values of ostriches around Lake Chivero Recreational Park in Zimbabwe. We hypothesised no significant relationship between community perceptions, legal knowledge, and traditional use of ostriches and conservation behaviour among local communities surrounding the LCRP.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted in the communal areas of Maine Farm, Mwashita Farm, and Caroline Farm, which border Lake Chivero Recreational Park (Figure 1). The protected area has a porous game fence which separates it from communal land by a buffer zone, being a road, thus, resulting in poaching (ZPWMA, 2024).

Data collection

The sampling frame comprised 282 permanent households across the three villages: 100 in Maine, 60 in Mwashita, and 122 in Caroline, drawn from village head registers.

A stratified random sampling design was used with villages as strata. The sample size was determined using Yamane’s formula n = N/(1 + N(e)2) at a 10% margin of error, resulting in a minimum of 74 households. To allow for non-response, we targeted 82 households and obtained 79 completed questionnaires, a 96.3% response rate and 28.0% coverage of the total household population. Within each village, we randomly selected households from registers using a random number generator. Household heads were the primary respondents and, in their absence, the next adult resident aged 18 years or older was interviewed after two follow-up visits. One respondent was interviewed per sampled household. Distance from each household to the nearest park boundary was measured using GPS coordinates collected during interviews and analyzed in QGIS using the park boundary shapefile. Households were categorized into near zone (≤10 km) and far zone (15–20 km) based on thresholds validated through community focus group discussions and key informant consultation. Data were collected from August 2024 to July 2025 using structured questionnaires (Appendix 1) administered through face-to-face interviews in Shona. The questionnaire captured demographic information, knowledge and awareness of ostriches, legal knowledge regarding the Parks and Wildlife Act Chapter 20:14, and traditional uses and values of ostrich products. Correct legal knowledge was defined as correctly identifying at least two of three legal provisions regarding ostrich protection, reporting obligations, and trade prohibitions.

Informed consent was obtained verbally from all participants, and anonymity was assured. The study was approved by the Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority research committee and adhered to Belmont Report ethical standards.

Data analysis

All data were coded, cleaned, and analysed using R statistical software, version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2024). An analysis was conducted that involved making descriptive estimates of the achieved sample and population-level estimates, including the stratified sampling design.

Weighted estimates that highlight differences between the two i.e. descriptive and design emphasis. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarise the demographic characteristics, knowledge and awareness of ostriches, perception of ostrich conservation, reporting behaviour, and traditional beliefs and knowledge of ostrich use of the respondents. The categories were presented with percentages and frequencies while continuous and index variables were summarised by appropriate descriptive statistics.

The respondents’ identification of an ostrich was tested by asking them Question C1 (see Appendix 1) in the questionnaire: “Can you identify an ostrich?” Yes or No was recorded as a response. The answer “Yes” meant that the respondent said that he or she could see an ostrich, and “No” meant that the respondent did not report being able to see one. This variable was taken to mean ability to identify the species as self-reported ostrich identification ability as opposed to general knowledge. This is because the ability to identify ostriches is a skill that can be learned, whereas reported sightings are not. A separate variable was created, “Sighting of Ostrich in community”, to determine if participants reported seeing an ostrich and/or many ostriches in their community. Therefore, the identification ability and sighting were analyzed separately.

Stratum-specific design weights were computed and used when calculating pooled population-level proportions in the analysis of the data in R, as the sampling fractions were different for the three villages. The sampling strata were the three villages: Maine with a population of 100 households, Mwashita with 60 households, and Caroline with 122 households, giving samples of 26, 26, and 27 households, respectively. We computed weighted population proportions for binary and categorical household-level variables in R, using design weights as per the ratio-form design-weighted estimator. The calculations were performed in R using the survey package, and the village was defined as the sampling stratum, while the base weights were defined as the stratum-specific sampling weights, and the size of the stratum populations was defined as finite population correction (FPC). The weighted point estimates and the sampling variances were calculated to reflect the unequal sampling fraction for the three villages.

The 79 households (28%) sampled from 282 households in the sampling frame resulted in FPCs being applied at the stratum level in R for estimation of the precision of population-level estimates because the sampling fractions varied between the villages. Therefore, 95% confidence intervals for weighted estimates did include these factors: unequal sampling fractions, finite population structure, and stratified design.

Weighted estimates were calculated where the purpose of the estimate was to estimate values for the pooled characteristics across the 282-household sampling frame. Percentages were calculated within each village to reflect the achieved respondents within each village, and were retained as unweighted sample percentages, in contrast. Direct estimates from the 79 respondents are listed as sample percentages and estimates generated with the R survey design and sampling weights are listed as design-weighted population estimates throughout the results.

Village comparisons

The categorical variables were first compared among the villages using Pearson’s chi-square test with sufficiently large cell frequencies. Exact tests were used in areas where few or no cells were present. Due to low cell frequencies (expected <5) and an absence of respondents with correct legal knowledge in one village, the association between village and correct legal knowledge was evaluated with the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test with a Monte Carlo procedure of (10^6) replications.

Cramér’s V was used to report effect sizes for contingency-table associations. These village-level comparisons were analyzed as an analysis of achieved sample and not as design-weighted population level hypothesis tests.

Legal associations, exploratory links to legal knowledge

Legal knowledge was codified as either correct or incorrect, where respondents who correctly identified at least two of three given legal provisions were treated as having correct legal knowledge. Conventional multivariable logistic regression was not performed because only 13 of the 79 respondents (16.5%) met this criterion and no respondents in the Maine stratum had correct legal knowledge. The very few outcomes and absence of outcomes in one or more strata may cause unstable estimates and separation in an ordinary logistic regression model.

Relationships between legal knowledge were examined instead with exploratory bivariate point-biserial correlations in R, using indicator variables with explicitly defined reference categories for binary explanatory variables. These analyses were considered as unadjusted bivariate associations and were interpreted as not indicative of independent predictors, causal or multivariable effects.

Eleven exploratory associations were tested and Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was used to control multiple testing at (q<0.05). Both raw (p)-value and FDR adjusted (q)-value were provided. The correlation coefficients were followed by 95% confidence interval based on Fisher’s (z)-transformation. The achieved sample was used for these exploratory bivariate analyses, and so was not design-weighted for the population level.

Interpretation and reporting

Throughout the analysis, the distinction between the sample-level and population-level estimates was kept. The 79 respondents to the survey are represented by unweighted percentages, while design-weighted percentages calculated in R represent the same characteristics in the 282-household sampling frame. Confidence intervals for weighted estimates were computed when weighted estimates were provided using the stratified survey design with the provided sampling weights and finite population corrections.

All inferential analyses that did not specifically call themselves design-weighted survey estimates were assumed to be unweighted analyses of the achieved sample. It was thus, concluded that the findings of significant bivariate relations with legal knowledge were treated as associations and not predictions. Independent, causal or multivariable interpretation was not assigned to these relationships.

Results

Demographic profile of respondents

A total of 79 respondents participated in this study, of which the gender ratio was relatively even, with 51.9% females and 48.1% males. Most of the cohort were young (53.2% aged between 18 and 40 years). Socio-economic characteristics revealed that most respondents had completed secondary school education or above (49.4%) and were employed (48.1%). Ostrich identification ability in LCRP

In the achieved sample, 49 of 79 respondents (62%) reported that they could identify an ostrich (Table 2). The proportion was highest in Mwashita (21/26; 80.8%), followed by Caroline (15/27; 55.6%) and Maine (13/26; 50%). A Pearson chi-square test showed no statistically significant difference in reported ostrich identification ability among the three villages (χ2 = 5.95, p=.051), with a Cramér’s V (V = 0.275), indicating a small-to-moderate association. Because the three villages were sampled at different fractions, stratum-specific base design weights were applied to obtain a population-level estimate. The resulting design-weighted estimate indicated that 59.0% of households in the 282-household sampling frame reported being able to identify an ostrich (95% CI: 49.2–68.7%).

Perceived importance of ostrich for conservation

The weighted population estimate indicated that 69.2% of households considered ostriches important for conservation, while 21.6% considered them unimportant and 9.2% were neutral. Most respondents in the observed sample (68.4%, n=54, 95% CI [57.4%, 77.6%]) considered ostriches important for conservation purposes. A smaller proportion (21.5%, n=17) indicated that ostriches were not important, while 10.1% (n=8) remained neutral (Table 3).

Reporting behaviour and conservation action

The design-weighted population estimate indicated that 55.4% of households would report an ostrich sighting to the relevant authorities, while 44.6% would take no action. Reporting to ZIMPARKS was the predominant action, representing 54.5% (n=24) of responses. Leaving ostriches undisturbed accounted for 31.8% (n=14), while no action (9.1%, n=4) and reporting to local leaders (4.5%, n=2) constituted minority responses. Village-level analysis showed relatively consistent reporting behaviour across all three villages (Table 4); the omnibus comparison was not statistically significant (χ² = 0.08, df = 2, p = 0.961, Cramér’s V = 0.032).

Legal knowledge concerning ostrich conservation

From sampled respondents, 13 individuals (16.5%, 95% CI [9.8%, 26.1%]) demonstrated correct legal knowledge about ostrich conservation. The weighted population-level estimate was 16.9% (95% CI: 9.7–24.2%), FPC-adjusted. Village-level variation was observed, with Caroline recording the highest proportion of correct legal knowledge (29.6%, n = 8), followed by Mwashita (19.2%, n = 5), while no respondent in Maine demonstrated correct legal knowledge (Table 5). Expected cell counts fell below five in all villages and one cell contained zero events; the association between village and legal knowledge was not statistically significant (Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact p = 0.061, Cramér’s V = 0.290).

Five associations remained below the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate threshold (q < 0.05) e.g., male sex, having sighted an ostrich, elderly age (61+ years), belief in myths, and distance from the park. Belief in myths (r = 0.245, raw p = 0.032, q = 0.044) and far distance from the park (r = −0.234, raw p = 0.039, q = 0.044) were marginal (Table 6) All other associations were non-significant before and after FDR control. Because these are unadjusted bivariate correlations, they cannot distinguish independent effects from confounding; in particular, village, distance, age, sex, education, and prior ostrich exposure are intercorrelated in this sample.

Correlates of traditional knowledge

Correlation analysis among the knowledge components revealed that medical use mentioned had the strongest positive association with overall use count (r = 0.63, p < 0.001), followed by knowledge of community use, which also showed a strong correlation with overall use count (r = 0.64, p < 0.001). Moderate positive correlations were observed between medical use mentioned and knowledge of medical trade (r = 0.43, p < 0.001), as well as between knowledge of medical trade and overall use count (r = 0.56, p < 0.001), suggesting that traditional medical knowledge is associated with awareness of commercial trade (Figure 3). Weak positive correlations existed between medical use mentioned and myth/belief (r = 0.32, p = 0.005), and between knowledge of medical trade and myth/belief (r = 0.31, p = 0.007). Knowledge of community use showed no significant correlation with myth and belief (r = −0.05, p = 0.664), indicating these knowledge domains operate independently (Figure 3).

Discussion

The perception-behaviour gap

There was clear evidence of a disconnect between community perceptions of conservation importance of the ostrich and the actual conservation actions. 68.4% of respondents saw ostriches as important for conservation while only 55.7% said they would report sightings to authorities. The perception–behaviour gap reflects results from other Zimbabwean protected areas (Makumbe et al., 2022; Matseketsa et al., 2019) and is similar to other conservation social science studies around the world that warn against equating positive attitudes with action or compliance (Kollmuss and Agyeman, 2002; Bennett et al., 2017; Manyama and Gayo, 2025). This shortfall will hamper the effectiveness of community-based monitoring in Lake Chivero, as the ostrich population has reduced from over 100 in 2020 to less than 20 in 2025 (ZPWMA, 2024; Cooper, 2025).

In our study, the neutrality of the demographic prediction variables differs from other studies that have shown that age, gender or education can have a marked effect on conservation engagement (Geto, 2022; Buba, 2025). This implies that the gap is not simply found among subgroups of peri-urban households but widespread, thus emphasising the need to target interventions at entire communities. Awareness is not enough to ensure compliance, and building of procedural knowledge and institutional trust is essential (Shoko et al., 2026). Furthermore, low awareness of legal provisions of Parks and Wildlife Act (PWA) (16.9% of households had the correct information) was indicative of an implementation gap as opposed to a legislative gap (Muboko and Murindagomo, 2014). Laws that communities do not know cannot be enforced, and an effective enforcement strategy cannot be wholly a policing strategy.

This gap is further complicated by the socio-economic pressures. Enforcement is not as strong in remote communities and access to conservation benefits is weak, meaning that they are more dependent on wildlife resources (Child, 2012; Muzvondiwa, 2022; Utete, 2024). The use of traditional activities, such as collecting bones for medicinal purposes, is still entrenched in culture and can contribute to population declines (Alves and Rosa, 2013; Muvengwi and Maroyi, 2025). However, the information contained in cultural knowledge systems can coexist with conservation law, if not complement it, as evidenced by the positive correlation between traditional beliefs and legal knowledge in our study, which aligns with the broader ethnobiological findings (Berkes, 2026).

Put together, these results highlight the need to shift beyond awareness raising to move to procedural learning, institutional trust and culturally adapted alternatives to unsustainable practices. If they are not taken, then positive sentiment could remain unfulfilled and the potential to engage local communities meaningfully in ostrich conservation would be reduced.

Correlates of legal knowledge

The low percentage of correct legal knowledge observed in this study (16.9%) indicates a need for critical implementation gap and not a legislative gap. The Act already has the framework in place, including permit requirements under Section 59(2)(a) and (b), and reporting requirements under Section 63, but community knowledge of these two provisions is quite limited. This is important because it is key to enforcement to rely on community cooperation, and where communities do not know what they need to do to report violations, it is less likely that they will. This is not something that can be achieved through policing alone, however, but through specific education for conservation that engages communities in a legal context, not just in being aware of the law. This would dovetail with Zimbabwe’s CAMPFIRE approach and the education aspects of the National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan and would help build the procedural knowledge such as the permit requirements and reporting obligations, and the consequences of non-compliance, which the present findings indicate are absent. Conservation outreach programs must therefore adopt gender-sensitive approaches that intentionally include women in legal literacy initiatives.

Personal experience, specifically having sighted an ostrich, was positively associated with legal knowledge (r = 0.312, 95% CI [0.09, 0.50], raw p = 0.008, q = 0.015), suggesting that direct interaction with the species may catalyze learning about its protected status. This finding has important implications for conservation education: programs that provide opportunities for communities to see and learn about ostriches may simultaneously increase legal awareness (Muboko and Murindagomo, 2014). The negative correlation between elderly age (61+ years) and legal knowledge (r = −0.289, 95% CI [−0.48, −0.07], raw p = 0.010, q = 0.015) indicates that older community members are particularly vulnerable to legal ignorance. This may reflect lower educational attainment among older generations or reduced engagement with formal institutions (Muvengwi and Maroyi, 2025). Targeted legal literacy programs for elderly residents, perhaps delivered through community elders’ forums or church groups, could address this gap.

Distance from the park boundary showed a weak negative bivariate association with legal knowledge (r = −0.234, raw p = 0.039, q = 0.044), with communities farther from the park (15–20 km) showing lower legal awareness. However, this association was marginal and did not remain significant after strict FDR control at a more conservative threshold; it should be treated as exploratory. The pattern aligns with the concept of conservation proximity effects, where communities closer to protected areas receive more information and encounter more enforcement (Naughton-Treves et al., 2005; Muzvondiwa et al., 2022), but confounding by village cannot be ruled out. Belief in myths showed a modest positive correlation with legal knowledge (r = 0.245, raw p = 0.032, q = 0.044), challenging the assumption that traditional beliefs necessarily undermine legal understanding. This suggests that cultural knowledge systems and formal legal awareness can coexist, a finding supported by broader African ethnobiology literature (Berkes, 2026). However, this association was also marginal and should be interpreted cautiously.

Limitations of legal knowledge analysis. Because only 13 of 79 respondents demonstrated correct legal knowledge, with zero events in Maine, conventional multivariable logistic regression was not feasible without penalization. The analysis presented here is therefore exploratory and bivariate; no independent or causal effects are inferred. The zero-event cell for Maine also produces complete separation in any unpenalized logistic model. Future research with larger samples should consider Firth penalized logistic regression to identify independent correlates of legal knowledge.

The persistence of traditional uses

The ethnobiological indices reveal that traditional knowledge of ostrich uses remains embedded within the LCRP community, with moderate use diversity (UV = 1.39) and strong consensus on key applications (IAR = 0.905 for medicinal uses). The Use Agreement Value (UAV) of 68.2% for ostrich bones in wound treatment indicates a particularly strong cultural preference for this specific product, suggesting that traditional medical practices are actively maintained knowledge systems (Muvengwi and Maroyi, 2025; Alves and Rosa, 2013). The low overall awareness of ostrich trade for medical purposes (17.7%) suggests that most traditional use is subsistence-based rather than commercial. This finding is encouraging from a conservation perspective, as commercial trade poses a greater threat to wildlife populations than localized subsistence use (Secretariat, CITES, 2023). However, the association between knowledge of medical uses and awareness of trade (r = 0.43, p < 0.001) indicates overlap; respondents who use ostrich products are more aware of their market value, raising the potential for escalation from subsistence use to commercial exploitation if economic pressures increase (Muboko and Murindagomo, 2014).

The economic rationale for ostrich use (UAV = 47.6% of perceived importance mentions) exceeded even medicinal applications, indicating that financial benefits, whether real or perceived, are a primary driver of traditional use. This aligns with broader conservation literature demonstrating that economic incentives often override cultural or ecological motivations in resource use decisions (Naughton-Treves et al., 2005; Bennett et al., 2017). For LCRP communities, some respondents are engaged in formal employment (48.1%) but facing economic uncertainty. Thus, ostrich products may represent an accessible supplementary income source (Muzvondiwa, 2022).

The type of use matters for conservation impact. Bone collection requires killing ostriches, whereas feather collection could be non-lethal if conducted sustainably (Allen et al., 2023). The predominance of bone use (68.2% of medicinal-use mentions) thus indicates a potential lethal threat to ostrich populations. Conservation interventions should prioritize alternatives to bone-based treatments, such as promoting non-wildlife medicines or sustainable feather collection (Muvengwi and Maroyi, 2025). The finding that traditional beliefs do not undermine legal knowledge (r = 0.245, raw p = 0.032) challenges the common narrative that cultural practices necessarily conflict with conservation (Berkes, 2026). Engagement with traditional healers could provide opportunities to discuss sustainable alternatives to ostrich derivatives.

Geographic patterns in conservation engagement

Caroline village recorded the highest UV (1.519) and the highest legal knowledge (29.6%), while Maine showed lower UV (1.269) and no correct legal knowledge. This counterintuitive pattern warrants careful interpretation. One possible explanation, which was not directly tested in this study, is that communities further from the park experience weaker enforcement of wildlife laws, reducing the perceived risk of using ostrich products (Muzvondiwa, 2022). If ranger patrols and enforcement activities are concentrated near the park boundary, remote communities may face lower oversight and thus greater freedom to exploit wildlife resources. This interpretation is supported by the finding that legal knowledge was lower among remote communities, suggesting less information about protected species (Naughton-Treves et al., 2005).

Reduced access to alternative resources may increase reliance on wildlife in more distant communities. For communities without secure resource rights or benefit-sharing mechanisms, where economic incentives may be absent, continued reliance on subsistence-based resource extraction is likely (Child, 2012). Communities very close to the park may derive benefits from tourism or conservation-related activities, while remote communities may have fewer livelihood options (Muboko and Murindagomo, 2014; Bennett et al., 2017). The higher UV in Caroline compared to Maine supports this interpretation, though socioeconomic factors were not directly measured, and the UV differences were not formally tested. Utete (2024) found that communities adjacent to protected areas in Zimbabwe often benefit from conservation-related employment and tourism revenue, while more distant communities experience fewer benefits but bear similar costs of wildlife presence, creating inequities that undermine conservation support. This finding suggests that benefit-sharing mechanisms must be extended to remote communities to reduce their dependence on wildlife resources.

The positive association between traditional beliefs and legal knowledge suggests that cultural knowledge systems and formal legal awareness can coexist, a finding supported by broader African ethnobiology literature (Muvengwi and Maroyi, 2025). This challenges the assumption that traditional beliefs necessarily undermine legal understanding. The geographic patterns observed in this study highlight the complexity of human–wildlife interactions in peri-urban landscapes. Simple assumptions about proximity effects may not hold where multiple factors interact to shape conservation engagement.

Implications for ostrich conservation

Synthesized, the findings paint a picture of ostrich conservation in the LCRP as a complex socio-ecological challenge where positive perceptions coexist with low legal knowledge, persistent traditional uses, and geographic disparities in conservation engagement. The reported population decline from over 100 individuals in 2020 to fewer than 20 in 2025 is likely driven by habitat loss, poaching, and environmental change (Child, 2019; Muzvondiwa et al., 2022), but human dimensions, particularly limited legal awareness and continued traditional use, may be contributing factors.

The perception–behaviour gap indicates that communities are not opposed to conservation; they recognize ostrich importance (68.4%) and express willingness to report sightings (55.7%). However, positive sentiment does not reliably translate into conservation action, suggesting interventions must move beyond awareness-raising to address barriers to action including limited legal knowledge and competing economic priorities (Manyama and Gayo, 2025; Bennett et al., 2017). Strengthening ranger-community relationships and demonstrating the effectiveness of reporting mechanisms could help bridge this gap (Muboko and Murindagomo, 2014).

The critically low legal knowledge (16.5%) is a priority concern. The finding that legal awareness varies by gender, age, and proximity to the park suggests that targeted education programs are needed for women, elderly residents, and remote communities (Muzvondiwa, 2022). Conservation authorities should partner with community organizations, schools, and traditional institutions to deliver legal literacy programs specifically addressing the Parks and Wildlife Act Chapter 20:14.

The persistence of traditional uses, particularly of ostrich bones for medicinal purposes (68.2% of medicinal-use mentions), highlights the need to integrate cultural values into conservation planning. Rather than simply prohibiting traditional practices, conservation programs should engage traditional healers in discussing sustainable alternatives to ostrich derivatives (Alves and Rosa, 2013; Muvengwi and Maroyi, 2025). The positive association between traditional beliefs and legal knowledge suggests cultural systems could become allies in conservation rather than obstacles (Berkes, 2026).

Geographic disparities suggest a one-size-fits-all approach to conservation education is unlikely to succeed. Communities closer to the park may benefit from strengthened enforcement and reporting mechanisms, while remote communities may require outreach addressing their higher dependence on wildlife resources and lower legal awareness (Muzvondiwa, 2022). Conservation programs should be decentralized and context-specific, recognizing that different communities have different needs and priorities. Utete (2024) emphasized that successful conservation in Zimbabwe requires integrated, multi-stakeholder approaches that address socio-economic drivers of wildlife exploitation while building institutional trust and legal awareness. Conservation strategies should move beyond enforcement by integrating local ecological knowledge and engaging traditional healers to discuss sustainable alternatives to ostrich derivatives. Strengthening ranger–community relationships are vital, as personal sightings were associated with higher legal knowledge. Leveraging existing positive perceptions of the ostrich’s economic and educational value could enhance community-led monitoring and stewardship programs aligned with local livelihoods and the protection of this relic population.

Conclusion and recommendations

Ostrich conservation in the LCRP is shaped by a complex interaction of perceptions, knowledge, and cultural practices. While communities generally support conservation in principle, this support is undermined by low legal awareness and continued reliance on traditional uses (Owusu Danquah et al., 2026). Ostrich awareness outreach should move beyond fostering basic species recognition and focus on building the procedural knowledge and institutional trust required for active community participation in formal monitoring. Wildlife authorities should implement targeted legal literacy programs that prioritize education on the Parks and Wildlife Act [Chapter 20:14] over general biological information.

These interventions must be demographically and geographically focused on elderly residents and remote communities located further from the park boundary, as these specific groups currently exhibit the lowest levels of legal awareness regarding the bird’s protected status.

Conservation strategies must move beyond a purely enforcement-based approach by integrating local ecological knowledge; specifically, engaging with traditional healers to discuss sustainable alternatives to ostrich derivatives for medicinal uses. Strengthening the relationship between rangers and the community is vital, as personal sightings were a key driver of higher legal knowledge. Leveraging on existing local positive perception of the ostrich’s economic and educational enhance community-led monitoring and stewardship programs aligned with local livelihoods with the protection of this relic population.
