Among the advanced techniques for wastewater treatment, emulsion liquid membranes (ELM) stand out, since it is environmentally friendly. In the present work, flupentixol (FL), an emergent micropollutant and antidepressant, was selected as the model pharmaceutical pollutant for removal using ELM system containing jujube oil (JJO-ELM). The primary effects and interactions of key independent variables, surfactant concentration (2–3 v/v %), carrier concentration (3–5 v/v %), concentration of stripping agent (HCl), (0.5–1.5 N), FL concentration (50–150 mg/L), and phase ratio (1:1–1:3 for water:JJO, respectively) were optimized using the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) design components. Optimized results showed that the ideal condition for the independent parameters, surfactant concentration; carrier concentration, HCl concentration, flupentixol concentration, and a phase ratio, respectively, were determined to be 2.41% v/v, 3.70% v/v, 0.52 N, 143.84 mg/L, and 1:1.5. The ideal condition could result in a maximum extraction efficiency of 97.40% FL. Further, the effectiveness of FL stripping was examined by adjusting the H 2 SO 4 concentration. Investigations were made into the mechanism of flupentixol extraction by JJO-ELM. The feed forward neural network (FNN) model, which has an output layer with one (flupentixol extraction) neuron and an input layer with five neurons, was used to simulate the extraction data. Biomethanol Liquid-liquid extraction Ionic Liquids Solid-phase microextraction Separation Science Liquid chromatography 1 Introduction Emerging pharmaceutical contaminants (EPCs) have recently been increasingly detected in aquatic environments at concentrations high enough that can cause harmful effects to the healthy environment [ 1 ]. Pharmaceutical pollutants, which have been frequently reported in the ng/L to µg/L range, are among the most extensively studied classes of emerging contaminants. In addition to human use, veterinary applications also contribute to the environmental emission of contaminant [ 2 ]. EPCs, such as Acebutolol, Acetaminophen, Atorvastatin, Betaxolol, Bisoprolol, Carbamazepine, Cetirizine, Ciprofloxacin, Diazepam, Diclofenac, Disopyramide, Enrofloxacin, Estradiol, Enoxacin, Fenofibric acid, Flupentixol, Ibuprofen, Indomethacin, Ketoprofen, Lomefloxacin, Lorazepam, Metronidazole, Monensin sodium, Naproxen, Norfloxacin, Tetracycline and Zolpidema are pharmaceutical contaminants in water that are readily detectable and are considered emerging pollutants [ 3 ]. Despite their presence in very low concentrations, these substances pose long-term risks due to their potential to cause chronic health effects. Hence, there is growing concern about the possible “cocktail effects” resulting from the mixture of various types of EPCs [ 4 ]. Flupentixol (FL), a thioxanthene derivative, is regularly used in the medical management of mild to moderately severe depression and irritable bowel syndrome when associated with anxiety-related symptoms [ 5 ]. FL medication is frequently prescribed in the country due to the increasing prevalence of psychiatric illnesses [ 6 ]. As a result, it has been detected in domestic and urban wastewater, highlighting a pressing need for an effective removal process. Several studies continue to explore more effective technology compared to alternative wastewater treatment methods. These alternatives include coagulation, electrochemical processes, adsorption, biosorption, ion exchange, pickering emulsion liquid membranes, and emulsion liquid membranes [ 7 ]. The technology demonstrates superior performance in the removal and recovery of various valuable organic and inorganic pollutants from aqueous solutions. However, despite their widespread use, these techniques have several drawbacks, such as expensive operating expenses, poor effectiveness at low mineral concentrations, a longer processing time, and a higher chemical consumption [ 8 ]. Despite the foregoing, there remains a persistent need to develop a method for treating EPCs that meets both environmental and economic criteria. In this line, both academic and commercial investigators have been particularly interested in emulsion liquid membranes (ELM) for the electrostatic processes of recovering EPC [ 9 ]. Other benefits identified in the ELM system include ease of use, high efficiency, the integration of extraction and stripping processes into a single step, and a larger interfacial area. Compared to traditional methods, ELM has demonstrated an excellent effectiveness in removing EPCs and hydrocarbons from wastewater [ 10 ]. In general, the ELM process utilizes a three-phase format (water/oil/water). The emulsion globule is formed when a constant aqueous phase (W) collides with hydrophobic organic liquid [membrane phase (O)], containing a suspension of stripping agent (W), which is dispersed in the form of small droplets. Owing to its extensive interfacial area, the resulting emulsion globule facilitates enhanced mass transfer between phases. Nevertheless, to ensure the formation and stability of the dispersion, as well as to enhance the membrane’s selectivity and flexibility, an appropriate concentration of surfactant must be added into the organic phase [ 11 ]. Accordingly, during a single operational stage, the FL micropollutant is effectively extracted from the feed phase and subsequently pre-concentrated within the stripping aqueous phase, enhancing overall process efficiency [ 12 ]. During emulsion splitting, electrostatic separation, thermal treatment, reduction, and compression techniques are employed to facilitate the preliminary concentration process. To keep permeation’s driving force constant, the FL micropollutant concentration at the membrane/aqueous interaction needs to be kept at constant. In contrast to solvent-based extraction techniques, the transport of micropollutant in this process governed by ambiguity principles [ 13 ]. As a result, many substances can be treated effectively at a commercial scale, using only a small amount of an expensive carrier. Although the ELM process entails minimal operational costs, a comprehensive understanding of its removal mechanisms is crucial for optimizing its performance [ 14 ]. ELM requires a number of laborious experimental approaches to predict the FL micropollutant elimination effectiveness. The traditional ‘one factor at a time’ method is impractical due to the excessive number of variables and the large amount of data it generates, making interpretation a complex task. To address these challenges, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) has been employed in this study, as it allows for a more efficient and systematic exploration of the process variables, optimizing both data interpretation and experimental design. Since it conserves space, raw material and time, RSM is preferable to conventional multi factors optimization [ 15 ]. In this study, RSM of the Box-Behnken Design (BBD) was employed for experimental design. BBD minimizes systematic errors and reduces the number of experiments, optimizing nonlinear multivariate systems with significant interactions using both large and small data sets, rather than relying solely on quadratic models. To the most effective, this work is unique in that it employs ELM to demonstrate the removal of the FL micropollutant. Furthermore, the investigation was conducted to analyze the combined effects of key factors using the RSM-BBD mathematical model and to predict the removal of the pharmacological micropollutant, FL. A neural network consists of artificial neurons connected by weights (coefficients), and the artificial neural network (ANN) is a machine learning technique inspired by biological models. With interactions bigger than the quadratic range, ANN can handle the optimization of nonlinear multivariate systems. Recent focus has been on comparing RSM with BBD data and ANN to better understand the effects of various process parameters [ 16 ]. In this regard, this paper is unique in that it employs a jujube oil- emulsion liquid membrane system (JJO-ELM) to demonstrate the removal of the FL micropollutant, while utilizing an RSM-BBD mathematical model with ANN to examine the combined effects and optimization of key variables. 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Chemicals used for the prepare the formulation of emulsions Without any additional purification, the compounds and reagents mentioned below were used. Flupentixol [(trifluoromethyl)thioxanthene] group (Molecular formula:C 23 H 25 F 3 N 2 OS, molecular weight: 435.5 g/mole, and ≥90% assay) was investigated as the model contaminant in this study [ 17 ]. It was procured from Sigma Aldrich Pvt, Ltd., India and each other chemical was of analytical grade and was used exactly as it was given. Extra-pure sorbitane monooleate (Span 80) (C 24 H 44 O 6 ) has a molecular mass of 428.6 g/mole. Utilized to keep the emulsion stable Di-(2-Ethyl Hexyl) Phosphoric Acid (D2EHPA) (C 16 H 35 O 4 P), molecular weight: 322.43 g/mole, and has an assay yield of greater than ( > 95.05%) to improve the emulsion’s stability, drops of HCl employed as a stripping phase were added. The jujube oil used in this investigation was picked near the agriculture harvest from Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu, India. 2.2 Extraction of JJO and preparation of JJO-ELM The jujube seeds were thoroughly collected and cleaned to remove contaminants, then subjected to drying at 110 °C for 20 h to eliminate residual moisture before further processing. 25 g of jujube seeds were ground to produce fine particles, approximately 1.25 mm [ 18 ]. Two hydrophilic solvents, n-hexane and ethanol, were used in the extraction procedure with a Soxhlet extractor. Organic solvents vary in polarity, with hexane being the least-polar and ethanol being the moderately polar. Different solvent-water solution concentrations were used at 75%, 50%, and 25% (v/v). A mixture of 100 ml of the solvent and the weighed crushed seeds was prepared. The extraction process was conducted for 4 h at the solvent’s boiling point temperature. The oil was then recovered by separating the oil-solvent mixture using a rotary evaporator, which facilitated nearly complete solvent removal. At 30 °C, the density (Densimeter, EIMCO, India) and digital rotational viscometer (LMDV-60, LABMAN, India) of JJO were determined to be 879,000 kg/m 3 and 3.5 × 10 −5 m 2 /s, respectively. The ELM was made by mixing used Jujube oil in a 250 mL beaker with determined amounts of surfactant (span 80 - 7 mL) and carrier (D2EHPA − 3 mL). A membrane-based solution (natural phase) was created and the components were combined in an electromagnetic separator (Remi, India) for 20 min at 1000 rpm. To improve the dispersion of the organic phase and form an emulsion, 25 mL of 1 N HCl was added dropwise to 12 mL of the organic phase. This also increased contaminants ions’ ability to cross the liquid membrane [ 19 ]. Surfactant carries the charge of stabilizing the interface that exists between aqueous and JJ oil phases thereby preventing re-coalescence of the acid droplets. After 20 min of stirring, a main emulsion a white viscous JJO-ELM was created via the emulsification process [ 20 ]. The overview of JJO-ELM is shown step by step in Fig. 1 . Further characterization of the extracted oil and the prepared JJO-ELM, before and after micropollutant removal, was performed using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy with a Thermo Nicolet Avatar 370 spectrometer. Using a pH meter (Elico, SG780, India), the solution pH was adjusted to 3 by adding an appropriate volume of 0.1 N NaOH or 0.1 N HCl. The primary W/JJO emulsion was first prepared and subsequently dispersed into the external aqueous phase to form a W/JJO/W emulsion system for FL extraction. Then, 10 mL of the prepared emulsion was added to the feed solution under continuous stirring to carry out the contaminant removal study. The mixture was then stirred using a mechanical agitator at 250 rpm for 18 min. The FL ions were transferred into the internal phase after diffusing the oil phase. Then, the extracted solution was collected and filtered to separate the phases. Following sections presents a discussion for influence of each variable and interactions. 3.3.1 Surfactant (span 80) concentration The emulsion’s characteristics in terms of nature and stability are determined by the surfactant used. The predicted outcomes of the surfactant’s influence on %FL extraction are shown in Fig. 5 (a–d). It was apparent that the fundamental nature of Span 80 and its concentration play a significant role in the ELM process. Surfactants are substances usually belong to organic substances that bind to groups that are hydrophobic as well as hydrophilic. As a result, they disperse in both organic and aqueous phases [ 33 ]. To minimize the sharing of water throughout the procedure of extraction, the surfactant must be used appropriately. As the surfactant concentration increases, the stability of the liquid membrane increases as well, progressively slowing down the rate of breakage. A growth in the amount of surfactant still reduces the removal effectiveness because of a higher mass transfer barrier. Herein, for maximal extraction of FL, an optimal Span 80 concentration was needed. The effect of Span 80 concentration was studied in the range of 2–4% (v/v). Fig. 5 Full size image 3D response surface and contour plots for flupentixol extraction onto JJO-ELM Flupentixol (pKa = 11.3) exists predominantly in its protonated form under acidic conditions. An increase in extraction efficiency was observed at both lower (2% v/v) and higher (4% v/v) Span 80 concentrations. This resulted in reduced π-π interactions electron-acceptability of FL, which in turn led to decreased extraction at higher Span 80 concentration (4 v/v%). The relative effect on the reaction was indicated by the coefficient of estimate’s positive sign. However, an adverse estimate coefficient indicates that the response was affected in the opposite way. Therefore, it was also clear from the ANOVA (Table 2 ) negative coefficients of estimate (9.89) that a rise in Span 80 concentration grounds a reduction in flupentixol extraction percentage. The combined effect of surfactant concentration with carrier concentration showed the improvement in FL extraction with carrier concentration (3–5 v/v%). The collective outcome of surfactant concentration and phase ratio showed that flupentixol extraction increased with increase in phase ratio. Perhaps, the combined effect of surfactant concentration and flupentixol concentration only slightly altered in the extraction percentage [ 34 ]. The regression coefficients represent the mathematical contribution of the respective terms to the fitted quadratic response-surface model and should not be interpreted independently as direct indicators of an increase or decrease in FL extraction. Because the model includes linear, quadratic, and interaction terms, the effect of each factor depends on the levels of the other variables within the investigated experimental domain. Therefore, the negative or positive signs of individual coefficients do not necessarily correspond to the overall direction of the experimental response. The effects of carrier concentration, HCl concentration, phase ratio, and FL concentration were consequently interpreted using the complete fitted response surface, significant interaction terms, quadratic effects, and corresponding contour plots. The optimum extraction condition was determined from the combined response of all significant model terms rather than from the sign of any individual regression coefficient. 3.3.2 Carrier (D2EHPA) concentration The carrier concentration affects several aspects of the emulsion liquid membrane, including its stability, extraction efficiency, and selectivity that are shown in Fig. 5 (a, e, f, and g). The stability of the emulsion is crucial for its performance. At low carrier concentrations (3 v/v%), the droplets in the emulsion may coalesce, leading to phase separation and loss of the membrane’s integrity. On other hand, higher carrier concentrations (5 v/v %) can enhance the stability of the emulsion by enlarging the interfacial tension between the dispersed droplets and the continuous phase. The emulsion liquid membrane extraction efficiency depends on the carrier concentration. In general, higher carrier concentrations can lead to higher extraction efficiencies due to increased availability of the carrier molecules for complexation with the target micropollutant concentration. However, there is an optimum carrier concentration beyond which the extraction efficiency may plateau or even decrease due to factors like increased viscosity and inter-droplet mass transfer limitations [ 35 ]. The selectivity of the emulsion liquid membrane refers to its ability to preferentially extract specific micropollutants over others. The carrier concentration can influence selectivity by affecting the competition between different micropollutant for the available carrier sites. Optimal carrier concentrations can be determined experimentally to achieve desired selectivity. Additionally, it was clear from the ANOVA (Table 2 ), negative coefficient of estimate (0.4175) that an increase in carrier concentration (3–5 v/v %) resulted in an increase in FL extraction [ 36 ]. Combined impacts of carrier concentration along with HCl concentration showed a gradual increase in the flupentixol extraction. Collaborative effect of phase ratio and flupentixol concentration showed that the gradual increase in extraction with raise in carrier concentration from 3 to 5 v/v %. 3.3.3 HCl concentration The HCl concentration can affect the emulsion stability by influencing the factors, such as interfacial tension and coalescence of the liquid droplets. The influence of the HCl concentration on FL extractive removal is shown in Fig. 5 (b, e, h, i). In certain cases, the addition of HCl can help improve the stability of the emulsion by enhancing the interfacial tension between the dispersed droplets and the continuous phase. However, excessive HCl concentration can cause destabilization or degradation of the emulsion that may cause phase separation. HCl can play a significant role in the micropollutant extraction from the feed solution. In some cases, HCl is used as a stripping agent to release the extracted pollutant from the carrier phase into the stripping solution. Thus, the extraction of micropollutant increased by increasing the concentration of HCl from 0.5 to 1.5 N. The concentration of HCl in the stripping solution can affect the efficiency of micropollutant transfer from the liquid membrane to the stripping phase. Hence, optimizing the HCl concentration in the stripping solution can help achieve higher recovery rates. Additionally, it was clear from the ANOVA (Table 2 ) negative coefficient of estimate (0.4800) that an increase in carrier concentration (0.5–1.5 N) resulted in an increase in FL removal percentage. The interaction of ELM parameters, HCl concentration, surfactant concentration, and carrier concentration led to an increase in flupentixol extraction with increasing HCl concentration. Perhaps, effect of phase ratio and HCl concentration showed that the slow increase of extraction for the micropollutant [ 37 ]. 3.3.4 Phase ratio The phase ratio can impact the ELM extraction system. In this study, the effect of phase ratio on the efficiency of FL removal is shown in Fig. 5 (d, g, i, and j). Generally, a higher phase ratio can provide a larger interfacial area for mass transfer, allowing for better contact between the target species in the feed solution and the carrier in the liquid membrane phase. Such an increased interfacial area can lead to enhanced extraction efficiency due to higher mass transfer rates. In addition, the phase ratio affects the viscosity of the emulsion. Hence, increase in the phase ratio (1:1–1:3) produces an increased in overall viscosity of the emulsion due to the higher volume fraction of the internal phase. Higher viscosity influences the system hydrodynamics, thereby affecting the factors, such as the interfacial mass transfer and the residence time required for efficient extraction or separation. In addition, it was clear from the ANOVA negative coefficient of estimate (1.23) that an increase in phase ratio (1:1–1.3) resulted in an increase in flupentixol extraction percentage. The combined effect of ELM for the phase ratio along with surfactant and carrier concentration increasing the efficiency of extraction by increase of phase ratio. Along with the HCl concentration and phase ratio indicated the slow increase in extraction of flupentixol. 3.3.5 Concentration of flupentixol Flupentixol micropollutants present in the feed solution can compete with the target species for the available binding sites on the carrier molecules in the liquid membrane phase. This competition can affect the extraction efficiency of the ELM system, as the carrier molecules may preferentially bind to the FL micropollutants instead of the desired target species. The impacts of FL concentration on its removal efficiencies are shown in Fig. 5 (c, f, h, and j). The presence of high concentration of micropollutants can reduce the extraction efficiency and selectivity of the ELM system. FL micropollutants can influence the stability of the emulsion by affecting the interfacial tension between the dispersed droplets and the continuous phase. In addition, micropollutants can alter the properties of the liquid membrane, leading to coalescence or phase separation [ 38 ]. This can result in reduced membrane stability and compromised performance of the ELM system. Furthermore, the positive estimate (0.6550) from the ANOVA results (Table 2 ) indicated that increasing the FL concentration (from 50 to 150 mg/L) could lead to a decrease in the extraction removal percentage. 3.4 ANN modelling In the present work, designed an ANN-based model was designed for investigating the extractive removal flupentixol from an aqueous phase. In this sense, feed-forward neural networks (FNN) - multilayer feed forward networks have been employed in artificial neural network (ANN) model, and they were used to extract flupentixol from 46 data points using 5 components (surfactant concentration [X 1 ], carrier concentration [X 2 ], HCl concentration [X 3 ], FL contaminant concentration [X 4 ] and phase ratio [X 5 ]) as the input information through the input layer. Different methods, topologies, and hidden layers were tested in order to determine the initial number of neurons to be present in the hidden layer for the ANN. For choosing the best architecture to minimize the error function, the mean square error (MSE), neuron number was optimized for better results [ 39 ]. The initial trial data set was divided into three distinct subsets: training set (60% of the initial exploratory data set), testing set (20% of the initial test data set), and validation set (20% of the initial trial data set). A portion of the test data was used to quantify how the neural system operated in anticipation of suppressed information that was not used for preparation and to assess the ANNs’ capacity for prediction. By trial and error, it was determined that the 5–10-1 (Five neurons in the input layer, 10 in the layer that is hidden, and 1 in the result layer) artificial neural network (ANN) design was the most effective one for flupentixol extraction when the mean square error (MSE) gradually reduced and stabilized. This acidic extraction or chemical-based provides some interface action. It traverses a separation and disintegration mechanism at the aqueous-organic layer contact, delivering ions of hydrogen to the water phase [ 41 ]. During flupentixol removal mechanism, the extraction and stripping of flupentixol were illustrated. The method of extraction progressed as following steps. (i) Initially, at the aqueous-membrane interface, the flupentixol form the complex with the carrier by releasing hydrogen ions. The structure of complex for the extraction of flupentixol with D2EHPA was FLX 2 .HX when the micropollutant concentration in the feed phase was low. (ii) Then, the complex of FLX 2 .HX formed was moved to the membrane’s interface, and (iii) the interior phase of concentrated hydrogen chloride strips the FLX 2 .HX complex from the membrane phase into the internal phase to form the flupentixol micropollutant as represented in Eq. ( 5 ) and releases the carrier back to the JJO-ELM membrane phase [Eq. ( 6 )]. The outcomes are depicted in Fig. 8 . In this context, the SE was enhanced by an increase in H 2 SO 4 concentration up to 3 N. However, beyond 3 M of H 2 SO 4 , the stripping efficiency reduced due to swelling of the emulsified granules and an increase in proton gradient concentration, which led to the dissociation of the carrier complex and the subsequent release of the micropollutant ions. Because of the disparity in ionic strength between the internal and exterior phases, membrane swelling and internal phase leakage resulted. For 6 cycles, the recovered membrane was used in the FL extraction tests (Fig. 9 ). The visualisation makes it evident that the FLE (96%) was constant for the process’s first 4 cycles. It was determined using an emulsion stability test that the recovered membrane phase’s emulsion stability decreases after the fourth cycle. The outcome shows that the recovered JJO-ELM remained stable up to cycle 4, after which there was a decrease in FL extraction due to a weak emulsion. JJO-ELM was used to successfully recycle the membrane for 4 cycles while removing pollutant. Fig. 9 Full size image Membrane recycle for FL extraction 4 Conclusion The extraction of flupentixol was studied using JJO-ELM made from used jujube oil. RSM-BBD based design of experiments and analysis were carried out to examine the influence of five significant variables, such as, surfactant concentration, carrier concentration, HCl concentration, FL micropollutant concentration and phase ratio. It was apparent that the parameters significantly affect the extraction of flupentixol. The ideal condition of aforementioned parameters, respectively, 2.41 v/v %, 3.70 v/v %, 0.52 N, 143.84 mg/L and 1:15, resulted in a maximum flupentixol extraction of 97.40%. Through recycle studies, the performance’s repeatability was confirmed. In addition, Flupentixol extraction’s intricate method was investigated. In order to forecast the extraction of flupentixol, feed forward neural network modelling has been effectively used, and the findings demonstrate that RSM-BBD model was more accurate than FNN. Consequently, it can be said that used jujube oil is an environmentally friendly, cost-effective, and sustainable alternative for the JJO-ELM for FL extraction.
