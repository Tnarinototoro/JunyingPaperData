Although roadkill has been widely documented in Colombia, studies in the Caribbean Region, which encompasses key vulnerable ecosystems such as the Tropical Dry Forest (TDF), remain scarce. Here, we assessed whether road characteristics influence the taxonomic composition of vertebrate roadkill on peri-urban roads in the department of Córdoba. We conducted systematic bicycle surveys along three inter-municipal roads over a three-month period during the dry-to-wet transition season, recording species identity and local road characteristics, including artificial lighting near houses, fences, water bodies, traffic signals, and wildlife crossings. We recorded 317 roadkill events, most of which were identified to species (217), with reptiles representing the largest proportion of records, followed by amphibians, mammals, and birds. The taxonomic composition of roadkill records was associated with local road characteristics. Mammal records were more likely on illuminated road sections and in the absence of fences, but less likely near water bodies. Bird records were less likely in the absence of fences and in the presence of wildlife crossings. Amphibian records were more likely in the presence of pole fences and less likely on illuminated road sections, whereas reptile records were more likely near water bodies. These findings indicate that local road characteristics shape the relative representation of vertebrate groups among roadkill records in peri-urban TDF landscapes. Ethology Mammalogy Primatology Biodiversity Tropical Ecology Animal Geography Wildlife Mortality and Road Ecology Impact Assessment Introduction Roads are one of the main drivers of habitat fragmentation and biodiversity loss in terrestrial ecosystems (Forman and Alexander 1998 ; Fahrig and Rytwinski 2009 ). Wildlife roadkill, in particular, poses a direct threat to numerous species, affecting population dynamics, movement patterns, and reproduction, and in some cases driving species from local to regional extinctions (Laurance et al. 2009 ; Teixeira et al. 2013 ; Grilo et al. 2021 ). Although this issue has been widely documented in Latin American countries such as Brazil, Mexico, and Costa Rica, evidence in Colombia remains limited and taxonomically uneven (Angarita-Corzo et al. 2023 ; Sukhontapatipak et al. 2025 ). Studies in the Colombian Caribbean have reported high rates of roadkill along roads bordering tropical dry forest (TDF) remnants, which are areas of high biodiversity (Monroy et al. 2015 ; Adárraga-Caballero et al. 2019 ). Evidence from TDF in the Colombian Caribbean, such as in the Montes de María region, shows high levels of mortality for amphibians, reptiles, and mammals along peri-urban roads, with peaks related to seasonal scarcity of resources (De la Ossa-Nadjar and De la Ossa V 2013 ; 2015 ). In the Colombian Andes it was also shown that nocturnal species and specialized trophic guilds are particularly vulnerable in peri-urban areas with remaining forest cover (Obando-Tobón et al. 2024 ). Tropical dry forest in Colombia is characterized by strong seasonal variation and high biodiversity and is one of the ecosystems most vulnerable to road impacts due to intense anthropogenic pressure (Pizano and García 2014 ). Less than 10% of its original extent remains, concentrated mainly in the Caribbean region and the Magdalena River valley, where agricultural expansion, urbanization, and road infrastructure have been the main drivers of habitat loss (Etter et al. 2008 ; Pizano and García 2014 ). Both formal (state-planned and licensed) and informal (unplanned or illegal private tracks) road construction has accelerated vegetation loss in ecologically valuable and protected areas (MAAP and FCDS 2025 ). Peri-urban roads not only facilitate human access but also act as mortality corridors for many species, especially for reptiles, birds, and small mammals, which are highly susceptible to collisions due to their behaviour, body size, and activity patterns (D’Amico et al. 2015 ). Studies across Latin America have shown that medium-sized mammals and large birds experience the highest roadkill rates, particularly in fragmented habitats (Medrano-Vizcaíno et al. 2022 ; Arévalo-Ayala et al. 2025 ). Moreover, evidence from tropical systems also suggests that ectothermic taxa such as reptiles and amphibians can be disproportionately affected due to behavioural traits such as thermoregulation and low mobility, highlighting potential context-dependent patterns of vulnerability (Rosen and Lowe 1994 ; Vargas-Salinas, et al. 2011 ). Despite these insights, roadkill studies in Colombian TDF remain scarce, and the influence of road features and surrounding environmental variables has rarely been examined. The present study characterizes wildlife roadkill along three inter-municipal roads traversing TDF remnants in the western Colombia Caribbean. Using systematic road surveys, we quantified and evaluated how local road infrastructure and environmental features were associated with the taxonomic composition of roadkill records in peri-urban TDF landscapes. Specifically, we assessed whether the probability that a roadkill record belonged to a given vertebrate class, relative to the remaining classes, varied according to local road and environmental characteristics. We expected that taxonomic composition of vertebrate roadkill would vary according to road and environmental features, reflecting differences in the ecology, movement behaviour, and habitat use of vertebrate groups. Methods Study area and sampling design This study was conducted in the department of Córdoba, in the western Colombian Caribbean, a region that still retains remnants of tropical dry forest (TDF) (Arce-Plata et al. 2026 ). We selected three inter-municipal roads surrounding the urban area of the municipality of Montería with varying traffic intensity and differing degrees of adjacent vegetation cover. The sampled road sections were: (i) Montería - Planeta Rica, (ii) Montería - Cereté, and (iii) Montería - Puerto Escondido (Fig. 1 ). These roads represent distinct gradients of landscape urbanization and surrounding vegetation cover. The Montería - Cereté road exhibits the highest degree of peri-urban development and infrastructure due to the rapid urban expansion corridor connecting both municipalities, featuring high traffic volume and highly fragmented agricultural matrices (Parra-Argel 2026 ). The Montería - Planeta Rica section serves as a major national freight route with high traffic intensity, passing through an intermediate urbanization gradient dominated by an extensive cattle-ranching matrix with scattered tree cover. In contrast, the Montería - Puerto Escondido road features the lowest traffic volume and urbanization level, crossing a rural landscape that retains larger, better-connected structural tropical dry forest in transition toward the coastal zone (Pizano and García 2014 ). Fig. 1 Full size image Map of the study area showing the sampled road sections in the department of Córdoba, Colombian Caribbean We implemented a linear transect sampling design along the roadside, conducting systematic surveys weekly over three consecutive months (April to June) in 2024. The climate around Montería is tropical wet and dry, with an average annual temperature of 28 °C and rainfall concentrated between May and October. During the period from April to June 2024, the region experienced a drastic meteorological transition. According to bulletins from IDEAM ( 2024 ) and CVS ( 2024 ), the quarter began with a thermal anomaly due to the El Niño phenomenon, registering highs of 35 °C and heat indices of 41 °C due to the high humidity. However, towards the end of May and throughout June, the first rainy season took hold due to the passage of tropical waves, generating significant rainfall and flooding in the surrounding rural areas. Surveys were conducted on Sundays to reduce traffic volume, thereby improving observer safety and ensuring a consistent sampling protocol across survey occasions. Each transect covered approximately 15 km per road (~ 45 km in total) and was surveyed by bicycle at a constant speed (~ 10 km/h). The low and standardized survey speed facilitated the detection of small-bodied roadkill, particularly amphibians and reptiles, which are often overlooked during surveys conducted from motorized vehicles (Meek 2009 , Jones et al. 2024 ). During each survey, all vertebrates found as roadkill on the road surface or within 1 m of the pavement edge were recorded. For each individual, we collected the following information: (i) species or the closest possible taxonomic identification performed in the field using specialized regional guides (e.g., Ballesteros-Correa et al. 2019 for amphibians and reptiles; Defler 2010 and Pérez-Gómez et al. 2025 for mammals; Ayerbe-Quiñones 2018 for birds); for highly damaged or decomposed carcasses, high-resolution digital photographs of diagnostic key features were taken for subsequent laboratory verification via consultation with taxonomic experts and literature, and (ii) the characterization of local road infrastructure and surrounding environmental features. Immediately following data collection, all carcasses were removed from the road and pavement edges to prevent attracting scavengers (thereby avoiding subsequent wildlife mortality) and to ensure that carcasses were not double-counted during future weekly surveys. At each roadkill location, we recorded environmental and road-related variables that could potentially influence wildlife mortality patterns, within an immediate ~ 100-m habitat buffer (Filius et al. 2020 , Karanasios et al. 2026 ). These variables were recorded categorically as presence or absence, including the presence or absence of live or pole fences (FENCES), traffic-calming signs or devices (TRAFFIC), artificial lighting associated with nearby houses (LIGHT), wildlife crossings (WLCROSS; e.g., drainage culverts acting as underpasses), and water bodies (WATER; e.g., active streams, temporary ponds, or drainage channels) located within this ~ 100-m radius. We also reviewed the IUCN Red List to classify the recorded species by their global conservation status. Data analysis All statistical analyses were conducted using R (R Core Team 2025 ). Graphics for data analysis were elaborated using ggplot2 package (Wickham 2016 ). Environmental and road-related variables were treated as factors. Records without taxonomic identification at higher levels were retained, when possible, by assigning them to the corresponding class. To evaluate whether road characteristics were associated with the taxonomic composition of recorded roadkill, we constructed four Bayesian generalized linear models (GLMs) with a Bernoulli distribution and logit link function, one for each major taxonomic class (Mammalia, Aves, Reptilia, Amphibia). For each model, the complete dataset of roadkill records was retained, and the response variable was recoded into a binary indicator denoting whether each record belonged to the focal taxonomic class (1) or to any of the remaining classes (0). Thus, each model estimated the probability that a recorded roadkill belonged to the focal taxonomic class, conditional on a roadkill having occurred, as a function of road characteristics while comparing the focal class against all other vertebrate groups. Predictor variables used for modelling were: WLCROSS, LIGHT, FENCES, TRAFFIC and WATER, codified as present (1) or absent (0) for each roadkill record. To control for non-independence in the data, road identity (ROAD, three levels) was incorporated as a fixed effect. We did not treat it as a random effect because estimating random-effects variance components is generally unstable or inaccurate when the grouping factor has fewer than five-six levels (Bolker et al. 2009 ). Multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed using the Generalized Variance Inflation Factor (GVIF) (Fox and Monette 1992 ). All predictors exhibited low collinearity (maximum GVIF = 1.66), well below commonly accepted thresholds (Dormann et al. 2013 ), and were therefore retained in the final model. The full model is specified as: $${y}_{i}\sim\;Bernoulli\left({p}_{i}\right)$$ $$\begin{array}{l} logit\left({p}_{i}\right)=\alpha\:+{\beta\:}_{1}{WLCROSS}_{i}+{\beta\:}_{2}{WATER}_{i}+{\beta\:}_{3}{LIGHT}_{i}\\+{\beta\:}_{4}{FENCES:none}_{i}+{\beta\:}_{5}{FENCES:poles}_{i}+{\beta\:}_{6}{TRAFFIC}_{i}\\+{\beta\:}_{7}{ROAD:planeta}_{i}+{\beta\:}_{8}{ROAD:puerto}_{i}\end{array}$$ Where \({y}_{i}=1\) indicates that the recorded roadkill belongs to the focal taxonomic class, whereas \({y}_{i}=0\) indicates that it belongs to any of the remaining vertebrate classes, α is the intercept and \({\beta\:}_{1},\dots\:{\beta\:}_{8}\) are the regression coefficients for predictors. Models were fitted using JAGS (Plummer 2003 ) through the jagsUI package (Kellner et al. 2019 ) to sample from the posterior distribution of each parameter using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. We ran three chains of 20,000 iterations each, with a burn-in of 10,000 and a thinning of 5. We specified uninformative normal priors (mean 0 and precision of 0.01) for all regression coefficients. Posterior distributions were summarized using means, 95% credible intervals (95% CRI), and the posterior probability that a coefficient is positive [P (β > 0)] or negative [P (β < 0)]. Coefficients with a high posterior probability of being strictly positive or negative were interpreted as having moderate evidence if P > 0.90 or strong evidence if P > 0.95 for an effect. Model convergence was assessed using the Gelman-Rubin statistic (Ȓ), and effective sample sizes were monitored for all parameters (Gelman and Hill 2007 ). Convergence was achieved for all the parameters (Ȓ < 1.001). Results A total of 317 roadkill was recorded during the study period. Identification to species level was possible for 217 observations (68.5%), 41 to genus (12.9%), two to suborder (0.6%), and 57 could only be assigned to the class or order level as unidentified vertebrates (18.0%). In general, reptiles were the most frequently recorded group (47.9%), followed by amphibians (21.8%), mammals (16.1%), and birds (14.2%) (Fig. 2 a and b). Most species ( n = 30) were classified as Least Concern according to the IUCN, one was classified as Vulnerable, and 17 taxa could not be assigned to an IUCN category because they were not identified to the species level (Table 1 ). Among the most common roadkill species were the common green iguana Iguana iguana , northern cat-eyed snake Leptodeira septentrionalis , Daniel’s keelback Helicops danieli , common opossum Didelphis marsupialis , tree frogs of Boana genus, and cane and Rivero’s toads Rhinella horribilis and R. humboldti (Table 1 ). Fences were mainly represented by poles (43.5%) and live fences (44.2%), while traffic signals were absent in the majority of records (61.8%) (Fig. 2 c-g, Supplementary material Table S1 ). In the following sections, positive effects indicate an increased probability that a roadkill record belonged to the focal taxonomic class relative to all other vertebrate classes, whereas negative effects indicate the opposite. Roadkill records from road points with artificial lighting near houses (LIGHT) were substantially more likely to belong to mammals than to the remaining vertebrate groups (P (β₃ > 0) = 0.997; Fig. 3 ). In contrast, roadkill records from illuminated road points were less likely to belong to amphibians than to the remaining vertebrate groups (P (β₃ < 0) = 0.967; Fig. 3 ). No evidence of an association between artificial lighting and the taxonomic identity of roadkill records was found for birds or reptiles (all posterior probabilities < 0.90, Supplementary material Table S1 ). Fence type (FENCE) also influenced the taxonomic composition of roadkill. Compared with live fences, roadkill records from road sections without fences were more likely to belong to mammals (P (β₄ > 0) = 0.956) but less likely to yield bird records (P (β₄ < 0) = 0.975). Pole fences were positively associated with amphibian records (P (β₅ > 0) = 0.996) and negatively associated with bird records (P (β₅ < 0) = 0.954). No evidence of fence effects was detected for reptiles. Road identity (ROAD) was also associated with the taxonomic composition of roadkill records. Compared with the reference road, both Planeta Rica and Puerto Escondido showed a higher probability of recording a mammal (Planeta Rica: P (β₇ > 0) = 0.999; Puerto Escondido: P (β₈ > 0) = 0.988). Amphibian records were also more likely on Planeta Rica (P (β₇ > 0) = 0.998) and, with moderate support, on Puerto Escondido (P (β₈ > 0) = 0.949). In contrast, bird records were less likely on both roads (Planeta Rica: P (β₇ < 0) = 0.995; Puerto Escondido: P (β₈ < 0) = 0.997), while reptile records were less likely on Planeta Rica (P (β₇ < 0) = 0.999). Discussion Although wildlife–vehicle collisions are relatively well documented in Colombia, it remains unclear how local road conditions shape the taxonomic composition of the vertebrates involved, particularly in the Colombian Caribbean, where tropical dry forest persists in varying states of conservation and constitutes one of the country’s most threatened ecosystems. Here, we found that roadkill composition varied among vertebrate groups in relation to artificial lighting, water bodies, fencing, wildlife crossings, and geographic location (road identity). These findings suggest that local roadside environmental conditions influence which vertebrate groups are represented in roadkill records, reinforcing the importance of considering local environmental context when assessing the impacts of roads on biodiversity. Our results also provide baseline information that can help guide efforts to mitigate the impacts of roads on biodiversity in the Colombian Caribbean. This study documented a substantial number of wildlife roadkill records along peri-urban roads crossing tropical dry forest (TDF) remnants in Córdoba, one of Colombia’s most threatened ecosystems. We recorded 317 vertebrate roadkills, a total higher than those reported elsewhere in the Colombian Caribbean, such as the 259 vertebrates recorded along a 72-km section of Route 74 in Córdoba (Aguas et al. 2022 ) and the 208 recorded along two sections of the Troncal del Caribe in Magdalena (Adárraga-Caballero and Gutiérrez-Moreno 2019 ). However, higher totals have been documented in other landscape matrices in the department of Sucre, including 431 individuals on the Toluviejo–Ciénaga La Caimanera road (De La Ossa-V and Galván-Guevara 2015 ) and 621 specimens on roads adjacent to the Montes de María corridor (De la Ossa Nadjar and De La Ossa-V 2013 ). Although these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because studies differ in sampling effort, road length, and survey methods, they indicate that wildlife–vehicle collisions represent a pervasive source of mortality across road systems in the Colombian Caribbean, consistent with patterns reported from other tropical regions (Medrano-Vizcaíno et al. 2022 ; Arévalo-Ayala et al. 2025 ). Beyond documenting roadkill occurrence, our analyses show that local road infrastructure and environmental characteristics are associated with the taxonomic composition of roadkill records, with some vertebrate groups disproportionately represented under particular roadside conditions. Reptiles constituted the largest proportion of records, followed by amphibians, mammals, and birds. This contrasts with studies in which mammals dominate roadkill assemblages (Gálvez 2021 ; Medrano-Vizcaíno et al. 2023 ; Arévalo-Ayala et al. 2025 ), but agrees with reports from tropical ecosystems in which ectotherms account for most collisions (Rosen and Lowe 1994 ; Vargas-Salinas et al. 2011 ). The bicycle-based protocol, which allowed low-speed surveys, probably also improved the detection of small-bodied carcasses relative to surveys from motorized vehicles (Meek 2009 ; Jones et al. 2024 ). Three species dominated reptile records: the green iguana ( Iguana iguana ), Daniel’s keelback ( Helicops danieli ), and the northern cat-eyed snake ( Leptodeira septentrionalis ). These species may be especially exposed to roads because of thermoregulation on road surfaces, slow movement, and cryptic coloration that reduces detectability by drivers (Quintero-Ángel et al. 2012 ; Obando-Tobón et al. 2024 ), as reported in other Colombian TDF systems (De La Ossa-Nadjar and De La Ossa V 2015 ; Arango-Lozano and Patiño-Siro 2020 ). Water bodies increased the relative representation of reptiles among roadkill records, but this pattern appears to be explained mainly by two of these species. Records of Daniel’s keelbacks were disproportionately associated with water bodies (~ 83%), consistent with its aquatic habits, as were those moderately of green iguanas (~ 61%), whose juveniles are closely associated with riparian habitats (Muñoz et al. 2003 ). In contrast, only 54% of northern cat-eyed snakes records occurred near water bodies. Overall, ~ 62% of snake records were associated with water bodies, which may reflect the attractiveness of these habitats to snakes, as they host a high diversity and abundance of amphibians, an important component of their diet (Rojas Murcia et al. 2016 ). Amphibians also represented an important fraction of records, consistent with their vulnerability arising from limited mobility and frequent movements between terrestrial and aquatic habitats (Ribeiro-D’Anunciação et al. 2013 ; Valencia-Aguilar et al. 2013 ). Records from illuminated sections were less likely to be amphibians than other vertebrates. This suggests that amphibians may be underrepresented in more urbanised roadside environments, possibly because of increased desiccation risk, avoidance of disturbed habitats, or rapid carcass removal by the mesopredators and scavengers these areas attract (Santos et al. 2011 ; Teixeira et al. 2013 ). Such opportunistic scavenging, supported by anthropogenic food subsidies, can quickly eliminate small carcasses and reduce detection during weekly surveys. In contrast, the positive association with pole fences may indicate that these structures influence local movements or coincide with suitable microhabitats (De La Ossa Nadjar and De La Ossa V 2015 ). Amphibian roadkill is usually associated with proximity to water bodies (Forti et al. 2022 ; Pinto et al. 2025 ). Although our models did not clearly detect this effect, the raw data showed higher absolute frequencies near water (Fig. 2 g). Although more amphibians were recorded near water bodies (40 vs. 29 records), more roadkill of all groups was also recorded there (181 vs. 136). As a result, amphibians made up almost the same share of records in both situations (22.1% near water vs. 21.3% away from water). Because our models compare the share of each group among all records, rather than counting individuals, they detected no association between amphibian roadkill and water bodies. We emphasize that this finding should be interpreted within the temporal scope of our surveys and should not be extrapolated to other seasons of the year. Mammals were less frequently recorded ( n = 51), and 44 of these records (86%) were bats and opossums, so the strong association between lighting near houses (LIGHT) and mammal roadkill may reflect the ecology of these two groups rather than a general mammalian response. Both are generalist, predominantly nocturnal species common in agricultural and peri-urban mosaics, where they exploit human-modified environments and a broad range of resources (Medrano-Vizcaíno et al. 2022 ). Roadkill records from illuminated sections were more likely to be mammals than other groups (Pinowsky 2005 ; Delgado 2007 ). Artificial lighting may alter prey availability, particularly by concentrating nocturnal insects and modifying their movement and distribution (Owens and Lewis 2018 ), and other features of human settlements, such as household waste, livestock, cultivated areas, and carrion, may provide food subsidies for opportunistic mesopredators (Adler et al. 1997 ; Newsome et al. 2015 ; El-Sabaawi 2018 ). Thus, the association between lighting and roadkill should not be interpreted as a direct effect of light alone, but as a component of a broader urbanization gradient that modifies food availability, animal activity, and trophic interactions near roads, potentially increasing road exposure for opportunistic nocturnal or crepuscular mammals. However, responses vary with species-specific foraging strategies, activity patterns, and sensitivity to light. In bats, the relationship between artificial lighting and road mortality is inconsistent across contexts (Frank et al. 2019 ; Deichmann et al. 2021 ; de Figueiredo Ramalho et al. 2021 ), and experimental evidence from Didelphis virginiana suggests that light may instead reduce movement and act as a barrier, as individuals used illuminated sections of wildlife crossing structures less often than unlit ones (Bliss-Ketchum et al. 2016 ). Likewise, the negative association between mammal records and water bodies may reflect habitat-use patterns that reduce road crossing near water (but see Ríos-Solís et al. 2025 ), although this result may overlook water-associated species such as the crab-eating fox (Freitas et al. 2015 ), which was recorded in low numbers ( n = 2). Our results should therefore be interpreted as an association strongly influenced by the dominance of bats and opossums among mammal records. Birds had the fewest records, as in other TDF areas (De La Ossa Nadjar and De La Ossa V 2015 ). Their high mobility may reduce collision probability relative to ground-dwelling taxa, but carcass removal and rapid decomposition can cause substantial underdetection (Santos et al. 2011 ; Teixeira et al. 2013 ), which is plausible given our weekly survey interval. Although some Caribbean bird species occasionally forage nocturnally under artificial light (Debrot 2014 ), this did not translate into higher bird roadkill on illuminated sections, possibly because most regional avian mesopredators are diurnal and birds’ superior visual acuity and vertical escape flight may reduce their vulnerability to oncoming traffic (DeVault et al. 2015 ). Records near wildlife crossings were less likely to be birds than other groups, suggesting that these structures may locally reduce the relative representation of bird collisions. This result should be interpreted cautiously, however, given the lack of information on the design, placement, and use of these crossings by birds. Because live fences were the reference category, the negative coefficients for pole fences and unfenced sections indicate that bird records were relatively more frequent where live fences occurred. Live fences provide foraging, nesting, and perching opportunities and may increase bird activity along roadsides (Orłowski 2008 ; Kociolek et al. 2011 ), which could explain their greater representation in these sections. Our inferences should be considered in light of several limitations. Because the analyses were based exclusively on roadkill records (presence-only data), the models evaluated how environmental variables are associated with the taxonomic identity of observed roadkill, not absolute collision risk for each group. The associations therefore describe changes in the relative composition of roadkill records under different roadside conditions, not changes in the probability of collisions. First, the lack of precise geolocation for each record prevented spatially explicit analyses of collision risk and the evaluation of different buffer sizes or scales of habitat influence (Kociolek et al. 2011 ; Gunson et al. 2011 ; D’Amico et al. 2015 ); we relied instead on a local characterization of road features and immediate surroundings. Second, vehicle speed and traffic volume, which are frequently associated with roadkill patterns (Fahrig and Rytwinski 2009 ), were not included in our models. Third, surveys were weekly and restricted to three months (April–June), which limits generalization to the full annual cycle and likely introduced detectability biases (Clevenger et al. 2003 ). Carcass decomposition and removal by opportunistic scavengers, such as opossums, crab-eating foxes, and raptors, may have been particularly important for small-bodied taxa (Santos et al. 2011 ; Teixeira et al. 2013 ). The low frequency of some groups may therefore reflect differential carcass persistence and detectability as well as actual mortality (Adler et al. 1997 ; De la Ossa V and Galván-Guevara 2015 ; Medrano-Vizcaíno et al. 2022 , 2023 ; Arévalo-Ayala et al. 2025 ). Nevertheless, including road identity (ROAD) as a fixed effect allowed us to account partly for spatial and environmental heterogeneity among the three roads (Heeres et al. 2025 ). Differences in urbanization, roadside vegetation, and hydrology may have contributed to the contrasting responses among taxonomic groups, as roadkill patterns often vary among road segments with local habitat context and infrastructure (Gunson et al. 2011 ; Kociolek et al. 2011 ; D’Amico et al. 2015 ; Pinto et al. 2025 ). The effects detected for individual predictors should therefore be interpreted within this broader context of spatially structured variation among roads rather than as isolated local mechanisms. Conclusion Our findings show that the taxonomic composition of roadkill records is associated with both local road features and environmental heterogeneity along the road (Obando-Tobón et al. 2024 ). These patterns must be interpreted in light of ecological and behavioural variability within each taxonomic group, since species within a group may share functional traits that condition their interaction with roads. The observed patterns therefore likely reflect differences in habitat use and movement behaviour rather than differences in absolute roadkill risk. Because vertebrate groups fulfil distinct ecological roles, shifts in the composition of roadkill may have consequences for processes such as seed dispersal, insect population control, and trophic dynamics (Quiles and Barrientos 2024 ). Increasing survey frequency and extending monitoring across dry and rainy seasons and multiple years would improve detection, reduce uncertainty, and help disentangle the relative importance of climatic variation, local road features, surrounding habitat, and road-specific characteristics (Carvahlo-Roel et al. 2023 ). Our results provide a baseline for understanding how local road features and road-specific conditions jointly shape the taxonomic composition of wildlife roadkill in TDF ecosystems, and support the need for mitigation measures that reduce wildlife mortality and maintain ecosystem functionality in these highly threatened landscapes. To this end, road planning should adopt a “green infrastructure” framework that prioritizes ecological connectivity (Arroyave et al. 2006 ). Following technical guidelines (Arroyave et al. 2006 ; INVIAS 2026 ), we recommend: (i) systematic monitoring and spatial modelling to predict roadkill hotspots from adjacent vegetation; (ii) underpasses for small vertebrates and canopy bridges for arboreal fauna, designed with natural substrates and appropriate dimensions to avoid rejection effects; (iii) drift fences to guide wildlife toward these crossings; and (iv) wildlife signage combined with speed reducers and deterrent technologies (e.g., reflectors or presence sensors) during peak periods of biological activity. Finally, compliance with standardized technical specifications should be enforced from the feasibility phase of road projects, in accordance with current national regulations.
