Brown Jr. Salinity Laboratory, Riverside, CA, United States Article metrics View details Abstract The Rio Grande, an important river that borders the USA and Mexico is recharged with untreated and treated wastewaters from binational sources and therefore is known to contain potentially toxic elements (PTEs) like As, Cd, Co, Cr, Pb, and Sb. These elements are nonbiodegradable and can accumulate in soil and crops when contaminated surface water is used for irrigation. This study was designed to assess the contamination levels of these elements in water, soil, and crops sampled from three farms (onion, cilantro, and Swiss Chard in Hidalgo Co., TX) and use the data to perform pollution assessment (bioaccumulation factor - BAF, enrichment factor - EF, and metal pollution index - MPI) and health hazard assessment (health index-HI, estimated daily intake–EDI, and cancer risk - CR). Matrixes were digested and analyzed using ICP-OES. The mean PTE concentrations in water (0.73–81.74 ug/L) were all within acceptable levels for irrigation water. Although the mean concentration of PTEs (mg/kg) in soil Cd (0.19), Co (5.04), Cr (10.04), and Pb (18.36) were within acceptable limits, all three had above normal levels of Sb (11.64–18.62) with elevated As levels detected in the onion and Swiss chard fields (7.35 and 8.20, respectively). The mean dry weight of As and Cr in onion, cilantro, and Swiss chard tissues were either above normal or excessive (As 2.90 mg/kg – 5.85 mg/kg; Cr 0.71 mg/kg – 15.79 mg/kg), but all crop tissues had normal or below normal levels of Sb (mean 9.50 mg/kg). Swiss chard tissues also had elevated levels of Cd, Co, and Pb (0.25 mg/kg, 7.04 mg/kg, and 19.11 mg/kg, respectively). Health risk assessment showed that the EDI of all PTEs studied in the crops were within the US FDA daily acceptable intake limits; the HI for cilantro was safe for cilantro, but high for onion and Swiss chard. CR assessment for cilantro was acceptable across all the metals studied, whereas CR for onion and Swiss chard were above the threshold and were considered unacceptable. These results indicate that chronic consumption of crops that bioaccumulate PTEs can present systemic toxicological risks within the water-energy-food nexus. 1 Introduction Surface water is the most accessible freshwater source, globally, and composes only 1.2% of all freshwater in the world (Shiklomanov, 1993; Mishra, 2023). Contamination of freshwater systems from industrialization, agriculture, and urbanization, along with geological attributes, are sources of potentially toxic elements (PTEs) in the environment that have become a major topic of concern. (Holt, 2000; Rusprayunita et al., 2025). In contrast to organic contaminants, potentially toxic elements (PTEs) are not biodegradable and therefore, bioaccumulate at high concentrations in biota at potentially toxic levels (Montouris et al., 2002; Clemens, 2006; Kovacik et al., 2025). Both environmental and human health agencies have highlighted the risk of PTE exposure, in particular, arsenic, cadmium, chromium and lead, to human health via environmental matrices (WHO, 1993; Vardhan et al., 2019). Contamination of surface waters from agricultural runoffs, urban wastewater/runoff, industrial discharge and mining activities pose a risk for downstream use, such as for municipal drinking water, recreation, and agriculture. Transport of dissolved and sediment-accumulated PTEs in rivers are redistributed into connecting aquatic systems, floodplains and agricultural systems via irrigation (Marshall, 2013). In particular, transport of PTEs from agricultural irrigation introduces contaminants into soils where they may persist and bioaccumulate into plants, thereby entering the food chain. Approximately 60%–75% of freshwater is used for agricultural irrigation, which can transport PTEs into agricultural fields and simultaneously increase the probability of regional droughts when rainfall levels are inadequate for crops (Shiklomanov, 1997; Leng et al., 2015). In turn, reduced levels from rivers and reservoirs could concentrate PTE levels. Unfortunately, unlike organic contaminants, PTEs persist in an ambient environment due to their ubiquity, toxicity, and non-biodegradability. The effects from exposure to excessively high concentrations may cause serious health problems including neurological damage, diabetes, endocrine disruption, oxidative stress, and carcinogenesis (ATSDR, 2008; Peralta-Videa et al., 2009; Jomova and Valko, 2025; Peana et al., 2022). Thus, determining the concentration of PTEs in water, agricultural soil, and plants is critical, especially for PTEs such as arsenic, cadmium and lead with primary human exposure routes through the food chain (Chaney, 1989; Peralta-Videa et al., 2009). Our study focuses on agricultural irrigation waters sourced from an international boundary along the US-Mexico border, the Rio Grande. Approximately 80% of water extraction from the Rio Grande is used for irrigation (IBWC, 1998). The Rio Grande stretches from Colorado to Texas with 2,053 km forming the international boundary between the United States and Mexico (IBWC, 1998; BLM, 2021). Influences on the international boundary of the Rio Grande include diversions for agriculture and domestic as well as industrial water needs. The river is recharged with untreated and treated wastewater from binational sources, defining it as an indirect use of recycled water for agricultural irrigation, municipalities, and industry (IBWC, 1998; Rodriguez et al., 2009). Environmental monitoring of the international boundary of the Rio Grande has shown PTE contamination, including water levels of Ag, Cu, Pb, and Zn exceeding EPA’s criteria for aquatic species (Miyamoto et al., 1995; Rios-Arana et al., 2003), elevated levels of Cd, Cu, and Cr in sediment pore water (Miyamoto et al., 1995), and Cd, Cr, Cu, Pb, and Zn accumulation in irrigated fields within the upper Rio Grande basin (Miyamoto et al., 1995; Assadian et al., 1998; Assadian et al., 1999). Most of what is known of metal contamination of the Rio Grande along the US-Mexico border region is focused on the upper Rio Grande Basin (Miyamoto et al., 1995; IBWC, 1998; Assadian et al., 1998; Assadian et al., 2003; Rios-Arana et al., 2003) that has had over two decades of drought with limited opportunities for transport of contaminants downstream to the Lower Rio Grande. Approximately 88% and 96% of the irrigation water sourced from the lower reach supports the major agricultural valleys of the United States and Mexico border regions, respectively (Miyamoto et al., 1995; BLM, 2021; Dunbar et al., 2022). Within the Lower Rio Grande Basin, As, B, Cr, Cu, Hg, Pb, Ni, Sb, and Zn have been identified as PTEs of concern (Wells et al., 1988; IBWC, 1998). Unusually high levels of arsenic and antimony in sediments were also reported for a tributary of the Lower Rio Grande, as runoff from a decommissioned antimony smelter plant affected downstream waters (Garcia et al., 2024). It is worth noting that critical risks of crop bioaccumulation and soil toxicity would apparently emerge when considering these elevated levels of PTEs found throughout the river system. Water quality of irrigation waters can be a determinant for crop field contamination and the risk to human health. Discharged wastewater into the Rio Grande has been attributed to heavy metal contamination of agricultural soils and crops (Assadian et al., 1998; 1999). Using an estimate of metal loading from irrigation waters, metal accumulation through irrigation accounted for 27%–31% of metal content in mixed irrigation fields (untreated wastewater and river water), while those receiving river water alone could account for less than 10% of PTE contamination. In comparison between three irrigation sources, treated effluent, fertilized well water, and river water, all three sources contributed to soil metal accumulation, but river water deposited the highest levels of PTEs (Soleimani et al., 2023). Several studies point out that levels of PTEs may be moderate or do not exceed guidelines for contaminant risks, yet PTE accumulation in soils and/or crops could still pose human health risks. (Li et al., 2025; Aschale et al., 2019; Manegabe et al., 2026). The primary goal of this study was to assess the effects of repeated irrigation with contaminated water on PTE in soils and crops, to quantify PTE translocation and accumulation using bioaccumulation (BAF), enrichment (EF) factors, metal pollution index (MPI), and then, to evaluate dietary human health risks using estimated daily intake (EDI), target hazard quotients (THQ), hazard index (HI), and cancer risk assessment (CR). 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Sampling sites In this work, a comprehensive field assessment was conducted at three separate commercial farms in the Rio Grande region. We collected and analyzed irrigation water, bulk soil, and three representative crops: Allium cepa (onion), Coriandrum sativum (cilantro), and Beta vulgaris (Swiss chard). Elemental analysis was performed via Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES) following microwave-assisted acid digestion in accordance with US EPA Method (González-Diaz et al., 2025; Biedunkova et al., 2025). We applied a suite of environmental indices, including the Metal Pollution Index (MPI), Bioaccumulation Factor (BAF), and Enrichment Factor (EF), to quantify the toxicological burden and translocation efficiency from the rhizosphere to edible tissues. Logistical constraints limited our study to single farm sites, per crop, with different sampling dates, and hence, different crop growth stages. These variables cannot be distinctively separated; therefore, interpretations represent the specific types of agroecosystems within a region dependent on indirect use of recycled water and not solely crop-specific physiology. 2.2 Sample collection and preparation Agricultural samples (water, bulk soil, and crop plants) were collected from commercial farms in the Rio Grande Valley, a major agriculturally productive region in South Texas (Soti et al., 2020). Three commercial farms were sampled, at a single sampling event for each farm, within Hidalgo County, TX (26.4656° N, 98.2213° W): an onion farm with two fields, a Swiss chard farm, and an organic cilantro farm. Agricultural farms received irrigation waters from canals, wherein water is diverted from the Rio Grande for flood irrigation and distributed in the field using layflat hoses. Irrigation frequency was not documented, but repeated irrigation was observed from October 2023 to February 2024 during farm visits and is standard practice for commercial farms in Hidalgo County during the winter months as recommended by the Texas A&M Agricultural Extension Office (Masabni, 2011a; 2011b; 2011c). Precipitation in Hidalgo Co., TX during the growing season (NOAA-NCEI, 2026) and the recommended irrigation (Masabni, 2011a; 2011b; 2011c) were as follows: 1) 208–253 mm total precipitation with recommended irrigation rates of 635–762 mm for onion; 2) 162–181 mm total precipitation with recommended irrigation rates of 152–305 mm for cilantro; and 3) 139–184 mm total precipitation with recommended irrigation rates of 254–381 mm for Swiss chard. Samples were collected once from each farm: during the mid-season for onions (20 week growth period, February 2024), late season for cilantro (13 week growth period, December 2023), and mid-season for Swiss chard (13 week growth period, February 2024). A grab sample of water was collected directly from the layflat hose at the onion field, directly from the irrigation canal along the fields (cilantro field), or the primary water pipe outlets used to flood fields (Swiss chard field). Water samples were collected, in triplicate, in 1-L amber HDPE bottles, disinfected with 10% bleach, rinsed with sterile Milli-Q water and allowed to dry, at least 24 h. Bottles were rinsed with the field-collected water at least three times prior to collection. Samples were maintained on ice, at 4 °C until processing. To capture spatial variation across the irrigation flow, we laid out three parallel transects perpendicular to the main layflat hose in each field. Along each transect, bulk soil and crop samples were collected from three points (1-m2 quadrats) relative to the primary field irrigation inlet, 1-m2 quadrats were designated along three transects per field (9 quadrats per field) as: (i) nearest point (within 5 m), (ii) midpoint (55–113 m from layflat hose), and (iii) farthest (153–253 m from layflat hose) (Figure 1; Supplementary Table S1). Two onion fields at the same farm were sampled (number of quadrats = 18), one cilantro farm (number of quadrats = 9), and one Swiss chard farm (number of quadrats = 9). Using a soil probe disinfected with 70% ethanol, bulk soil samples were collected as a composite sample from five points within the 1 m2 quadrat, at 0–20 cm in depth, and consolidated into sterile Whirl-Paks®. Three to five crop plants were collected within each quadrat, using a hand trowel disinfected with 70% ethanol, and transferred into sterile Whirl-Paks®. All samples were kept at 4 °C until they were processed. Prior to chemical analysis, soil samples were transferred into acid-washed beakers, dried in an oven at 100 °C, and pulverized in a ceramic mortar. Vegetable samples were washed with water and dried in the oven at 60 °C to constant weight. These dried plant samples were subsequently pulverized following the same protocol established for the soil samples. 2.3 Chemicals and instrumentation Triton X-100, nitric acid (HNO3, 70%), and hydrofluoric acid (HF, 51%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Standard reference materials for trace elements in water, soils, and spinach were obtained from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Analytical-grade heavy metal stock solutions (100 mg/L) were purchased from VWR (Radnor, PA, USA) and diluted to prepare working calibration standards. Sample matrix digestion was performed using a CEM MARS 6 Microwave Digestion System (CEM Corporation, Matthews, NC, USA). Elemental concentrations were subsequently analyzed by using an Agilent ICP-720 Inductively Coupled Plasma-Optical Emission Spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). 2.4 Sample preparation and matrix digestion Water samples were transported to the laboratory and filtered through a 0.45 µm PTFE syringe filter. A 10‐mL aliquot of the filtered water was acidified with 400 µL of concentrated trace-metal grade HNO3 following EPA Method 3015A (U.S. EPA, 2007) and stored at 4 °C in acid-washed centrifuge tubes prior to ICP-OES analysis. Solid samples were digested in accordance with EPA Method 3052 (US Environmental Protection Agency, 1996) using a CEM MARS 6 microwave digestion unit fitted with high-pressure PTFE vessels. A 0.500 g subsample of pulverized soil or dried plant tissue (edible parts) was weighed into each vessel and combined with an acid mixture consisting of 7 mL of trace metal grade HNO3 (70%) and 3 mL of HF (51%). Vessels were sealed, placed in the carousel, and heated under a controlled temperature profile where power reached up to 1600 W. The internal vessel temperature ramped from room temperature to 180 °C over 15 min, followed by an isothermal hold at 180 °C for another 15 min. After the heating cycle finished, the vessels cooled inside the unit exhaust chamber until temperatures dropped below 50 °C. Digests were then carefully vented inside a fume hood, quantitatively transferred into acid-washed 50 mL volumetric flasks, diluted to volume with deionized water, and filtered through 0.45 µm PTFE membranes into clean centrifuge tubes for analysis. Batch blanks and certified standard reference materials (NIST SRM 2710a Montana I soil and SRM 1570a spinach leaves) underwent the same acid additions and microwave heating run alongside every set of field samples. 2.5 Physicochemical water quality parameters During the sampling process, essential physicochemical water quality parameters, including water temperature, pH, electrical conductivity (EC), dissolved oxygen (DO), and total dissolved solids (TDS), were measured once, using a composite of three grab samples. These measurements were performed at the time of sampling using a handheld Thermo Scientific™ Orion Star™ A329 multi-parameter meter to ensure the collection of real-time environmental data. 2.6 Physicochemical soil quality parameters Soil samples were air-dried and sieved (2-mm mesh screen) prior to analyses. For soil pH and EC, three replicate 5-g subsamples (n = 3) from consolidated farm samples were analyzed using a 2:1(v:w) ratio of deionized water to soil. Each soil solution was shaken for 1 h at 120 rpm and soil was centrifuged at 4,500 × g for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was then used for pH and EC determination. Consolidated farm samples were submitted to the Texas A&M AgriLife Extension Soil, Water and Forage Testing Laboratory (Texas A&M University, College Station, TX) for soil particle analysis and soil organic matter content. Soil particle analysis was performed using the hydrometer method (Day, 1965). For organic matter content, soils were ground to pass an 80 mesh screen prior to analysis using a combustion method using a total carbon analyzer (Schulte and Hopkins, 1996; McGeehan and Naylor, 2008; Storer, 2008). 2.7 Quality control All glassware and plastic containers used were soaked in an acid-wash bath consisting of 0.1% Triton X-100% and 5% HNO3 for 24 h and rinsed with deionized water multiple times. All sample analyses were performed in triplicates to ensure statistical reliability. The accuracy of the ICP-OES was maintained using a calibration blank and a reference standard, both of which were analyzed after every ten samples. To eliminate memory effects between samples, deionized water was passed through the analysis tubing to remove any residual metallic traces. The accuracy of the digestion protocol was verified using certified standard reference materials, including SRM 1570a Spinach leaves, 2710a Montana I Soil, and 1643f trace elements in water, which were digested according to U.S. EPA method. Furthermore, method blanks were utilized to monitor background contamination throughout the process. The results of the quality control/quality assurance are presented in Supplementary Tables S2-S5. The limit of detection for As, Cd, Co, Cr, Pb, and Sb were 0.0002454, 0.0008821, 0.001847, 0.0008232, 0.002185, and 0.002070 mg/L respectively; the limit of quantification for the metals were respectively 0.000818, 0.002940, 0.006158, 0.002744, 0.007282, and 0.006901 mg/L. The working standard solutions (0, 0.05, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0 p.m.) were prepared by serial dilution of a 100 mg/L stock solution and the calibration curve was linear within the concentration range selected with the least R2 being 0.99993. Analytes that were over the calibration range were reanalyzed after further dilutions were made. Analysis of 1643f -trace metals in water gave a recovery range of 84.04%–100% and relative standard deviation (RSD) ranged from 0.18% to 5.05%. This implies that the analysis was both accurate and precise. Analysis of Spinach SRM gave a recovery range from 90.0% to 102.9% which shows high accuracy, but the RSD range from 3.77% to 22.22%. Higher RSD values were obtained from analysis of As and Pb which had low analyte concentrations. SRM analysis of Montana I soil gave a recovery range of 87.80%–110% and RSD range of 0.73%–7.40%. overall, the recovery and RSD are within acceptable range with regards to environmental matrices like soil and plant tissue. 2.8 Statistical analysis Statistical analyses were performed using Prism Version 11.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, US), R (Version 4.6.1; R Core Team, 2026) and RStudio (Version 2026.8.2.200; Posit Team, 2026). Descriptive statistics of experiment data were analyzed using the rstatix package (Kassambara, 2026). Within each farm/crop, PTEs were the independent factor (df = 5) and the designated dependent factors were: PTE concentration of waters, soils, crop tissues; and bioaccumulation factors (BAFs) for crop PTE uptake. Differences in PTE concentrations and BAF values were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis Test in base R followed by a Dunn’s Post-Hoc Test with the Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment method to control the family-wise error rate (Benjamin and Hochberg, 1995), using the FSA package (Ogle et al., 2026), when significant effects were determined (adj. p < 0.05). Independent sample units (n) in all statistical models represented distinct physical samples or field quadrats rather than instrumental replicates. Irrigation water comparisons were n = 3 independent physical grab samples per farm (N = 9). Soil comparisons were: n = 18 for the onion farm (9 quadrats across two fields), n = 8 for the cilantro farm, and n = 7 for the Swiss chard farm (N = 33 total field replicates). Crop tissue comparisons were: n = 17 for onion, n = 7 for cilantro, and n = 7 for Swiss chard (N = 31 total composite plant samples). Bioaccumulation Factor (BAF) comparisons were: n = 17 for onion, n = 8 for cilantro, and n = 7 for Swiss chard (N = 31 total composite plant samples). Enrichment Factor (EF) comparisons were: n = 18 for onion farm soils, n = 9 for cilantro farm soils, and n = 7 for Swiss chard farm soils (N = 34 total samples). The drawing of all figures was conducted by Origin 9.5 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, US). 2.9 Bioaccumulation studies The Bioaccumulation Factor (BAF) was calculated to evaluate the accumulation of trace elements within the agricultural system. The transfer of metals from the soil to the vegetable tissues was determined using the Bioaccumulation Factor (BAF), expressed as the ratio of the metal concentration in the vegetable (Cvegetable) to that in the soil (Csoil): A value ≤1 denotes the plant’s ability to absorb heavy metals without significant accumulation, whereas a BAF >1 indicates active accumulation within its tissues. Furthermore, according to FAO/WHO (2015) values higher than 0.20 in certain crops are considered indicative of high anthropogenic contamination and associated health risks. 2.10 Enrichment factor To further evaluate the degree of anthropogenic influence on farm soils, the Enrichment Factor (EF) was calculated using the following equation: Where CX sample is the concentration of the metal of interest in soil samples. The MPI serves as a robust approach for assessing the severity of contamination for a given sample, and its resulting values are interpreted according to standardized thresholds. Specifically, an MPI value less than 1 indicates a lack of contamination, whereas values between 1 and 2 suggest slight contamination. Moderate contamination is indicated by values between 2 and 3, while significant contamination is denoted by values ranging from 3 to 5. Furthermore, an MPI greater than 5 but less than 10 implies severe contamination, and any value exceeding 10 represents extreme contamination (Mokhtarzadeh et al., 2020). 2.12 Health risk assessment Potential non-carcinogenic health risks resulting from the dietary consumption of contaminated vegetables by the PTEs were evaluated using the estimated daily intake (EDI) and the hazard quotient (THQ), hazard index (HI), and cancer risk (CR). 2.12.1 Estimated daily intake (EDI) The EDI (mg kg-1-day-1) was calculated using the exposure equation:where Cv is the concentration of PTEs in the vegetable, IR is the daily vegetable ingestion rate (onion = 25 g/day, cilantro = 5 g/day, Swiss chard 150 g/day), EF represents the frequency of exposure in day/year, ED is taken as the duration of exposure in years, BW is the exposed person’s body weight (70 kg), ED is taken as the duration of exposure in years, and AT is expressed in days as the period over which the dose is averaged. 2.12.2 The hazard quotient (THQ) The non-carcinogenic risk from individual metals was quantified using the THQ equation (USEPA, 1989): Where RfD is the oral reference dose (RfD, mg kg-1-day-1) established by the USEPA for a single PTEs each specific metal. The broader risk assessment process follows the four foundational steps established by the USEPA: hazard identification, exposure assessment, dose-response evaluation, and risk characterization (Orosun et al., 2023; Lewandowski and Norman, 2015). Hazard identification establishes the specific pollutants present within a given farm, delineating their concentration levels and spatial distribution. Finally, risk characterization integrates these exposure and toxicity metrics to predict the probability of both carcinogenic development and non-carcinogenic health risks within the exposed population. 3 Results and discussion 3.1 Physicochemical parameters and PTEs concentrations in water The results of physicochemical parameters of the Rio Grande River water are summarized in Table 1. The findings reveal that pH values in the study area ranged from 7.9 to 8.3, with the highest value at the Swiss chard farm and the lowest at the onion farm and the cilantro farm (Table 1). Surface water pH is typically influenced by soil traits, agricultural runoff, temperature, and localized biochemical reactions (Liu and Hanlon, 2012). Because the recorded values fall within the acceptable irrigation threshold of 6.5–8.5, the pH of the water poses no threat to local cultivation. EC ranged from 1010 to 1290 μS/cm, which is permissible for irrigation waters (Fipps, 2021), peaking at the cilantro farm and dropping lowest at the Swiss chard farm. Elevated EC points to higher dissolved ion concentrations, which can increase soil salinity and hinder osmotic water uptake by crop roots (Machado and Serralhiero, 2017). Recorded EC values suggest that only onion productivity would be affected by 10% with an EC of 1,200 μS/cm, but no reduction would be expected for cilantro or Swiss chard using a recommendation of 1,300 μS/cm for spinach (Fipps, 2021). A similar trend was also observed for TDS levels ranged from 605 to 775 ppm, with maximum concentrations also at the cilantro farm. As such, all sampled values remain well within standard agricultural guidelines, indicating no immediate risk to soil structure or crop productivity. Within each farm, there were significant differences between concentrations of tissue metals examined (Kruskal–Wallis, (H(5) = 91.39 (onions), 37.83 (cilantro), 37.84 (Swiss chard); p < 0.0001) η2 = 0.900 (onion), 0.912 (cilantro), 0.912 (Swiss chard)). Significant post hoc comparisons between PTE concentrations, within a crop, were also detected (Dunn’s Test, p < 0.05, Figure 2; Supplementary Table S14). For onion and cilantro, the concentration of PTEs in tissues (on a dry weight basis) ranked the same from highest to lowest mean concentrations: Sb > As > Pb > Co > Cr > Cd. In contrast, Swiss chard exhibited an inverted PTE profile of Pb > Cr > Sb > Co > As > Cd. Despite the difference in PTE rankings, all crops had above normal levels of As on a dry weight basis (1–1.7 mg/kg) and Cr (0.1–0.5 mg/kg dry weight), but within normal values of Sb (7–50 mg/kg dry weight) (Kabata-Pendias, 2010). Swiss chard tissues were recorded as having the highest PTE concentrations compared to the other crop species. We recognize that comparisons are limited because of different crop irrigation intervals for each crop along with sowing dates. However, when Swiss chard leaves were sampled, the growth period of Swiss chard was approximately 7 weeks shorter than the onion crops within the same sampling date, and yet edible tissues PTE concentrations were between 1.7 to 22.2-fold higher than onion PTE levels. Lead levels in Swiss chard leaves were significantly higher (Dunn’s Test, adj. p < 0.01) compared to As (5.85 mg/kg dry weight), Cd (0.25 mg/kg dry weight), and Co (7.04 mg/kg dry weight). Further, PTEs in Swiss chard leaves were above normal for Pb (5–10 mg/kg dry weight) but below phytotoxic levels (30–300 mg/kg dry weight), potentially phytotoxic for Cr (5–30 mg/kg dry weight) as well as intolerable for agronomic crops (>2 mg/kg dry weight), above tolerable for Co in agronomic crops (5 mg/kg dry weight) by three-fold, and excessive or even phytotoxic for As levels (5–30 mg/kg dry weight) (Kabat-Pendias, 2010). Even with a longer growth period for onions (7 weeks longer), onion and cilantro PTE levels were found to be similar. Antimony levels, however, were significantly higher than other PTEs in onions (Dunn’s Test, adj. p < 0.01), other than As, and significantly higher than Cd, Co and Cr in cilantro (Dunn’s Test, adj. p < 0.01). For all three crops, levels of As could potentially result in 10% yield loss with concentrations of 1–20 mg/kg (dry weight) As in tissues (Kabata-Pendias, 2010). Given the elevated levels of As, Co, and Cr in Swiss chard, we did not observe any obvious signs of metal toxicity such as yellowing or browning of roots, interveinal chlorosis, or necrotic spots, respectively. While Beta vulgaris demonstrated an exceptional capacity for metal/metalloid accumulation in its aboveground biomass, Allium cepa exhibited low translocation of Cr and Cd in edible tissues, although its roots still contained a notable Pb burden (1.87 mg/kg dry weight). Aschale et al. (2019) pointed to elevated accumulation of metals in Swiss Chard compared to other leafy crops, such as cabbage and kale. Although our study is limited by a single sampling event, overlapping irrigation periods between the cool season crops sampled and the shorter growth period of Swiss chard compared to onions indicate that crops with a broad leaf growth form have the potential to contain excessive PTEs compared to other crops. These divergent trends highlight that crop variety, tissue morphology, and localized soil-water interactions are critical variables driving PTE bioaccumulation in degraded agricultural ecosystems. 3.4 PTEs among different farms and their bioaccumulation As supported in other studies (Arefin et al., 2016; Soleimani et al., 2023), we found that irrigation with river water serves as a route for PTE transport and deposition into agricultural fields. With a one-time sampling event, we recognize that irrigation water PTEs fluctuate; therefore, we calculated the bioaccumulation factor (BAF), to compare the potential accumulation of PTEs in edible tissues of crops relative to soil levels, and hence, PTE bioavailability specific to the crop and field soil. A BAF value below 0.1 indicates low accumulation, 0.1–1.0 moderate accumulation, and higher than 1, an excessive level that is considered one of several factors indicating hyperaccumulation (Mendez and Maier, 2008; Orosun, 2021; IAEA, 2010; Huang et al., 2025). Within each farm, significant differences were observed between PTE bioaccumulation factors (Kruskal–Wallis; H(5, onions) = 77.03, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.750; H(5, cilantro leaves) = 16.65, p = 0.005, η2 = 0.323; H(5, Swiss chard leaves) = 27.45, p < 0.0001), η2 = 0.623). Significant comparisons between BAF values for metal uptake within a crop were also determined (Dunn’s Test, adj. p < 0.05; Supplementary Table S15). When comparing BAFs of PTEs for all three crops, Swiss chard had the highest number of PTEs that were excessively accumulated (BAF >1) with the order of Cr > Pb > Cd > Co > As > Sb (Figure 3). The BAF of Cd, Co, Cr, and Pb for Swiss chard was not statistically different (Dunn’s Test, p > 0.05), but significantly higher than the BAF for metalloids As and Sb (BAF = 0.7; Dunn’s Test, adj. p < 0.0001; Figure 3). Elevated levels of Pb were detected in irrigation waters and soils collected at all three farms (Tables 4 and 5); however, Swiss chard had the highest BAF for Pb (BAF = 1.5, Figure 3) and was the only crop with above normal levels of Pb (Table 5), two-to four-fold the expected range in plant leaf tissues (5–10 mg/kg dry weight, Kabata-Pendias, 2010). Alkaline pH levels, organic matter and carbonates reduce bioavailability of cadmium, chromium, cobalt, and lead (Peralta-Videa et al., 2009; Kabata-Pendias, 2010; Collin et al., 2022). Given the alkaline pH of the irrigation waters and the agricultural soil, these metals should be less bioavailable (Cavallaro and McBride, 1980; Martinez and Motto, 2000; Ma and Hooda, 2010; Kushwaha et al., 2018; Collin et al., 2022). Swiss chard farm soil metal and organic matter content (2.4%, Table 3) was similar to cilantro soils, yet BAF of the metals tested in this study were higher for Swiss chard. Both Cr and Co were detected at similar concentrations in irrigation waters and soils across sampling locations, and Cd was similar in irrigation waters. The elevated bioaccumulation of Cr, Co, and Cd cannot be explained by irrigation water content or soil pH. Bioaccumulation of lead in Swiss Chard, along with the Cr (BAF = 2.9), Co (BAF = 1.5), and Cd (BAF = 1.5), may be occurring through aerial deposition of pollutants and adsorption to leaf surfaces along with uptake of PTEs through stomata (Ma and Hooda, 2010; Collin et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2023). Other studies have determined lower bioaccumulation levels in Swiss Chard (<0.6) when irrigated with river water, even with higher levels of Cd, Cr, Co, and Pb in matrices compared to the present study (Aschale et al., 2019; Manegabe et al., 2026). Cilantro tissues had similar As and Pb tissue levels as onions, but the As bioaccumulation was excessively high (BAF = 2.5) and high for Pb (BAF = 1.3). The metalloids As and Sb were significantly higher than the BAF for chromium (Dunn’s Test, adj. p < 0.05, Figure 3) with BAF indices in the order of As > Pb > Sb > Co > Cr > Cd. In onion tissues, bioaccumulation factors for all PTEs were considered between low to intermediate as all BAF values were between 0.1–0.5 (Figure 3). The levels of metalloid (As/Sb) BAF values of 0.4 and 0.5, respectively, were significantly higher than the metals Cd, Pb, and Cr (Dunn’s Test, adj. p < 0.05) with BAF indices decreasing in the order of Sb > As > Co > Cd > Pb > Cr. Generally, the evaluation thresholds dictate that CR values lower than 10–6 are considered negligible, values lying between 10–6 and 10–4 fall within an acceptable target range, and values exceeding 10–4 are deemed unacceptable (Ruan et al., 2023). For all the vegetables studied, the CR values of Pb and Cd fell within the non-cancerous or acceptable region. They ranged from 5.59 × 10−8 to 8.32 × 10−5 for Pb and 5.99 × 10−7 to 4.89 × 10−5 for Cd. For cilantro, CR values for all the PTEs studied were below the upper acceptable risk threshold of 1.0 × 10−4, ranging from 5.59 × 10−8 to 2.07 × 10−5. The combined CR was 2.26 × 10−5, with As contributing the highest individual CR; however, this value remained below the upper acceptable risk threshold. These results indicate that the estimated lifetime cancer risks associated with consumption of cilantro in this study were within the acceptable risk range. In the case of onion, the CR values for Cd (3.80 × 10−7), Cr (1.22 × 10−5), and Pb (5.84 × 10−7) were below the upper acceptable risk threshold of 1.0 × 10−4. In contrast, the CR for As (1.65 × 10−4) exceeded this threshold, indicating an elevated estimated lifetime cancer risk associated with As exposure. The combined CR for onion was 1.78 × 10−4, which also exceeded the upper acceptable risk threshold. Thus, As was the primary contributor to the elevated cancer risk estimated for onion. The CR values for Swiss chard were within the acceptable range for Cd and Pb, whereas As (3.60 × 10−4) and Cr (3.25 × 10−4) exceeded the upper acceptable risk threshold of 1.0 × 10−4. The combined CR for Swiss chard was 6.96 × 10−4, which also exceeded the upper acceptable risk threshold of 1.0 × 10−4, indicating an elevated estimated lifetime cancer risk associated with consumption of Swiss chard under the exposure assumptions used in this assessment. Overall, the combined CR for cilantro was below the upper acceptable risk threshold indicating that the estimated lifetime cancer risk associated with cilantro consumption was within the selected acceptable range. In contrast, the combined CR values for onion and Swiss chard exceeded the upper acceptable risk threshold, indicating elevated estimated lifetime cancer risks for consumers. FIGURE 6 Carcinogenic risk assessment of PTEs in onion, cilantro, and swiss chard samples. 4 Conclusion The mean concentrations of potentially toxic elements in the irrigation water of all three farms followed the order Pb > As > Sb > Cr > Co > Cd and were all within the recommended limit for trace elements in reclaimed irrigation water (Fipps, 2021). Even though the concentration of PTEs in water was within limits, the pollution index assessment indicated moderate to high enrichment of PTEs in the agricultural soils. The soils were enriched with the PTEs when compared with regional agricultural soils. This finding suggests that compliance with irrigation water with recommended concentration limits does not necessarily preclude the accumulation of PTEs in receiving soils over time. The accumulation of PTEs in soil was accompanied by their detection in the edible portions of the crops studied, although the degree of accumulation varied substantially among crop types. Onion tissues exhibited low to intermediate bioaccumulation, with BAF values ranging from 0.1 to 0.5, cilantro had intermediate to high levels of bioaccumulation (BAF 0.2–2.5), and Swiss chard exhibited moderate to high bioaccumulation (BAF 0.7–2.8). High bioaccumulation of PTEs in Swiss chard suggests that crop variety, morphology, and localized soil-water interactions drive bioaccumulation rather than absolute soil concentrations alone. The presence of PTEs in the edible portions of the crops indicates that irrigation with PTE-containing water may contribute to dietary exposure even when the concentrations in irrigation water remain within recommended limits. However, since dry-weight tissue should not be interpreted as evidence of adverse health effects, the potential health assessment of these concentrations was evaluated using wet weight. The EDI for the PTEs studied in the vegetables were below their corresponding reference levels, indicating that these vegetables were safe to be consumed. The HI of cilantro indicated the estimated non-cancer health risk is below the level of concern. In contrast, the HI values calculated for onion and Swiss chard exceeded 1, which implies the potential for non-carcinogenic health effects when these vegetables are consumed. The CR for cilantro was within the acceptable risk range for the PTEs evaluated. In contrast, the CR values for onion and Swiss chard exceeded the selected acceptable-risk criterion, with arsenic making a substantial contribution to the estimated cancer risk. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that irrigation water containing PTE concentrations within recommended limits may still contribute to the accumulation of PTEs in agricultural soils and their subsequent transfer to edible plant tissues. Further, one index, in particular dry mass basis of PTE concentrations, should not be the single factor influencing human health risk. It is therefore recommended that periodic monitoring of irrigation-water; soil and edible crop tissues, together with dietary exposure and risk assessment, be conducted to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of potential health implications.
