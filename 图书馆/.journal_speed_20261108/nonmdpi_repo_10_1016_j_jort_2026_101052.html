Outdoor recreation in a changing climate: Visitors’ impacts, coping strategies, and intent to return to Parks and Protected Areas in New Hampshire, USA

Abstract

Across the United States (U.S.), outdoor recreation participation has steadily increased over the past 15 years, reaching a record-breaking 175.8 million participants in 2023. Simultaneously, each year since 1976, the Earth's annual average surface temperature has exceeded the 20th-century mean. Research suggests that the Northeast and North Central regions of the contiguous U.S. are projected to experience the most climate-related changes in outdoor recreation participation. Resource managers and researchers are eager to better understand how climate change affects visitors' behaviors in Parks and Protected Areas (PPAs). This mixed-methods study examined New Hampshire (NH) residential visitors' perceptions of environmental and infrastructural impacts associated with annual changes in climate conditions representative of long-term trends (e.g., extreme weather events, water/wildlife, forest/foliage, season-specific conditions) and their effects on visitor coping behaviors and intent to return in NH PPAs. Web-based survey data (n = 1364) were collected in winter 2023 as part of the New Hampshire Statewide Comprehensive Outdoor Recreation Plan (NH SCORP). Findings revealed that visitors perceived climate-related impacts across all areas measured as predominantly negative, particularly in relation to winter recreation. Structural equation modeling showed that winter, summer, water/wildlife, and extreme weather conditions significantly influence visitor coping behaviors and intent to return to NH PPAs, with summer impacts uniquely bypassing coping and producing a direct negative effect on intent to return. As climate conditions continue to shift, embedding visitor behavioral data into statewide recreation planning frameworks offers a transferable model for PPAs navigating the intersection of climate adaptation and outdoor recreation management.
