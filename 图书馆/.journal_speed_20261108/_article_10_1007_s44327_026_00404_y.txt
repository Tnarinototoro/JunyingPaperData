It explores homeowners’ perceptions of the social, economic, and ecological benefits of domestic gardening and identifies the main factors influencing engagement across two socioeconomically distinct communities, East Legon and Kwabenya. A comparative quantitative survey design, complemented by illustrative field observations, was used to collect data from 226 household heads through structured questionnaires. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to examine differences in perceptions between the two communities, while the Relative Importance Index (RII) was used to rank the perceived ecological, personal, and economic drivers of domestic gardening. The results indicate significant differences between the two communities in perceptions of healthy eating ( r = 0.804), poverty reduction ( r = 0.490), and sustainable technology adoption ( r = 0.316). Despite these differences, homeowners in both communities recognized several important ecological functions of domestic gardening, particularly microclimate regulation, flood mitigation, and soil enrichment. Field observations further illustrate differences in gardening practices, ranging from ornamental and technology-supported gardens in East Legon to more food-oriented gardens in Kwabenya. The findings highlight domestic gardens as important socio-ecological assets in sub-Saharan African (SSA) cities and provide evidence for incorporating private residential green spaces into broader urban sustainability and climate adaptation strategies. Planting Field Trials Olericulture Plant Domestication Horticulture Subsistence Agriculture Plant Materials 1 Introduction One such strategy to mitigate the impact of climate change is urban agriculture, particularly domestic​ home​ or backyard gardens [ 1 ]. These gardens are integral to the urban green and blue networks and are widely recognized as a method of climate adaptation. Urban gardens make up a significant portion of urban areas globally, accounting for 16% to 36% of urban land [ 2 , 3 ]. Additionally, it is estimated that 10,000 community gardens in the U.S. have sequestered 190,000 tons of carbon over the past ten years, offsetting a year’s worth of carbon emissions for approximately 30,400 Americans [ 4 ]. Several scholars have explored and described gardens from different perspectives. For instance, Turner [ 5 ] defines gardens as “areas set aside for the cultivation and enjoyment of plants and other natural life.” His definition emphasises how gardens blend cultivation (enhancing the natural environment or producing food) with enjoyment (recreational use), distinguishing them from purely agricultural or natural areas. Similarly, Cameron [ 6 ] notes that domestic gardens are typically located adjacent to residential buildings, within a local and homeowners’ household setting. He further explains that homeowners, whether owners or tenants, have the autonomy to manage their gardens personally or hire professionals, highlighting the private nature of these spaces. SSA countries, for example, have experienced a 34% reduction in agricultural productivity growth due to the impact of climate change since 1961, more than any other region globally [ 7 ]. Given the overreliance of the population on agriculture for their livelihoods, domestic gardens are increasingly critical as supplementary food production systems. In the same vein, Ayambire [ 8 ] describe backyard gardening as the cultivation of crops in physically enclosed and private domestic spaces, referred to as ‘backyards,’ primarily for household consumption. Domestic gardens can be defined as multi-layered, multi-purpose spaces close to a family’s residence, serving as small-scale supplementary food production systems maintained by household members. These gardens also support a variety of plant and animal species, mimicking natural ecosystems. In general, domestic gardens are characterised by their size, terrain, or location, and are often established on sites that are marginal or unsuitable for field crops or pasture agriculture. According to Galhena et al., [ 9 ] and Singh et al., [ 10 ] home gardens, being near the house, contain a wide variety of plant species, while family consumption and income typically come from other sources. Urban gardening is gaining academic attention for its contributions to the ecological system, climate change mitigation, and city planning [ 11 , 12 ]. However, scientific ​literature on this topic in SSA remains limited [ 8 , 13 ]​ and existing literature mostly highlights ​ social benefits, such as stress relief and well-being [ 14 , 15 , 16 ]. Little is known about the resilience of urban gardening and how it is perceived​ [ 17 ]. In particular, ​domestic gardens have been largely overlooked in urban climate research​ in ​SSA, possibly due to their small scale, spatial fragmentation, and private nature. ​ Their ​ benefits ​ as a form of climate change adaptation remain underrecognized, especially in the Global South [ 13 , 18 , 19 ]. Despite their potential to significantly contribute to climate change adaptation, local quality of life, and various ecosystem services, research on domestic gardens remains sparse. Although ​ p​revious studies for e.g., Camps-Calvet et al., [ 20 ] have examined the contribution of gardens to ecosystem services, few have focused on how domestic gardens are perceived [ 21 , 22 ] accross socioeconomic distinct communities. In Botswana, the government introduced a backyard gardening initiative in 2010 under the National Strategy for Poverty Reduction 2003 [ 23 ]. The goals of this initiative were to eradicate poverty, promote household food security, and foster small-scale entrepreneurship and income generation among beneficiaries. In Ghana, domestic gardening is commonly practiced, but the gains and homeowners’ engagement are not sufficiently recognized in contemporary literature. Backyard gardening in Ghana constitutes the second largest form of urban agriculture, after open space farming [ 24 ]. Drechsel and Keraita [ 25 ] estimated that Accra alone has over 80,000 backyard gardens, covering approximately 50–70 hectares. This demonstrates the importance of gardens to the local population. These initiatives suggest that city authorities can promote and incentivise backyard or domestic gardening at both resident and citywide levels, as noted by ​Osei Boateng et al. [ 8 ]. The potential of domestic gardens in Ghana aligns well with the socio-ecological paradigm, which emphasizes the interconnectedness of social and ecological systems. Domestic gardens act as microcosms of social-ecological interaction, where human decisions directly impact ecological systems and vice versa. For instance, in terms of social contribution, domestic gardens provide food security, economic benefits, and promote well-being, addressing social challenges such as poverty and health issues. Moreover, in terms of ecological contribution, it was indicated that gardens can enhance urban biodiversity, mitigate heat islands, and prevent flooding, contributing to ecological stability [ 26 , 27 ]. This study acknowledges that homeowners’ choices are influenced by broader social, cultural, and environmental contexts [ 4 , 28 , 29 ]. For instance, perceptions of domestic gardening are shaped by homeowners’ awareness, environmental education, and local experiences with climate impacts. In urban areas like East Legon, strong economic and well-being benefits could drive engagement in domestic gardening due to their social and economic status, while in Kwabenya, ecological benefits like shading and food could be more profound based on economic status. The factors driving homeowners to engage in domestic gardening, such as access to fresh food, cost savings, and local environmental conditions, are also framed within this paradigm. Social norms, local knowledge, and access to resources shape homeowners’ behaviors and motivations; the importance of localised benefits is key [ 30 ]. While existing studies acknowledge the social and ecological value of domestic gardens, there is still a gap in understanding how these values are perceived and prioritised in the context of class-diverse urban environments in the Global South. Notably, little is known about how socioeconomic status, urban planning regimes, and environmental awareness influence the perceived drivers and benefits of domestic gardening in rapidly urbanizing SSA cities such as Accra. This study therefore bridges this gap by offering comparative empirical evidence on homeowners’ perceptions in two socially distinct urban neighborhoods in Ghana. This study provides both shared and divergent socio-ecological perceptions between East Legon and Kwabenya; the research adds a new layer to the literature on urban green spaces, and grounds these findings in context-specific realities that have been underrepresented in scholarly discourse. From the foregoing context, this study seeks to investigate the role of domestic gardens in two distinct urban spaces in SSA, using Ghana’s distinct urban spaces as a case study. Specifically, the following research questions are addressed in this article: What homeowners perceive as the benefits of domestic gardening? What factors drive homeowners to engage in it? The study tends to consolidate existing knowledge on the potential of domestic gardens and provide well-documented data on their contributions to the socio-ecological system in SSA, Ghana. This data could serve as a starting point for policymakers and planners looking to leverage that potential within the context of climate change adaptation policy within class-diverse urban environments in the sub-Saharan regions. 1.1 Theoretical framework: social-ecological systems (SES) Theory This study draws on Social-Ecological Systems (SES) theory to examine domestic gardening as an interaction between household practices and the urban environment. SES theory views social and ecological systems as interconnected, where human activities can influence ecological conditions and the benefits obtained from these systems can also influence subsequent human practices [ 29 , 31 ]. This perspective is relevant to domestic gardens because they are managed by households, shaped by socioeconomic and spatial conditions, and capable of generating social, economic, and ecological benefits. The SES framework identifies four interacting components: resource systems, resource units, users, and governance systems [ 31 ]. In this study, the domestic garden is considered the resource system, providing the physical and ecological setting for gardening. Resource units refer to the products and perceived benefits associated with gardening, such as fresh produce, medicinal resources, fodder and green manure, soil enrichment, shade, improved local climate, air-quality improvement, and flood mitigation. The users are homeowners who establish, manage, and benefit from domestic gardens, while governance and contextual conditions refer to the household and neighbourhood conditions that influence gardening, including access to land and water, housing conditions, income, and the use of practices such as composting and water harvesting. Gardening practices are considered the main interaction mechanism between users, the garden, and the perceived outcomes of gardening. These practices include food production, ornamental gardening, medicinal use, gardening as a hobby, composting, and the use of sustainable technologies. The framework also provides a basis for examining perceived feedback between gardening practices and their outcomes. For example, perceived benefits such as food production, improved air quality, reduced heat, soil enrichment, and flood mitigation may encourage households to continue gardening. These relationships are examined as perceived social-ecological interactions rather than direct measurements of ecological performance. The SES framework therefore provides a basis for examining how household and neighbourhood conditions influence domestic gardening practices and perceived social and ecological benefits, and how these relationships differ between East Legon and Kwabenya in the Greater Accra region of Ghana. 2 Method 2.1 Study area The study was conducted in the Greater Accra region, the administrative capital of Ghana. Specifically, East Legon and Kwabenya were used as a case study to examine how the homeowners perceive domestic gardens in two district residential classes. East Legon is a first-class residential area and Kwabenya is a second-class residential area [ 32 ]. The communities were selected due to their preference for food gardening and ornamental gardening. Kwabenya is located in Ghana’s Greater Accra Region’s Ga East Municipality with a population of 283,379 GSS [ 32 ] where our sample was drawn. The municipality falls in the savannah ecological zone. The pattern of rainfall is bimodal. The municipality also possesses a significant amount of underground water, some of which has been tapped to supply drinkable and household water to the municipality’s local towns and settlements. These areas are potential for domestic gardening due to access to water supply. East Legon, which is located in the Ayawaso West Municipality, has a population of 75,303 [ 32 ]. Operational definitions. In this study, first-class residential area refers to relatively low-density, high-income residential areas characterized by larger plot sizes, developed road infrastructure, and predominantly detached dwellings. Second-class residential area refers to relatively higher-density, middle-income residential areas characterized by mixed-density housing, ongoing residential development, and variable levels of infrastructure provision. A homeowner is defined as an individual with legal, customary, or inherited ownership of the residential property surveyed, while the household head refers to the adult household member recognized by the household as having primary responsibility for major financial, social, and managerial decisions. 2.2 Research design A cross-sectional comparative quantitative survey design was adopted for this study. The cross-sectional approach enabled the study to examine household perceptions at a single point in time and compare patterns between two socioeconomically distinct communities. The primary unit of inquiry was the household head, with respondents drawn from East Legon and Kwabenya in the Greater Accra Region. Primary quantitative data were collected using structured household questionnaires administered through KoBoToolbox, a mobile data collection platform, and subsequently analyzed using SPSS version 26. Secondary literature was used to establish the theoretical foundation of the study, particularly the Social-Ecological Systems (SES) framework, and to situate the findings within existing scholarship on urban agriculture and domestic gardening. Field photographs and brief observational descriptions were also used to provide contextual information on the physical characteristics and typologies of domestic gardens observed in the two study areas. The study was primarily quantitative and did not involve qualitative thematic or coding analysis. Instead, the analysis focused on structured measures of household perceptions and the factors influencing domestic gardening. Differences between East Legon and Kwabenya were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test, while the Relative Importance Index (RII) was used to rank the perceived social, ecological, and economic drivers of domestic gardening. The combination of survey data and illustrative field observations provided a basis for comparing both the perceived benefits and the observable characteristics of domestic gardening across the two communities. 2.3 Sampling The study employed the stratified and simple random sampling techniques. This sampling method is often used in social research to enhance representativeness by dividing the population into distinct subgroups and randomly selecting from each [ 33 , 34 ]. The study selected East Legon and Kwabenya in the Greater Accra region based on the diversity in gardening practices and socioeconomic and housing diversity. In terms of the diversity in gardening practices, it was observed that East Legon is known for its affluent status, with homeowners often engaging in ornamental gardening as part of landscape aesthetics and status display. Whereas Kwabenya, a more diverse community, includes homeowners who prioritize food gardening as a practical solution for food security and cost reduction. Regarding the socioeconomic and housing diversity, it was also observed that East Legon represents a high-income neighborhood with formalized housing layouts, while Kwabenya features a mix of middle- and low-income housing, including informal settlements. This variety in housing classifications could influence gardening space availability, choices, and the role gardens play in household economies. The sampling approach can adequately provide information on context-specific conditions like income, housing types, and space, which influence perceived benefits and driving factors in domestic gardening. This diversity ensures a robust comparison between gardening benefits, motivations and practices. This dichotomy is consistent with urban agricultural literature that highlights how gardening motivations often vary by socioeconomic status and urban context [ 8 , 35 ]. The populations of East Legon and Kwabenya are 75,303 and 283,379, respectively. The study employed stratified simple random sampling to select household respondents in East Legon (Ayawaso West Municipal District) and Kwabenya (Ga East Municipal District). To determine the sample size (n 0 ) for this study, Cochran’s [ 36 ] formula was used; $$\:{n}_{0}=\frac{{Z}^{2}\cdot\:p\left(1-p\right)}{{e}^{2}}$$ Assuming a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), 5% margin of error (e = 0.05), and a maximum variability of 50%, then n 0 = 384. The sampling frames for the study areas rely on official municipal population figures from the Ghana Statistical Service [ 32 ], which record N = 75,303 for East Legon and N = 283,379 for Kwabenya. Since the populations are finite, the formula was adjusted using the finite population correction (FPC); $$\:n=\frac{{n}_{0}}{1+\left(\frac{{n}_{0}-1}{N}\right)}$$ Yields adjusted required samples of n = 382 for East Legon and N = 383 for Kwabenya. However, due to significant field constraints, including restricted access to gated residential properties, security protocols in high-income neighborhoods, and homeowner availability, a target operational sample of 283 households (145 in East Legon and 138 in Kwabenya) was selected for questionnaire administration. A total of 226 completed and valid questionnaires were successfully obtained ( N = 116 in East Legon; N = 110 in Kwabenya), yielding an overall response rate of approximately 80% relative to the 283 targeted outreach contacts. High response rates are essential for maintaining sample representativeness and reducing non-response bias [ 37 ]. Though the achieved sample size falls below the ideal statistical baseline, it provides sufficient statistical power to detect medium-to-large effect sizes (r = ≥ 0.30) in non-parametric group comparisons (Mann-Whitney U) at α = 0.05. A sample size of 226 provides adequate statistical power (β = 0.80, α = 0.05) to detect medium-to-large effects ( r > 0.30) in non-parametric group comparisons. However, the study may have limited sensitivity to smaller effects ( r < 0.20) and subtle differences across household subgroups. Official Ghana Statistical Service (GSS) census data are reported at the municipal level, specifically for Ga East Municipal District and Ayawaso West Municipal District, rather than for the individual neighbourhoods of Kwabenya and East Legon. Therefore, the population figures used for sampling represent municipal totals and were used as the basis for estimating the sample distribution. Data collection, however, was conducted exclusively within the defined geographical boundaries of Kwabenya and East Legon. 2.3.1 Sampling strategy and participant identification Data collection targeted the household member primarily responsible for day-to-day decisions concerning property and landscape management. In line with Ghana’s statutory adult age classification, participants were required to be at least 18 years of age, the threshold at which individuals are recognized as capable of home ownership or home management. Most respondents were owner-occupiers. Where the property owner was unavailable during fieldwork, the household head or a long-term tenant with responsibility for managing the dwelling’s garden and yard was surveyed. This approach focused on the person with direct responsibility for domestic gardening rather than formal property ownership, consistent with the study’s interest in everyday decisions concerning garden and vegetation management. Non-resident domestic workers, temporary gardeners, and short-term occupants were excluded from the study. 2.4 Data collection Primary data were collected in January 2024 using a structured quantitative questionnaire comprising closed-ended Likert-scale items and categorised multiple-choice questions. The questionnaire was organized into four main sections: (1) household demographic and socioeconomic characteristics; (2) perceived social, economic, and health benefits of domestic gardening; (3) factors influencing engagement in domestic gardening; and (4) perceptions of the contribution of domestic gardening to climate resilience. The questionnaire was programmed in KoBoToolbox and administered face-to-face using the KoBoCollect mobile application. Data collection was conducted during morning (6:00 AM-10:00 AM) and evening (4:00 PM-6:00 PM) periods to increase the likelihood of reaching homeowners at home. Field observations were conducted alongside the survey using a standardized visual checklist. These observations were used to document and photograph the main garden typologies observed in the two communities, including food gardens, ornamental gardens, shade-oriented gardens, and residential plots with commercial uses. 2.5 Operationalization of the SES framework The SES framework was used to organize and interpret the variables collected through the household survey and field observations. The four SES components were operationalized as resource systems, resource units and perceived outcomes, users, and governance and contextual conditions. Gardening practices were treated as the main interaction mechanism linking homeowners with domestic gardens and their perceived outcomes. The resource system was represented by the domestic garden and its observed characteristics. Field observations were used to identify different garden types, including food-oriented, ornamental, and other forms of domestic gardening, as well as their physical and environmental characteristics. The users were the homeowners who participated in the survey. User characteristics included gender, age, education, marital status, income, household size, and length of residence. These variables were used to describe the socioeconomic characteristics of respondents and to compare household conditions between East Legon and Kwabenya. Resource units and perceived outcomes were measured through respondents’ assessments of the benefits associated with domestic gardening. These included fresh and organic food, economic benefits, well-being, stress reduction, healthy eating, poverty reduction, medicinal resources, fodder and green manure, air-quality improvement, ecosystem protection, and other environmental benefits. Climate​ and ecosystem related outcomes included heat reduction, local climate regulation, flood mitigation, soil enrichment, erosion control, biodiversity support, and CO₂ absorption. Since these outcomes were based on respondents’ perceptions, they were interpreted as perceived social and ecological benefits rather than direct measurements of ecological performance. The governance and contextual conditions were represented by the socioeconomic, spatial, and material conditions that influence gardening practices. These included household income, housing context, availability of gardening space, access to water, and household management practices. The study also considered climate-resilient practices and sustainable technologies, particularly composting and water-harvesting systems. Governance was therefore examined at the household and neighbourhood level rather than through formal institutional arrangements. Gardening practices served as the interaction mechanism connecting users, the resource system, and perceived outcomes. These practices included food production, ornamental gardening, aesthetic use, gardening as a hobby, medicinal use, composting, and the use of sustainable technologies. The Relative Importance Index (RII) was used to rank the reported motivations and benefits of domestic gardening, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess differences between East Legon and Kwabenya in perceived benefits and climate-resilience-related practices. This operationalization enabled the study to examine how household and neighbourhood conditions are associated with differences in gardening practices and perceived social and ecological outcomes. 2.6 Data analysis This study analysed the quantitative data with SPSS (v26) to generate tables and inferential statistics such as the Relative Importance Index (RII) and Mann-Whitney U-Test. 2.6.1 Mann-whitney U test The Mann-Whitney U-test was selected to compare differences in perceptions and driving factors of domestic gardening between East Legon and Kwabenya. This test was chosen due to the ordinal nature of the data, collected through Likert-scale questions, which do not satisfy the assumptions of normality required for parametric tests like the t-test [ 38 ]. The Mann-Whitney U test is widely recognized as appropriate for analyzing two independent samples with non-normally distributed or ordinal-level data [ 39 ]. The null hypothesis (H₀) stated that there was no significant difference between the response distributions in East Legon and Kwabenya, while the alternative hypothesis (H₁) stated that a significant difference existed between the two groups. The analysis was based on ranked observations, and statistical significance was assessed at the 5% level. Mann-Whitney U Test was calculated using the formula: $$ \frac{{U_{1} = N_{1} N_{2} + N_{1} (N_{1} + 1) - R_{1} }}{2} $$ (1) $$ \frac{{U_{2} = N_{1} N_{2} + N_{2} (N_{2} + 1) - R_{2} }}{2} $$ (2) Where: U = The smaller U 1 and U 2 . N 1 =Sample size of East Legon homeowners. N 2 = Sample size of Kwabenya homeowners. R 1 = Sum of ranked scores from the Likert scale for responses of East Legon homeowners. R 2 = Sum of ranked scores from the Likert scale for responses of Kwabenya homeowners. Level of Significance: 5%. A significance threshold of α = 0.05 was used. The null hypothesis was rejected when p ≤ 0.05 and was not rejected when p > 0.05. Statistical significance was denoted as p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001. To assess the magnitude of significant differences between the two communities beyond p-values, effect sizes (r) were calculated using the formula: $$\:r=\frac{\left|z\right|}{\sqrt{N}}$$ where z is the standardized Mann–Whitney test statistic and N is the total combined sample size across the two study groups. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (0.10 ≤ r < 0.30), medium (0.30 ≤ r < 0.50), and large ( r ≥ 0.50). A total of 19 separate Mann–Whitney U tests were conducted across the study indicators. To account for potential Type I error inflation from multiple testing, standardized effect sizes (r) are reported for each indicator to emphasize practical significance alongside statistical output. 2.6.2 Relative importance index (RII) The Relative Importance Index (RII) is a tool employed to assess the relative weight of highly prioritized factors. Utilizing RII, the measures required to determine the important factors were tested and analyzed. RII was employed to determine the weighted sum of all factors based on homeowners’ rankings of the factors that drive homeowners to engage in domestic gardening. One of the frequently employed tools for ranking the attributes based on their total weight in prior works is the RII [ 40 ]. These rankings enabled cross-comparison of the factors’ relative importance as reported by respondents. The RII strength lies in its capability to handle subjective judgments and quantify the relative importance of different criteria, making it a robust framework for evaluating and ranking variables. The relative importance of the factors was then calculated using a formula, yielding the relative importance index for the factors that drive homeowners to engage in domestic gardening. Using the equation below, the RII was derived. $$ {\text{Relative Important Index }} = \frac{{\sum {\text{W}}}}{{\left( {{\text{A}}*{\text{N}}} \right)}} $$ Where: W = weighting given to each factor by homeowners (ranging from 1 to 5). A = highest weight (i.e., 5 in this case). N = total number of homeowners surveyed. The greater the value of RII calculated from the equation, the more relevant each component is. The RII, which is used to rank the factors, varies depending on the component. The factors, including reduce stress, economic benefits, environmentally friendly, urban ecosystem, flexible job, overall well-being, air quality, Fresh and Organic Food, reduce poverty, healthy eating, and health of society, were included in the methods used to calculate the RII. The East Legon and Kwabenya homeowners assessed the factors on a five-point Likert scale, with strongly agree (5 points), agree (4 points), undecided (3 points), disagree (2 points), and strongly disagree (1 point). Responses of “No” to optional items were retained as valid responses and were not treated as missing data. Therefore, no respondents were excluded on this basis, and the sample size remained constant across all items included in the Mann–Whitney U tests (East Legon: N = 116; Kwabenya: N = 110). 3 Results 3.1 Demographic characteristics of respondents Table 1 shows the demographic characteristics of the respondents. 226 homeowners were surveyed to compile this data. The results show that in both communities, males are more than females, but the gender distribution is relatively balanced. In terms of age, East Legon has a younger population with a higher percentage of homeowners aged 31–45 years, while Kwabenya has more aged population (61 and above). Both communities have a similar number of homeowners with SSS/SHS and tertiary education. Over half of the respondents are married in both East Legon and Kwabenya. Most respondents earn between Ghc1000.00 and Ghc20,000.00. Household sizes show that most households in both areas have between 4 and 6 members, with a small portion having more than 6, especially in East Legon. In terms of community residency, East Legon and Kwabenya are similar, though Kwabenya has a slightly higher proportion of homeowners who have lived in the community for 6–10 years, while East Legon has a larger percentage of newer homeowners who have lived there for 1–5 years. East Legon and Kwabenya have similar demographic characteristics in terms of gender, education, marital status, income, household size, and duration of stay. The main differences appear in age distribution, with Kwabenya having a higher percentage of older homeowners (61 + years). The chi-square test shows significant differences only in the age category (p-value of 0.042), implying that age varies significantly between East Legon and Kwabenya. All other demographic factors (gender, education, marital status, income, household size, and duration of stay) show no statistically significant differences across the two areas. The findings show that 74% of respondents in East Legon and 80% in Kwabenya had a positive perception of domestic gardening. A smaller proportion reported that they were unable to engage in domestic gardening because of constraints related to time, finances, available space, and knowledge. The statistical differences between the two communities indicate a clear socioeconomic gradient to the extent to which domestic gardening contributes to household well-being. The largest difference was observed for Healthy Eating (U = 1100.0, Z = − 12.087, p < 0.001, r = 0.804), followed by Poverty Reduction (U = 3198.0, Z = − 7.359, p < 0.001, r = 0.490). For both benefits, East Legon respondents recorded substantially higher mean ranks (159.02 and 140.93, respectively) than respondents in Kwabenya (65.50 and 84.57). This suggests that households in East Legon may have greater access to the resources needed to use domestic gardens for nutritional and economic benefits, including financial resources, suitable space, and greater security of tenure. In Kwabenya, resource and space constraints may limit the scale and productivity of household gardens, restricting their contribution largely to supplementary food production. Significant differences were also observed for Economic Benefits ( r = 0.282), Overall Well-Being ( r = 0.269), Air Quality ( r = 0.222), and Fresh Food Access ( r = 0.186), with higher perceptions reported in East Legon. These findings suggest that socioeconomic conditions may influence the extent to which households are able to derive benefits from domestic gardening. However, no significant differences were found for Stress Reduction ( p = 0.372), Flexible Jobs ( p = 0.193), or Environmental Friendliness ( p = 0.168). This indicates that some perceived benefits of domestic gardening are relatively consistent across the two communities, irrespective of socioeconomic context or class-diverse urban context. In particular, the similar perceptions of stress reduction and environmental benefits suggest that domestic gardens may provide valued social and environmental benefits even where households have fewer resources. Viewed through a Social-Ecological Systems (SES) lens, these findings suggest that household capacity influences the extent to which the potential benefits of domestic gardening are realised. In East Legon, greater financial resources, secure access to land, and better infrastructure may enable households to obtain a wider range of benefits from gardening, particularly those related to food provision and economic well-being. In Kwabenya, constraints such as limited space and unreliable access to water may reduce households’ ability to expand or sustain garden production and, consequently, limit the contribution of gardening to poverty reduction. These findings highlight the importance of context-sensitive urban agriculture policies. Rather than treating domestic green spaces as homogeneous, interventions should respond to the specific constraints faced by households in different socioeconomic settings. In lower- and middle-income communities, measures such as improved access to non-potable water, gardening extension services, and protection of suitable spaces for household cultivation could help households realise the wider benefits of domestic gardening. This finding is particularly relevant in rapidly expanding tropical cities, where increasing urbanisation and the urban heat island effect contribute to rising temperatures. Domestic gardens can help reduce these effects through vegetation and shade, providing a simple household-level means of improving thermal conditions. Improving the local climate was also highly rated in both communities, with East Legon recording an RII of 0.938 (Rank 2) and Kwabenya 0.935 (Rank 2). The close scores suggest a shared recognition of the role of residential greenery in regulating local environmental conditions. Domestic gardens can influence neighbourhood microclimates through vegetation cover and evapotranspiration, while also contributing to improved air quality and thermal comfort. These functions become increasingly important as urban areas experience greater environmental and climatic pressures. Flood prevention was another highly rated benefit, with an RII of 0.869 (Rank 4) in East Legon and 0.913 (Rank 3) in Kwabenya. This finding reflects the importance of stormwater management in urban areas where impervious surfaces and inadequate drainage can increase surface runoff. Domestic gardens provide permeable surfaces that allow greater water infiltration and can help reduce the volume and speed of runoff during heavy rainfall. In this sense, residential gardens can contribute to local flood management while also complementing existing drainage infrastructure. Soil enrichment received similar ratings in both communities. East Legon and Kwabenya recorded identical RII values of 0.862, ranking fifth, respectively. This result suggests that homeowners also recognise the contribution of domestic gardening to maintaining soil quality. Gardening can support soil fertility and organic matter while sustaining vegetation and other ecological processes within residential spaces. The relatively high ranking of this factor therefore indicates an appreciation of the broader environmental value of maintaining healthy soils around the home. In contrast, economic and educational motivations received relatively low ratings in both study areas. Environmental education and research recorded an RII of 0.423 (Rank 15), producing fresh produce recorded 0.450 (Rank 14), and generating income recorded 0.529 (Rank 13). These results suggest that domestic gardens in East Legon and Kwabenya are generally not established primarily for commercial purposes or formal educational activities. Instead, their value is more closely associated with environmental benefits and personal well-being. Overall, the findings indicate that domestic gardens in East Legon and Kwabenya are valued mainly for their ecological functions and contributions to household well-being rather than for income generation or formal educational purposes. This pattern highlights the potential for urban planning and sustainability initiatives to build on existing household motivations for gardening. Supporting residential greening could therefore provide a practical means of strengthening urban environmental quality and climate resilience in both communities. Policies could support household-level measures such as rainwater harvesting and composting while also protecting and expanding shared urban green spaces and tree-canopy networks. Drawing on Ostrom’s [ 31 ] SES framework, these residential spaces can be understood as settings where governance conditions, household resources, and user characteristics shape everyday gardening practices, which in turn influence social and perceived environmental outcomes. The comparison between East Legon and Kwabenya further shows how similar natural resource systems, in this case domestic garden plots, can produce different socio-ecological outcomes when situated within contrasting socioeconomic and spatial contexts. This perspective is consistent with urban sustainability research, which suggests that residential greening is closely connected to neighbourhood infrastructure and household socioeconomic conditions [ 29 , 30 ], as illustrated by the diverse garden typologies and functional uses observed across the study sites (Fig. 1 ). The findings also show that governance and contextual conditions influence how households manage their gardens. Household economic resources, property rights, and access to municipal infrastructure shape the type and intensity of gardening practices. In East Legon, relatively higher disposable income and more reliable municipal water connections allow residents to maintain more resource-intensive gardens. These practices include the use of automated drip irrigation, purchased soil amendments, and ornamental or high-value plant species. In Kwabenya, however, water shortages, higher utility costs, and limited space encourage more resource-conserving practices, including manual bucket watering and lower-density planting. These findings are consistent with Colding et al. [ 41 ] and Langemeyer et al. [ 42 ], who show that property-rights arrangements and access to resources influence the management and ecological characteristics of urban green infrastructure. In Greater Accra, therefore, household resources help determine whether domestic gardening can be maintained as a well-supported landscape investment or remains a more constrained household activity. One of the more unexpected findings of the study is that homeowners in affluent East Legon reported higher ratings for the economic, poverty-reduction, healthy-eating, and fresh-food benefits of domestic gardening than respondents in Kwabenya. This finding differs from a common assumption in the urban agriculture literature that lower-income households derive greater material and economic benefits from household food production as a response to urban poverty [ 7 , 9 ]. Viewed through an SES perspective, several factors may help explain this result. First, financial resources influence the ability of households to sustain productive gardening. East Legon residents are more able to invest in regular irrigation, soil improvement, and other inputs during dry periods, which can support more reliable harvests of vegetables and herbs. These harvests may provide useful additions to household consumption and reduce some food expenditure [ 35 ]. In Kwabenya, water shortages and limited investment in soil improvement can reduce yields, making the economic contribution of household gardens less visible. Second, lower-income households facing wider economic pressures may evaluate small garden harvests against their overall household expenses. In such circumstances, limited production may be perceived as insufficient to make a meaningful difference to household financial pressures. Affluent households, in contrast, may view even relatively small quantities of high-value herbs and vegetables as worthwhile contributions to household food consumption. Third, the higher ratings among East Legon respondents may also reflect differences in the cultural meaning attached to gardening. In more affluent settings, home food production can be associated with health, wellness, organic consumption, and environmental responsibility [ 14 , 15 ]. Gardening in these contexts may therefore be valued not only for the quantity of food produced but also for the lifestyle and health benefits associated with growing and consuming fresh food. Finally, limited space and unreliable water infrastructure in areas such as Kwabenya can make continuous food production difficult. The material benefits that households perceive from domestic gardening are therefore shaped not only by economic need but also by access to resources, cultural meanings, and the wider level of household security. The interaction between homeowners and their immediate environments also produces strong perceived ecological feedback in both study areas. Respondents associated domestic gardens with cooling, improved air quality, and reduced stormwater runoff, with heat reduction and microclimate regulation emerging as particularly important motivations for maintaining residential greenery. These findings are important within an SES framework, but they also require a clear distinction between perceived environmental benefits and measured biophysical effects. This study did not include direct environmental monitoring, such as recording ambient temperatures before and after gardening interventions, measuring particulate matter using sensors, or sampling stormwater runoff. The reported environmental benefits therefore represent residents’ perceptions and experiences of their surroundings, including their responses to shade, vegetation, environmental quality, and perceived environmental risks. This distinction is important because perceived environmental conditions do not always correspond directly with measured environmental conditions. Nikolopoulou and Lykoudis [ 43 ] and Tomatis et al. [ 44 ] for example, show that perceptions of outdoor microclimates can differ from objective measures of thermal comfort and environmental conditions. Perceived cooling in East Legon or Kwabenya may therefore result from immediate access to shade, the presence of vegetation, or a sense of psychological comfort rather than from a measured reduction in temperature. Recognising this distinction avoids making claims about the biophysical performance of domestic gardens that cannot be established from the present data. At the same time, these perceptions remain important because they can influence how residents value, maintain, and invest in their gardens. Viewing domestic gardens as interconnected SES units also provides useful implications for urban planning and climate adaptation in SSA cities [ 45 , 46 ]. Urban greening policies often assume that households have similar capacities to establish and maintain green infrastructure and can benefit from it in comparable ways [ 47 , 48 ].The findings of this study challenge this assumption. Differences in water access, household resources, available space, and gardening inputs mean that households do not have equal capacity to maintain domestic gardens or obtain the same benefits from them. Targeted structural support could therefore help address some of these differences. In lower-income communities such as Kwabenya, municipal support for non-potable irrigation water, affordable organic inputs, and urban agricultural extension services could strengthen the connection between domestic gardening and household food production [ 49 ]. In East Legon, initiatives could focus on linking household-level practices such as composting and rainwater harvesting with neighbourhood programmes, allowing individual gardening practices to contribute to broader environmental management [ 35 ]. Such interventions would recognise that household gardening is not only a private activity but can also contribute to wider urban environmental goals. Overall, the findings suggest that the benefits and practices associated with domestic gardening are shaped by the interaction between household resources, infrastructure, environmental conditions, and residents’ perceptions. Domestic gardens therefore have different meanings and functions across socioeconomic contexts, even when households are managing similar types of green space. Equitable urban climate resilience requires planning approaches that recognise these differences and address the infrastructural constraints that limit the capacity of lower-income households to benefit fully from domestic green spaces [ 8 , 50 ]. 5 Limitation of study and future research This study has some methodological limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the geographic scope was limited to two neighbourhoods, East Legon and Kwabenya, in the Greater Accra Region. Although these sites were selected to represent contrasting socioeconomic contexts, they do not capture the full range of urban and peri-urban conditions across Ghana or Sub-Saharan Africa. Domestic gardening practices, motivations, and constraints are shaped by local environmental conditions, cultural practices, land availability, and tenure arrangements. Therefore, the findings may not be directly generalized to other urban contexts. Future research should include a wider range of ecological zones and settlement types to provide a more comprehensive basis for context-sensitive urban greening policies. Second, the timing and duration of data collection may have introduced sampling bias. Fieldwork was conducted over one week and was limited to two daily periods, from 6:00–10:00 AM and 4:00–6:00 PM. Although these periods were selected to increase the likelihood of contacting household heads, they may have favoured residents who were at home during these hours, such as retirees, home-based workers, and unemployed residents, while underrepresenting full-time commuters and shift workers. Additionally, the final sample ( n = 226) fell short of the initial target calculated through Cochran’s [ 36 ] formula (Sect. 2.3), primarily because the initial baseline population figures encompassed areas beyond our tightly defined study boundaries. The initial calculation was based on population estimates that covered areas broader than the specific study boundaries, and the smaller achieved sample may have reduced the statistical power and precision of the estimates. A key limitation concerns the spatial resolution of the population data, as official GSS statistics are reported at the municipal level (Ga East and Ayawaso West) rather than for the specific neighbourhood boundaries of Kwabenya and East Legon. Access restrictions and heightened security protocols in gated, high-income properties limited access to some households in East Legon, creating a potential non-response bias toward more accessible, non-gated properties. Consequently, findings on high-income household characteristics should be interpreted with some caution, as the most exclusive residential enclaves may be underrepresented. Future studies should use larger samples, extend data collection to weekends and a wider range of times, and employ more precise neighbourhood-level population and boundary data. Third, the indicators of environmental and climate-resilience benefits were based on respondents’ perceptions rather than direct environmental measurements. Although these subjective assessments provide useful information about how homeowners understand the benefits of domestic gardening, they should not be interpreted as evidence of measured ecological performance. In particular, the study did not directly measure variables such as ambient temperature, air quality, carbon fluxes, or stormwater retention. Future research could combine household surveys with environmental monitoring to assess the extent to which perceived benefits correspond to measurable ecological outcomes. Finally, field observations and photographs were used primarily to provide descriptive context for the survey findings (Sect. 2.4) rather than as a systematically coded qualitative dataset. Consequently, the observations provide contextual evidence but do not support a detailed qualitative analysis of gardening practices. Future studies could strengthen this approach by combining survey data with systematic qualitative methods and direct environmental measurements. Such an integrated design would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how domestic gardens function as social-ecological assets within different urban contexts. 6 Conclusion and recommendations This study examined homeowners’ perceived benefits and key drivers of engagement in domestic gardening across two socioeconomically distinct communities in Greater Accra, Ghana: East Legon and Kwabenya. The findings show that domestic gardening is widely valued in both communities, with generally positive perceptions across socioeconomic contexts. Engagement is driven primarily by perceived ecological benefits, particularly heat reduction, local climate regulation, flood mitigation, and soil enrichment, rather than by commercial food production or formal educational activities. Despite these shared ecological priorities, significant differences were observed in health, economic, and technology-related dimensions. Homeowners in East Legon reported stronger perceptions of healthy eating, economic benefits, and poverty reduction, as well as greater engagement with composting and sustainable gardening technologies. In Kwabenya, residents placed greater emphasis on direct environmental functions such as air-quality improvement and carbon absorption but faced greater spatial, financial, and infrastructural constraints, particularly in relation to reliable water access. These differences suggest that although domestic gardens function as important social-ecological assets in both communities, household resources and local conditions influence the extent to which their potential benefits can be realized. The findings have implications for urban planning and policy in Greater Accra. Domestic gardens should be recognised not only as private amenities but also as potential components of urban green infrastructure and climate adaptation. Supporting residential greening could complement municipal efforts to reduce heat exposure, manage stormwater, improve soil conditions, and enhance local environmental quality. However, policy support should reflect differences in household capacity. In communities such as Kwabenya, interventions should prioritize practical constraints, including improved access to non-potable water for irrigation, affordable organic inputs, and urban agriculture extension services. Such measures may be more effective than awareness campaigns alone in enabling households to sustain and expand gardening activities. In higher-income areas such as East Legon, municipal authorities could encourage further adoption of sustainable practices through appropriate incentives, including recognition programmes or other mechanisms that support rainwater harvesting, composting, and efficient irrigation. Across both communities, participatory planning could help identify neighbourhood-specific constraints and align household gardening practices with broader climate-resilience objectives. A differentiated approach that combines household-level support with citywide green infrastructure planning could strengthen the contribution of domestic gardens to more resilient and socially inclusive urban environments in Greater Accra and other cities in Sub-Saharan Africa.
