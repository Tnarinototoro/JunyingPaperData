However, evidence on how different dimensions of CIS, including awareness, access, and intensity of use, influence CSA adoption remains limited. This study examines the relationship between CIS and CSA adoption among 550 smallholder maize farmers in Ghana’s Ashanti Region. Using Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) to estimate adoption under exposure and Generalized Propensity Score Matching (GPSM) to evaluate the effects of CIS use intensity, we distinguish between the effects of access to climate information and the intensity with which farmers engage with multiple information sources. The results show that universal exposure to CIS and CSA could substantially increase adoption rates, indicating considerable unmet potential for climate adaptation. Institutional factors, including farmer training, credit access, farmer organizations, and knowledge, significantly increase both CIS use and CSA adoption. However, greater CIS use intensity is weakly associated with lower CSA adoption intensity (β = −0.425, p = 0.091), suggesting that expanding the number of information channels alone may not translate into greater technology uptake. This relationship may reflect information quality limitations, conflicting advisories, institutional constraints, or increasing information demands relative to farmers’ capacity to act, although these mechanisms were not directly measured. The findings highlight the importance of complementing climate information with effective extension services, financial inclusion, and institutional support to strengthen climate adaptation and promote sustainable agricultural development among smallholder farmers. Information Studies Agriculture Climate-Change Adaptation Agricultural Geography Climate Change Management Agricultural Economics 1 Introduction As one of the world’s most important staple crops, maize underpins global food systems by providing over 20% of global cereal-derived calories and anchoring the livelihoods of hundreds of millions of smallholder farmers across tropical and subtropical regions [ 1 ]. In Ghana, maize is the most widely cultivated cereal, grown on over 1.5 million hectares annually, and serves as a primary income source for smallholders, contributing up to 70% of annual earnings [ 2 , 3 ]. Yet average productivity stagnates at 1.5–2.5 MT/ha, well below the 5–6 MT/ha achieved in Latin America and Asia, reflecting significant unrealised potential that Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA) adoption could help unlock [ 4 ]. Climate change compounds these challenges. Since 2000, Ghana has experienced a 12% decline in rainfall and temperature increases of up to 1.8 °C [ 5 , 7 ]. In northern Ghana, growing seasons have shortened from 120 to 90 days, while fall armyworm infestations affect nearly 50% of maize farms annually and yield losses reach up to 50% [ 8 , 9 , 10 ]. Projections indicate 15–30% further yield declines by 2050 under high-emission scenarios [ 11 , 12 ]. Within this context, Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA) has emerged as a system-transition pathway that simultaneously enhances productivity, resilience, and emissions efficiency [ 13 , 14 , 15 ]. In Ghana, CSA practices such as drought-tolerant maize varieties, conservation agriculture, and agroforestry have delivered measurable benefits in yield stability, reduced vulnerability, and household welfare [ 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ]. Scaling CSA, however, requires timely, localised, actionable information. Climate Information Services (CIS), here defined as the production, translation, and delivery of climate data in forms usable for farm decision-making (weather forecasts, seasonal rainfall predictions, drought warnings, and agronomic advisories, delivered via radio, mobile phones, extension agents, and farmer-based organisations), are therefore a cornerstone of Ghana’s resilience strategy [ 20 , 21 ]. Their value, however, depends on whether information translates into farm-level decisions [ 22 ]; Fraisse et al. [ 23 ] showed that climate information generates value only when farmers have a defined adaptive response and the institutional support to act on it. In Sub-Saharan Africa, CSA adoption can raise smallholder productivity by 30–60%, but scaling requires credit, extension, and farmer organisations alongside information [ 24 , 25 ]; in West Africa, timely advisories raised maize yields by about 20% in Burkina Faso and early warnings cut pest losses by about 30% in Nigeria [ 26 , 27 ]. In Ghana, CIS users adopt CSA at higher rates than non-users [ 27 , 28 ]. Yet how the intensity of CIS use, not merely access, shapes adoption remains poorly understood, particularly in West Africa where infrastructure and digital-literacy deficits limit farmers’ ability to act on advisories. Despite its potential, CIS adoption in Ghana remains uneven and weakly understood as a systemic driver of adaptation. While aggregate adoption rates can appear high when CIS is broadly defined to include radio broadcasts and extension-based advisories, as is the case in the present study, access remains substantially lower among resource-poor, remote, and marginalized households, and the effective utilization of CIS for farm decision-making is considerably more limited than raw adoption figures suggest [ 3 , 29 ]. Existing studies primarily conceptualize CIS as a binary exposure (access versus no access) and focus on average adoption or yield effects [ 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ], thereby overlooking the fact that CIS operates as a dynamic information system whose impacts depend on intensity of use, frequency of engagement, and integration into farm decision-making. Moreover, while earlier work documents determinants of CSA uptake and the general role of CIS in adaptive capacity [ 34 , 35 , 36 ], little is known about how CIS access interacts with CSA adoption intensity to shape adaptation outcomes [ 37 , 38 ]. Empirical evidence is also geographically skewed toward Eastern and Southern Africa [ 39 , 40 ]; Ouedraogo et al., 2024), limiting insights for West Africa’s distinct agroecological and institutional context. In addition, potential unintended and distributional effects, such as dependence on external forecasts, exclusion of digitally marginalized groups, and the reinforcement of existing inequalities, remain largely unexplored [ 41 , 42 ]. These gaps constrain understanding of whether CIS merely increases information availability or fundamentally restructures adaptive pathways within maize systems. This study addresses these gaps by modelling how the access to and intensity of CIS influence CSA adoption among maize farmers in Ghana’s Ashanti Region. It makes three contributions: (1) it models CIS as a continuous intensity variable rather than a binary exposure, the first such application in the West African smallholder context; (2) it applies GPSM to estimate dose-response relationships between CIS intensity and CSA adoption intensity, advancing beyond binary treatment designs; and (3) it provides the first empirical test of a theoretically predicted divergence between CIS access effects (positive) and CIS intensity effects (negative) on CSA adoption. Drawing on Rogers’ [ 43 ], Diffusion of Innovations framework and Information Gap Theory [ 44 ], we expect access to enable adoption by moving farmers through the knowledge and persuasion stages, while high intensity may paradoxically suppress it when information accumulates faster than farmers’ resources and institutional support allow them to act. Testing this divergence in the Ghanaian smallholder context is the paper’s central empirical contribution, with direct relevance for Ghana’s National Climate-Smart Agriculture and Food Security Action Plan and for SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) [ 2 , 45 ]. 1.1 Conceptual framework CSA adoption depends not only on access to CIS but on the extent to which information is actively used in farm decisions [ 21 , 46 ]. Drawing on Rogers’ [ 43 ] Diffusion of Innovations (DOI) theory, adoption is a multi-stage process, knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation. Access first builds knowledge [ 47 ], but actual use is what persuades farmers and enables informed decisions [ 37 ]. Socio-economic characteristics (sex, age, education, marital status, household size) shape the knowledge stage [ 48 ]; farm-level attributes (land size, experience) and communication assets (radio, TV, phones, internet) determine exposure and capacity to use CIS [ 49 ], with limited technology or digital literacy impeding the shift from knowledge to use, consistent with Partial Population Exposure [ 50 ]. Institutional factors, training, extension, farmer-group membership, market access, government support, and credit, help bridge knowledge and adoption [ 27 , 51 ]. These map onto Information Gap Theory (IGT), in which individuals seek information to close gaps between what they know and what they need to know [ 44 ]. Crucially, the CIS-intensity–adoption relationship need not be linear: at low-to-moderate levels, more use builds knowledge and persuasion and enables adoption, but at high levels, when content is frequent yet poorly contextualised, complex, or unsupported, DOI predicts farmers stall at persuasion or decision [ 43 ], while IGT predicts that excessively wide or unresolvable gaps suppress rather than motivate action [ 44 ]. This intensity-dependent divergence is the study’s central proposition (depicted in the framework below) and yields two directional hypotheses. H1: Access to CIS is positively associated with the likelihood of CSA adoption (knowledge and persuasion stages of DOI). H2: Higher CIS-use intensity is negatively associated with the number of CSA practices adopted at high exposure levels, consistent with information overload or unresolvable information gaps (IGT), though these mechanisms are not directly measured. This framework informs the empirical design: the IPW and GPSM covariates (sex, age, education, household size, land size, farmer-group membership, credit and extension access, and CIS knowledge and perception indices) correspond to the DOI stages; CIS intensity (a continuous count of CIS sources used) is the GPSM treatment, operationalising “actual use,” and CSA-adoption count is the outcome, capturing implementation (See Fig. 1 below). Fig. 1 Full size image Conceptual framework 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Study area and data source The research was conducted in the Ashanti Region of Ghana, specifically within maize-producing communities in the Mampong and Ejura Sekyedumase Municipalities (see Fig. 2 ). The Ashanti Region is Ghana’s second most populous region, home to approximately 5.4 million people, representing about 16% of the national population [ 52 ]. As a major agrarian and commercial hub, agriculture engages nearly 42% of the regional labor force, with maize, cassava, cocoa, and plantain constituting key livelihood and market commodities [ 2 ]. Maize (Zea mays L.) remains the dominant cereal crop, contributing significantly to household food security, livestock feed supply, and smallholder income generation. Despite this importance, the region faces persistent constraints, including declining soil fertility, rainfall variability, and low adoption of improved technologies [ 11 ]. Fig. 2 Full size image Map of the study area Mampong and Ejura Sekyedumase lie in Ghana’s Forest–Savannah Transitional Zone, one of the country’s most productive yet climate-vulnerable agro-ecological zones. It has a semi-humid tropical climate with bimodal rainfall (peaks April–July and September–November), averaging 1,200–1,500 mm annually and 26–32 °C, though rainfall has become increasingly erratic (delayed onset, early cessation, dry spells, occasional flooding). Dominant ferric acrisols and ochrosols are moderately fertile but prone to leaching and erosion, and farming is largely rain-fed, exposing smallholders to drought and soil degradation. The zone shares biophysical and production characteristics with major maize areas in Brong-Ahafo, Eastern, and parts of Northern Ghana, and its intermediate conditions capture both the technological opportunities and climatic constraints of Ghana’s maize sector, making findings broadly relevant to comparable agro-ecological zones across Ghana and West Africa. The study employed a quantitative research design, using structured questionnaires for data collection and analysis. According to records from the Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA) district offices and agricultural extension agents, the population of smallholder maize farmers in the two municipalities was approximately 5,200. To ensure data quality, the questionnaire was pre-tested, enumerators were trained, and completed forms were checked for consistency and completeness before analysis. However, potential uncertainties such as recalling errors in self-reported data and response bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Furthermore, as the study employs a cross-sectional observational design, causal interpretation of estimated effects requires caution. While the IPW and GPSM approaches address observable selection bias by conditioning on a rich set of covariates, unobserved confounders, such as farmer motivation, risk preferences, or social network effects, may still bias estimates. Table A1 (Appendix) shows the districts, communities, and sample size estimation from each community. To ensure robustness and account for potential non-response or incomplete surveys, the sample size increased by 50%, resulting in a target of 557 maize-farming households. A total of 550 valid responses were obtained after data collection. The multistage sampling procedure was designed to enhance representativeness across different maize-producing communities within the study area. In the first stage, the Ashanti Region and the Mampong and Ejura Sekyedumase Municipalities were purposively selected because of their strategic importance in maize production and their exposure to increasing climate variability. In the second stage, four maize-producing communities were selected from each municipality based on maize production intensity, accessibility, and variation in exposure to Climate Information Services (CIS). In the final stage, farmer lists obtained from the Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA), extension officers, and farmer-based organizations were used as sampling frames from which respondents were selected using simple random sampling procedures. This approach minimized sampling bias and ensured adequate representation of smallholder maize farmers across different socio-economic and agro-ecological conditions within the study area. Sampling weights were not applied because the sampled households were proportionally distributed across the selected communities relative to their farming populations. Data were collected through structured questionnaires administered face-to-face with respondents, given relatively low literacy levels and limited digital infrastructure in rural communities. Trained enumerators fluent in the local language facilitated interviews to enhance comprehension and accuracy. The questionnaire captured information on household socio-economic characteristics, access to CIS, adoption of Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA) practices, and welfare outcomes, including yield, net farm income, and food security status. 2.2 Empirical estimation strategies In this study, descriptive tools including means, frequencies, and charts were employed to present farmers’ socio-economic characteristics, CIS access, and CSA adoption. The Partial Population Exposure (PPE) method was used to examine the extent of CIS exposure and CSA adoption. To assess both the determinants and the impacts of CIS use and CSA uptake, the Generalized Propensity Score Matching (GPSM) model was applied. 2.2.1 Estimation of exposure and adoption rates of CIS and CSA with inverse probability weights (IPW) The estimation of exposure and adoption rates of Climate Information Services (CIS) and Climate-Smart Agricultural (CSA) practices was carried out using the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) framework [ 54 ]. The approach estimates the expected population mean adoption outcome \(\left({E\left({{p_i} - {p_0}} \right)} \right)\) while distinguishing between two potential outcomes for each farmer: \({p_i},\) the adoption outcome if exposed to CIS or CSA, and \({p_0},\) the adoption outcome if not exposed. Exposure is represented by the binary variable \(w,\) where \(w = 1\) indicates exposure to the technology and \(w = 0\) indicates no exposure. The conditional expected values, \(E ({p_0}|w = 1)\) and \(E ({p_0}|w = 0)\) correspond to the average treatment effect on the treated (ATE1) and the average treatment effect on the untreated (ATE0), respectively. These values represent the mean adoption outcomes for the exposed and unexposed subpopulations. The observed adoption outcome \(p\) is given by \(w{p_1},\) where \(p\) reflects the observed status of exposure (1 if exposed, 0 otherwise). Therefore, the population mean joint exposure and adoption parameter (JEA) and population adoption gap (GAP), are respectively give $$JEA = E\left(p \right) = E \left(1 \right) \left({w{p_1}} \right), GAP = E\left(p \right) - E \left({{p_1}} \right)$$ (2) This gap reflects the unmet demand for CIS or CSA adoption due to insufficient exposure across the population. The identification of ATE is based on the conditional independence assumption, which states that the treatment status \(w\) is independent of the potential outcomes \({p_1}\) conditional on the observed set of covariates \(x\) that determine exposure \(\left(w \right).\) These covariates include farmer characteristics (gender, age, education, household size, and farming experience), farm-level factors (land size, quantity of maize harvested, livestock ownership), institutional factors (access to extension, credit, and farmer associations), and technology attributes (perceptions of affordability, accessibility, and ease of use). Simtowe et al. [ 50 ] explain that ATE represents the population mean adoption outcome, equivalent to the adoption rate assuming all farmers are exposed to CIS or CSA. This population mean outcome serves as the most accurate estimate for adoption studies [ 54 ]. The population adoption gap (GAP), also known as the non-exposure bias (NEB), represents the difference between ATE and JEA. It captures the shortfall in adoption caused by limited exposure. Meanwhile, the Population Selection Bias (PSB), calculated as the difference between ATE and ATE1, reflects whether farmers in the exposed subpopulation have similar adoption probabilities to the overall population. The ATE parameters can be estimated from a random sample of observed data using a weighting estimator or an estimator based on a parametric regression procedure. Here, the inverse probability weighting (IPW) estimator of ATE was adopted. The weight estimator utilizes a two-step process. Initially, the propensity score \(\left({P\left(z \right)} \right),\) which is the likelihood of treatments (CIS or CSA) given the covariate vector \( z,\) is calculated. In the second step, the ATE, ATE1, and ATE0 are estimated using probability weighting estimators. These estimators are specific cases of the general weighting estimators for ATE, ATE1, and ATE0 when \({p_0} = 0,\) as developed by Diagne and Demont [ 54 ] $$\widehat {ATE} = {1 \over n}\mathop \sum \limits_{i = 1}^n {{{y_i}} \over {\hat p\left({{Z_i}} \right)}}$$ (3) $$\widehat {ATE} = {1 \over {{n_e}}}\mathop \sum \limits_{i = 1}^{{n_e}} {{{y_i}} \over {\hat p\left({{Z_i}} \right)}}$$ (4) $$\widehat {ATE} = {1 \over {n - {n_e}}}\mathop \sum \limits_{i = 1}^n {{(1 - \widehat {p\left({{Z_i}} \right))}} \over {\hat p\left({{Z_i}} \right)}}{y_i}$$ (5) where the estimate of the propensity score is evaluated at the covariate vector and the sample of exposed farmers. For CIS, the outcome is adoption (1 = adopted, 0 = otherwise) and the treatment is awareness (1 = aware, 0 = otherwise). For CSA, the outcome is the number of CSA practices adopted (a count reflecting intensity rather than a binary decision), maintained consistently across the IPW and GPSM analyses. Four terms are used distinctly throughout: CIS awareness (knowing a service exists); CIS adoption, used interchangeably with CIS access (an aware farmer taking up at least one channel; the IPW outcome); CIS use intensity, used interchangeably with CIS intensity (the count of channels actively used, 0–9; the GPSM treatment); and CSA adoption (the number of CSA practices taken up). Z denotes farmer factors (gender, age, farming experience, household size, education, off-farm work), farm factors (land size), institutional factors (farmer association, extension, credit), and technology-attribute perceptions (accessibility, user-friendliness, reliability). 2.2.2 Generalised propensity score matching (GPSM) We used generalized propensity score matching (GPSM) to estimate the distributional impact of CIS use on CSA adoption. Although CIS intensity is a count (0–9), a Gamma GLM with a log link was chosen for the first-stage GPS rather than a Poisson or negative-binomial model: the GPSM framework [ 55 , 56 ] treats the continuous treatment via a flexible parametric family, and the Gamma is well suited to a continuous, non-negative, right-skewed exposure (Appendix Fig. A2 ). Descriptive statistics confirm this: CIS intensity has a mean of 2.40 channels (SD = 1.17), a median of 2, and positive skewness of 0.47 ( n = 550), with values ranging from 0 to an observed maximum of 7 of the 9 channels available—a unimodal, non-negative, right-skewed distribution consistent with the Gamma approximation (Appendix Fig. A2 ). Among continuous-treatment alternatives (log-normal, inverse Gaussian) the Gamma minimised both AIC and BIC, and residual diagnostics, standardised deviance residuals against fitted values and a Q–Q plot against the theoretical Gamma, showed no systematic departure except mild tail deviation (Appendix Fig. A3 ). We treat the Gamma as an adequate approximation for first-stage GPS estimation rather than an exact distributional claim, consistent with the tolerance built into Hirano and Imbens [ 55 ]. GPSM extends standard PSM [ 57 ] to continuous treatments [ 55 , 58 ]. CIS intensity is the count of distinct CIS channels a farmer actively used in the year (see Table A4 ), from 0 to a maximum of 9 available in the study area; because the treatment is continuous and multi-valued, binary PSM is inappropriate. Estimation follows the three-step approach of Mahama et al. [ 56 ]. In step one, the generalized propensity score (GPS) is estimated as a function of the treatment and covariates: $$S = s\left({T,X} \right)$$ (6) where S is the GPS, T is the treatment (climate information levels), and X is a vector of covariates, s(T, X) is the conditional density of the treatment given the covariates. The density function can be mathematically denoted as: $$s\left({T,X} \right) = {f_{{T \over X}}}\left({{t \over x}} \right)$$ (7) The conditional density is divided into strata with the same values, and within each stratum, it is assumed that the probability of Y = y is independent of X. This assumption is referred to as the balancing property in standard propensity score modelling. The balancing property and the assumption of confoundedness regard the treatment as being a random treatment [ 56 ]. The second step involves the estimation of the conditional expectation of the outcome, in this case, the adoption of climate-smart agricultural practices. Based on the assumptions, the conditional expectation of the outcome is estimated using the treatment and the GPS to eliminate the estimation bias due to differences in covariates [ 58 ]. The conditional expectation of adoption of climate-smart agricultural practices is estimated as a function of climate information levels and the estimated GPS, S (Fig. A1 for common support). This is specified as: $$\beta \left({t,s} \right) = E\left\{ {Y\left(t \right){\rm{|}}s\left({t,X} \right) = s} \right\} = E\left({Y{\rm{|}}T = t, S = s} \right) $$ (8) From Eq. 3 , the dose function is specified as: $$\mu \left(t \right) = E[\beta \left\{ {t,s\left({t,X} \right)} \right\},t\epsilon T$$ (9) where \(\beta \left({t,s} \right)\) is the conditional expectation of adoption of climate-smart agricultural practices use. We estimated the dose-response function from the different levels of the treatment from the 1 st , 2 nd , 3 rd and 4 th quartiles by taking an average of the conditional expectation, \(\hat \beta ,\) estimated from the initial step over the treatments’, t levels. The estimated dose-response function μ(t) cannot be given a direct causal interpretation when considered in isolation [ 55 ]. However, when the conditional expectation is averaged over the distribution of covariates at each treatment level t, it yields the marginal dose-response function for the treatment. According to Serrano-Domingo and Requena-Silvente [ 59 ], a causal interpretation is obtained when the dose-response function at one treatment level, μ(t), is compared with that at an alternative level, μ(t’), as the difference between them represents the average treatment effect of moving from t’ to t. Following the estimation of Mahama et al. [ 56 ], the outcome based on the estimated parameters is specified as: $$\widehat {\mu \left(t \right)} = {1 \over N}\mathop \sum \limits_{N = 1}^N (\widehat {{b_0}} + \widehat {{b_1}t} + \widehat {{b_2}}{\rm{log}}\left({\widehat {pscore}} \right) + \widehat {{b_3}}{t^2} + \widehat {{b_4}}{(\log \left({\widehat {{\rm{pscore}}}} \right))^2} + \widehat {{b_5}}\left({\log \left({\widehat {{\rm{pscore}}}} \right)t} \right) $$ (10) Because the treatment (CIS use) is continuous, PSM is inapplicable and GPSM is used. CIS intensity was measured as the count of distinct CIS channels a farmer accessed during the most recent growing season, radio broadcasts, mobile SMS advisories, extension agents, television forecasts, community information centres, and farmer-group meetings, from 0 (none) to the total available. This captures the breadth of engagement rather than single-channel preference, consistent with recent CIS work treating multi-channel access as an intensity proxy [ 21 , 47 ]. One limitation is that counting channels without weighting by frequency, quality, or trust treats all channels as equally informative, which may introduce measurement error if impact is driven more by information quality than breadth; weighted CIS indices are a priority for future work. Fig. 3 Full size image Exposure and adoption rates of CIS and CSA practices among maize farmers As shown in Fig. 3 , Panel A, awareness of CIS among the surveyed farmers is particularly high, with 85.71% reporting awareness of CIS practices. Among those who are aware, 80.91% have adopted CIS, indicating strong uptake relative to awareness levels. Nevertheless, 14.29% of respondents remain unaware, and nearly one-fifth (19.09%) of aware farmers have not adopted CIS. This suggests that while outreach and dissemination efforts have been largely effective, awareness alone does not guarantee adoption. Barriers such as forecast timeliness, lack of localized information, and limited tools for interpretation may still inhibit full utilization of CIS. These findings are consistent with earlier studies in Ghana. Baffour-Ata et al. (2024) reported similarly high awareness of CIS in the Atwima Nwabiagya District, with 86% awareness of weather warnings and 70% awareness of daily forecasts, largely driven by the need for proactive planning and reduced crop losses. Tahidu [ 35 ] also documented widespread access to CIS across six regions of Ghana, particularly for rainfall onset information, although adoption of climate-responsive practices remained limited. Compared to these studies, the relatively high adoption rate observed in this study (80.91% among aware farmers) suggests stronger engagement with CIS, possibly due to more targeted interventions or improved access to localized climate information. Nonetheless, the persistence of the awareness–adoption gap underscores the continued importance of addressing usability and relevance challenges. In contrast, Fig. 3 , Panel B illustrates awareness and adoption of CSA practices, which are comparatively lower than those observed for CIS. The results show that 74.18% of respondents are aware of CSA practices, and among them, 66.55% have adopted at least one CSA practice. However, 25.82% of respondents remain unaware, and 33.45% of those who are aware have not adopted CSA. This indicates a substantial adoption gap, reflecting the greater complexity and resource requirements often associated with CSA practices. Evidence from previous studies supports this pattern. Shaibu et al. [ 68 ] found that although farmers in semi-arid regions were aware of CSA practices such as intercropping and mulching, adoption varied considerably, particularly for capital-intensive practices like irrigation. Asante et al. [ 69 ] similarly demonstrated that adoption of reduced tillage, row planting, and drought-tolerant seed varieties improved maize yields and income, yet uptake was constrained by limited access to information, finance, and production inputs. Amanor and Antwi-Agyei [ 48 ] further highlighted the complexity of CSA adoption decisions, showing that farmers often combine multiple CSA practices depending on access to extension services and land tenure security. In addition, Quarshie et al. [ 70 ] emphasized the role of digital tools, such as mobile phones and radio, in expanding CSA awareness, while noting that limitations in digital literacy and tool availability continue to hinder adoption in rural areas. 3.3 Inverse probability weights (IPW) estimates of adoption rates of CIS and CSA practices among maize farmers Table 3 presents the Inverse Probability Weights (IPW) estimates of adoption rates for Climate Information Services (CIS) and Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA) practices among maize farmers. The Average Treatment Effect (ATE), which captures the full population adoption rate under hypothetical universal exposure, is 0.846 for CIS and 0.792 for CSA. This implies that if all farmers were exposed to these practices, approximately 85 and 79%, respectively, would adopt CIS and CSA. These ATE values represent the potential or true adoption rates and are thus the most appropriate measures for evaluating adoption outcomes, as they correct for exposure bias, unlike the Joint Exposure and Adoption (JEA) rate, which only reflects actual adoption among those already exposed [ 54 ]. The ATE values in Table 2 suggest a high latent demand and suitability for both CIS and CSA practices. In contrast, the JEA rates are 0.857 for CIS and 0.742 for CSA. The small adoption gap for CIS (ATE – JEA = −0.011) indicates that current exposure nearly matches potential adoption, with no statistically significant difference. This suggests that most farmers who are exposed to CIS are already adopting it, and exposure levels are broadly adequate. However, the adoption gap for CSA is 5% (ATE – JEA = −0.050), and this is statistically significant at the 5% level. This finding indicates that current exposure to CSA practices is insufficient and that the adoption rate could rise by at least 5 percentage points if exposure were expanded. This aligns with findings by Asante et al. [ 71 ], who argue that low adoption rates of agricultural innovations in developing countries are often the result of incomplete exposure rather than farmer disinterest or resistance. The results reveal that factors such as sex, training on CIS, training on CSA, access to credit, membership in maize farmers’ groups, and knowledge levels positively influenced CIS use. Specifically, the findings suggest that being male, participating in training programs, having access to credit facilities, belonging to organized farmer groups, and possessing higher awareness scores are associated with higher levels of CIS use. These results underscore the role of targeted capacity-building, financial inclusion, and social networks in promoting the uptake of climate-related services [ 72 ]. Conversely, variables such as age, marital status, ownership of basic mobile phones, and perception index exhibited significant negative effects on CIS use. This indicates that older farmers, married individuals, those relying on basic phones rather than smartphones, and those with more negative perceptions of CIS are less likely to use such services extensively. The finding that being male positively predicts CIS use, while female farmers are significantly less likely to use CIS intensively, reflects well-documented socio-cultural barriers specific to the Ashanti Region and broader Ghanaian context. Women farmers in the region typically face intersecting constraints, including time poverty from domestic responsibilities, restricted mobility limiting access to extension meetings and market centres, lower rates of phone ownership and digital literacy, and limited control over household income needed to invest in information-enabling technologies [ 2 , 73 ]. Social norms in the Ashanti patrilineal system also tend to concentrate agricultural decision-making authority with male household heads, reducing women’s agency in adopting new practices even when they perform the majority of farm labour [ 48 ]. Similarly, the negative age effect on CIS use reflects reduced digital literacy and greater reliance on traditional knowledge systems among older farmers, as well as lower uptake of radio-based advisory formats that require active engagement with new information channels [ 11 ]. These demographic barriers underscore the need for gender-responsive and age-sensitive CIS delivery strategies, including community-based women’s advisory groups, literacy-independent voice messaging, and peer-to-peer information sharing through farmer organizations, to ensure equitable access to climate services across the Ashanti Region. Table 5 presents GPS model estimates of the effects of CIS intensity on CSA adoption. The central result is a negative coefficient on CIS intensity (β = −0.425, p = 0.091), which is marginally significant at the 10% level. While this result provides tentative empirical support for H2, its marginal significance warrants caution against over-interpretation. The finding is suggestive evidence of a negative intensity–adoption gradient rather than a definitive causal conclusion and should be interpreted alongside the theoretical mechanisms and broader pattern of results discussed below. Two additional coefficients in Table 5 provide insight into the mechanism. First, the strong positive coefficient on GPS scores (β = 1.323, p < 0.05) indicates that a farmer’s structural capacity and institutional embeddedness, shaped by characteristics such as formal training, credit access, land size, and farmer group membership, are considerably more consistent predictors of CSA adoption intensity than information exposure alone. This implies that farmers who are institutionally well-positioned benefit from CIS, while those who are not may gain little regardless of how many services they access. Second, the insignificant interaction term (CIS×GPS scores: β = −0.192, p > 0.10) showed that the effect of CIS on farmers was roughly the same, regardless of how structurally advantaged or disadvantaged a farmer was. In other words, CIS did not hurt poorer or less-informed farmers more than others; it affected nearly all farmers similarly. This tells us that the problem is not about any one group of farmers; rather, it is a broader, system-wide issue with the disconnect between information and action in this setting. Fig. 5 Full size image Average number of CSA practices on the full sample 4 Conclusion and recommendations This study examined the role of Climate Information Services (CIS) in enabling Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA) adoption among smallholder maize farmers in Ghana’s Ashanti Region. Using descriptive statistics, Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW), and Generalized Propensity Score (GPS) estimation, the findings reveal that while CIS and CSA awareness were high, adoption levels lagged, highlighting a significant adoption gap. The IPW analysis shows that if all farmers were exposed, adoption could increase for both CIS and CSA, demonstrating strong latent demand. Socio-economic and institutional factors such as training, access to credit, farmer group membership, and knowledge significantly enhance CIS use, while age, marital status, and reliance on basic phones limit uptake. More critically, higher intensity of CIS use is negatively associated with the number of CSA practices adopted, a counterintuitive divergence that this study theorises and finds tentative empirical support. Beyond access, however, farmers need to be able to make (effective) use of climate information for it to bring any useful information in their agricultural and related activities. Even if farmers have access to climate information, if they are not able to put it to good use through processing and using the information for agricultural or other activities (such as adaptation and mitigation activities), it is not going to make much difference. To enhance adoption outcomes, targeted policy interventions are needed. The broader implication is relevant for climate policy in Ghana’s maize-producing zones and, by extension, for comparable agroecological contexts in West Africa where similar smallholder constraints prevail. Information delivery must be integrated with the institutional scaffolding that makes that information actionable, including credit access, quality extension services, farmer group networks, and targeted training. The IPW analysis further reveals a substantial population adoption gap for CSA, with approximately 5% of eligible farmers remaining outside the CIS exposure system, indicating significant unrealised adaptive potential across the region that well-designed outreach programmes could unlock. The Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA), in collaboration with the Ghana Meteorological Agency and agricultural extension services, should strengthen CIS delivery by tailoring forecasts to local contexts, improving timeliness, and promoting user-friendly platforms accessible via smartphones, radio, and community centers. Importantly, while the study highlights the potential of digital CIS platforms, the study reveals that smartphone ownership among surveyed farmers is low (23.3%), and that most rely on basic mobile phones and radio for information access. This implies that CIS delivery strategies should prioritize radio-based and SMS-compatible channels rather than smartphone-dependent digital platforms, at least in the short-to-medium term, until digital literacy and infrastructure gaps are adequately addressed. Credit and financial inclusion policies should be expanded to enable farmers to invest in CSA practices once they receive CIS: since credit access (β = 0.199, p = 0.003) is among the strongest institutional predictors of CSA adoption in this study, subsidised credit schemes and village savings and loan associations (VSLAs) directly linked to CIS outreach programmes represent a high-priority policy lever. Extension training programmes should emphasise not just information delivery but the actionable interpretation of advisories, given that training on CIS and CSA (β = 0.321, p = 0.001) significantly increases adoption likelihood; MoFA and the Ghana Meteorological Agency should scale up participatory agro-meteorological training across the Ashanti Region. Farmer-based organizations (FBOs) should be further strengthened to promote peer learning and collective resource mobilisation: with farmer group membership (β = 0.776, p < 0.001) emerging as the single strongest predictor of CIS adoption, FBO support programmes offer the highest return-on-investment for policy targeting. Finally, the integration of CIS and CSA into agricultural education curricula, paired with digital and radio literacy campaigns tailored to smallholder contexts, will ensure long-term sustainability and generational transfer of climate-resilient practices. Several limitations merit acknowledgement. The data are cross-sectional and self-reported, limiting causal interpretation and admitting recall bias. The study covers two municipalities in the Ashanti Region, so findings may not generalise to zones with markedly different institutions, tenure, or climate. GPSM addresses observable confounding but cannot remove unobserved heterogeneity (e.g., motivation, risk preferences) in a cross-sectional design, nor fully rule out reverse causality, farmers with lower CSA adoption may seek more CIS to compensate, partly explaining the negative intensity–adoption association. Finally, the equal-weighted CIS-intensity measure may attenuate the estimated relationship. Future work should use longitudinal or instrumental-variable designs, objective CIS-quality measures, wider geographic scope, and moderation analysis of institutional conditions within the GPSM framework to validate this component of the theoretical model.
