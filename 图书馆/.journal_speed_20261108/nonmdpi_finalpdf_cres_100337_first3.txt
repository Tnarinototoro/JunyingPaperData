ABSTRACT
Over the past 25 years, Ghana's Community Resource Management Area (CREMA) program has expanded to 
cover 2 million hectares across 600 communities, aiming to achieve its dual objectives of biodiversity conser-
vation and poverty reduction. As Ghana's flagship Community-Based Natural Resource Management (CBNRM) 
initiative, it has attracted significant investment from international organizations for livelihood interventions to 
reduce poverty and incentivize conservation. Yet little is known about how these interventions are implemented 
or who benefits. Using qualitative methods, this study examines stakeholders' perceptions of the types, socio-
economic impacts, and equity dynamics of livelihood interventions in three CREMAs in northern Ghana. Our 
findings reveal that interventions that enhance existing livelihoods, such as modern beekeeping, remain the most 
frequently implemented. Although the socioeconomic impacts of these interventions vary across and within 
CREMAs, they provide individuals with modest, seasonal income and support community development projects. 
The gender dimensions of the interventions show that gender-targeted, pro-poor distribution norms have 
increased women's inclusion and access to cash income but also raise concerns about exclusion and intersectional 
inequities. By situating our findings within debates on distributional and contextual equity, we offer insights into 
how pro-poor norms interact with gender and other contextual factors to shape economic and equity outcomes in 
incentive-based conservation. This article is relevant beyond the case study, as it can inform the design of 
equitable, context-sensitive, and sustainable livelihood interventions in current and future incentive-based 
conservation efforts across the tropics and beyond.
1. Introduction
The long history of the deprivation of use rights associated with 
protected area establishment led to resentment of biodiversity conser-
vation among local communities (Garland, 2008; Vasquez and Sunder-
land, 2023). Access and use rights to forests and their resources enjoyed 
by local communities prior to the imposition of fortress conservation 
systems were either completely restricted or significantly reduced 
(Duffy, 2010). The mid-1980s saw increased recognition of local com-
munities as owners and guardians of customary territories and greater 
efforts to rectify decades of displacement and exclusion from these ter-
ritories (Dressler et al., 2010). Community-based natural resource 
management (CBNRM) emerged as a conservation approach that em-
powers communities to collaboratively manage their natural resources 
to protect biodiversity and spur livelihoods and economic growth in 
rural areas (Adeyanju et al., 2021; Foli et al., 2018; Murphree, 2009). 
Variations of CBNRM have been rolled out in almost all regions of the 
world, including Africa, Asia, Latin America, and North America 
(Dressler et al., 2010; Bulkan et al., 2022). Some of the earliest CBNRM 
programs in Africa were established in southern Africa, including

Ghana's official CBNRM program began in 2000 with the introduc-
tion of the Community Resource Management Areas (CREMAs) program 
by the Wildlife Division of the Ghana Forestry Commission. CREMAs 
were established to encourage active local participation in natural 
resource management, leading to biodiversity conservation and 
improved livelihoods (Adeyanju et al., 2021; Asare et al., 2013; Forestry 
Commission of Ghana (FC), 2004). CREMAs are often areas with sig-
nificant forest and wildlife resources, where communities collaborate to 
manage their shared natural resources sustainably (Agyare, 2017). 
CREMAs are promoted as a successful model of community-based nat-
ural resource management in Ghana and beyond (Adeyanju et al., 2021; 
Agyare, 2017), with at least 59 CREMAs registered at the Wildlife Di-
vision of the Ghana Forestry Commission (World Bank, 2024). A total of 
45 CREMAs have been mapped, and many others are at various stages of 
development (World Bank, 2024).
As with many CBNRM programs, the CREMA scheme largely de-
pends on donors and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) for initial 
and ongoing funding and technical support (Adeyanju et al., 2021; 
Baruah et al., 2016; see Appendix A in the Supplementary material for 
key ongoing or completed donor-funded projects in Ghanaian CREMAs). 
These external actors (donors and NGOs), including the United Nations 
Development Program (UNDP), International Union for Conservation of 
Nature (IUCN), and Arocha Ghana, play an important role in financing 
livelihood interventions (e.g., ecotourism, shea nut certification, and 
beekeeping) to reduce poverty and unsustainable resource exploitation 
(Agyare et al., 2015a; Baruah et al., 2016; Dzekoto and Bosu, 2018; Gilli 
et al., 2020). Hence, most communities anticipate improved livelihoods 
from participating in the CREMA program (Agyare et al., 2015a; Bem-
pah et al., 2019; Gilli et al., 2020).
Prior research has examined the conservation, economic, and so-
ciocultural outcomes of the CREMA program (Agyare et al., 2015a, 
2015b, 2024). However, these studies have primarily relied on struc-
tured survey data, which, while valuable for generalization, are often 
inadequate to produce rich, contextual data on complex topics (Newing 
et al., 2011). Rural livelihoods are complex, diverse, and dynamic, 
shaped by intersecting political and environmental processes across 
scales (Scoones, 2009). Moreover, existing scholarship tends to treat 
livelihood interventions – the main mechanisms for achieving CREMA's 
economic objectives – as secondary to conservation outcomes, rather 
than the focus of analysis (Bayala et al., 2024; Bempah et al., 2019). Few 
studies have critically evaluated these interventions, and existing evi-
dence suggests that CREMA communities have achieved only limited 
economic empowerment (Mawutor and Hajjar, 2024). This lack of 
focused analysis represents a critical gap in understanding how CREMAs 
deliver on their livelihood goals. Our study addresses these shortcom-
ings by characterizing the nature, scope, impacts, and dynamics of 
livelihood interventions within three CREMAs in northern Ghana. Spe-
cifically, we ask: (1) how can various livelihood interventions imple-
mented within CREMAs be categorized; (2) how do key 
stakeholders—community members, government officials, NGOs, and 
donors—perceive the socioeconomic impacts of livelihood interventions 
within CREMAs; and (3) how are the benefits of livelihood interventions 
distributed within and across CREMAs, by gender, and what factors 
determine the extent of the impact of livelihood interventions? By 
addressing these questions, the study provides the empirical depth 
policymakers and practitioners need to understand how introduced in-
terventions have affected participating communities. Ultimately, this 
work informs evidence-based policies to guide the design and effective 
integration of livelihood interventions and poverty-reduction strategies 
into CREMAs and similar programs across the region.
We present the conceptual background in Section 2. Section 3 out-
lines the methodology, including the study context (Section 3.1), case 
study selection (Section 3.2), and data collection and analysis (Section 
3.3). Sections 4 and 5 present and synthesize the results and discuss the 
findings. Section 6 concludes the article and offers recommendations for 
policy and practice.
2. Conceptual framework
This paper draws on the literature on incentive-based conservation 
(Charnley, 2023; Suich, 2013; Wright et al., 2016). Past conservation 
program failures have often been attributed to a lack of tangible benefits 
for local communities (Chambers et al., 2022; Duffy, 2010). In response, 
conservation organizations have shifted toward incentive-based mech-
anisms, such as alternative livelihood programs and market-based ap-
proaches, to address human well-being and other development goals 
alongside conservation objectives (Hajjar et al., 2021; Roe et al., 2015; 
Wicander and Coad, 2018).
Most CREMAs in Ghana operate as incentive-based conservation 
initiatives (World Bank, 2024; see Section 3.2) and meet the four criteria 
outlined by Charnley (2023): (1) they are formally established through 
an external initiative; (2) the initiative is driven by forest and wildlife 
conservation goals; (3) they alter current or customary forest access, use, 
and management to facilitate conservation; and (4) extrinsic incentives 
to participate are part of the initiative. At the core of these initiatives are 
livelihood interventions designed to generate economic benefits and 
meet local needs (Roe et al., 2015; Wright et al., 2016).
The impact of incentive-based conservation approaches on local 
livelihoods varies widely and can be positive, negative, or neutral 
(Charnley, 2023; Roe et al., 2015; Suich, 2010). Even when outcomes 
are positive, benefits often accrue at the community or higher levels 
rather than reaching poor and marginalized households (Hajjar et al., 
2021; McDermott and Schreckenberg, 2009). These disparities have 
brought social equity considerations to the forefront of incentive-based 
conservation approaches (Friedman et al., 2022; Klein et al., 2015; Law 
et al., 2018; McDermott and Schreckenberg, 2009).
Equity in this context is multidimensional, encompassing procedural, 
distributional, recognitional, and contextual elements (Law et al., 2018). 
Without equitable participation (procedural equity), marginalized 
groups (i.e., across gender, age, social status, ethnicity, and wealth) risk 
exclusion, enabling elite capture of benefits (distributional equity) 
(Friedman et al., 2022; Leisher et al., 2016). By respecting local norms, 
knowledge systems, and rights, incentive-based conservation ap-
proaches can better respond to the varied aspirations and needs of local 
people (recognitional equity) (Sene-Harper et al., 2019; Wright et al., 
2016). Finally, the social, political, economic, and cultural contexts in 
which these approaches are implemented critically influence their out-
comes (contextual equity) (Hajjar et al., 2021; Klein et al., 2015; Law 
et al., 2018). Ignoring these contextual factors can undermine both 
conservation and livelihood objectives.
Our work specifically analyses the distributional equity dimension of 
livelihood interventions within CREMAs, examining how benefits are 
apportioned across genders and what factors shape these outcomes. By 
doing so, it contributes to the literature on incentive-based conservation 
by linking equity and contextual considerations to the effectiveness of 
livelihood interventions.
