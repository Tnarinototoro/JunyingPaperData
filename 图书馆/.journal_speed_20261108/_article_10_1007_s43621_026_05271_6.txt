Abstract Regulators increasingly treat disclosure quantity as the main policy lever for sustainable finance. This conceptual paper argues that disclosure revises investor beliefs only insofar as investors regard it as reliable, and that reliability is shaped largely by assurance providers, who verify, and rating agencies, who aggregate. We develop the credibility wedge, the proportional discount investors apply to disclosed sustainability information, and distinguish it from disclosure quality, assurance quality, rating disagreement, information uncertainty and ambiguity. We specify a preparer-assurer-rater architecture with alternative information paths drawing on costly state verification, signalling, ambiguity-based asset pricing and institutional theory. A formal model shows that wider disclosure scope raises the credible information available to investors unless reliability falls more than proportionately with scope; when verification dilution and rater disagreement operate together, this reduces to a testable inequality between two estimable parameters. Twelve conditional propositions link regime design to the wedge, the wedge to the precision of investor beliefs and, through ambiguity, to the cost of capital. A typology based on observable verification strength and input comparability reveals four regimes in 2026: the European Union, jurisdictions adopting IFRS S1 and S2, the United States, and India. The Core of India’s Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR) glide path allows for fuzzy regression-discontinuity designs, eight estimable models of which are specified. The report-counting indicator for target 12.6 of the 2030 Agenda measures the volume of disclosures, not decision-useful information.
