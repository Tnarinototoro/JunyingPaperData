Al-Ghamdi * Environmental Science and Engineering Program, Biological and Environmental Science and Engineering Division, King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST), Thuwal, Saudi Arabia Article metrics View details Abstract Often promoted as a key to greener cities, walkability is widely praised for its sustainability, yet the extent to which it can be considered truly sustainable remains unclear. Despite its growing prominence in urban planning and policy, the true environmental, social, and economic impacts of walkability remain largely assumed rather than rigorously quantified. This perspective examines walkability through the three sustainability pillars, highlighting both its potential benefits and its overlooked trade-offs. Rather than treating walkability as an inherently sustainable solution, this perspective frames it as context-dependent, shaped by urban form, climate conditions, infrastructure design, and patterns of use. By synthesizing existing sustainability assessment tools and frameworks, including life cycle–based, social, and economic evaluation approaches, the perspective outlines pathways for more rigorous, holistic, and data-driven evaluations of walkability. Such approaches are essential for equitable, environmentally sound, and economically viable urban policies, and ensure that walkability meaningfully contributes to long-term urban sustainability. Introduction Unsustainable transportation systems are among the main contributors to global environmental challenges, particularly climate change, air pollution, and energy depletion (Khalili et al., 2019; Lyfenko and Ponkratov, 2025; Colvile et al., 2001; IPCC, 2023a). In 2019, the transportation sector accounted for approximately 15% of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, equivalent to 8.7 GtCO2-eq, and contributed to 23% of energy-related CO2 emissions worldwide (IPCC, 2023a, 2023b). Road vehicles dominate transport emissions, responsible for 70% of total transport-related CO2, followed by aviation, shipping, and rail (IPCC, 2023b). These systems often prioritize private vehicle use over public or active transport, resulting in traffic congestion and longer commute times. Rapid urbanization and growing population densities have increasingly strained cities’ transportation infrastructure, making it more difficult to maintain efficient access to goods, services, and opportunities (Çolak et al., 2016). Car-centric systems also reduce space for pedestrians and cyclists, undermining urban livability and discouraging healthier, more sustainable mobility options, while contributing to poor air quality and public health risks. Unsustainable transportation systems also have important social and economic implications. Car-centric cities often prioritize wealthier populations who can afford private vehicles, while low-income residents face limited access to reliable public transport or are forced to become car owners despite financial contains (Vermesch et al., 2021). This inequity can reduce job opportunities, increase commuting costs, and exacerbate social disparities. Economically, traffic congestion and inefficient transport systems increase fuel expenses and strain city budgets for road maintenance and infrastructure expansion. Promoting sustainable, accessible, and affordable mobility options can therefore improve both equity and economic efficiency, creating more inclusive and resilient urban environments. Addressing the environmental, social, and economic challenges of unsustainable transportation is therefore critical for building more livable and resilient cities. As cities focus on creating more sustainable transportation systems, walkability emerges as the beacon of progress, illuminating a path to healthier, greener, and more connected urban environments. However, while walkability is often celebrated as a sustainable solution, there remains a lack of comprehensive scientific studies that quantify its true sustainability and the extent of its impact (Jeong et al., 2023). Sustainability is often treated as an assumed output rather than a rigorously evaluated condition. In reality, the economic, social, and environmental advantages of walkability can differ significantly based on daily use patterns, infrastructure design, urban form, and climate. Although improvements in pedestrian connectivity may lower transportation-related emissions, they may also necessitate more resource consumption, continuous maintenance, and material-intensive retrofitting. In a similar vein, walkable environments can improve accessibility and social interaction, but they can also create equity issues if benefits are not shared equally or if rising land values cause displacement. Walkability can reduce long-term transportation and healthcare costs from an economic standpoint, but it may also come with high upfront costs and opportunity costs, especially in cities that prioritize cars. These possible trade-offs highlight the necessity of going beyond normative claims and systematically assessing whether, and under what circumstances, walkability can be truly regarded as sustainable. Rather than establishing another conceptual link between walkability and sustainability, this Perspective argues that walkability should be treated as a sustainability intervention whose performance must be demonstrated through quantitative assessment rather than assumed from its intended benefits. Previous studies have examined the environmental, social, and economic dimensions of walkability and have highlighted associated benefits and trade-offs; however, these dimensions are rarely evaluated within a coordinated assessment structure that allows their results to be examined together while retaining their distinct boundaries and uncertainties. We therefore propose a sequential and iterative assessment architecture that combines established environmental, social, and economic assessment methods to quantify the benefits and trade-offs of a walkability intervention relative to a defined baseline, spatial boundary, functional unit, and time horizon. The resulting evidence can then be examined jointly to identify synergies, conflicts, distributional effects, and uncertainty across the three sustainability pillars, rather than assuming that improvements in one dimension necessarily imply overall sustainability performance across the three pillars. Walkability and environmental sustainability The development of walkable cities is widely regarded as a key strategy to enhance environmental sustainability, as it is frequently promoted as a practical and effective means of reducing the urban environmental footprints of urban areas. By reshaping how cities consume land, energy, and natural resources, walkability has the potential to generate meaningful reductions in emissions across transportation and energy infrastructures (Baobeid et al., 2021). At its core, walkability encourages a modal shift away from motorized transportation toward human-powered mobility, particularly for short routine trips. For instance, replacing vehicle trips with walking can significantly reduce per capita CO2 emissions by lowering dependency on cars, buses, and trains for everyday commutes, thereby reducing the vehicle kilometers traveled (VKT). For example, in Europe, light-duty passenger vehicles have an official emission baseline of approximately 120 gCO2/km, but real-world driving conditions often result in emissions as high as 168 gCO2/km, with emission peaks reaching up to 193 gCO2/km under heavy congestion due to inefficient driving speeds and stop-and-go traffic (Fontaras et al., 2017). Similarly, in Bangkok, light-duty diesel and gasoline vehicles show slightly different averages, at 182.5 gCO2/km and 171.01 gCO2/km, respectively, with emissions rising to 232.25 gCO2/km at lower driving speeds due to increased fuel consumption (Sirithian et al., 2022). By reducing reliance on such vehicles, walkable urban environments can substantially cut emissions while also lowering demand for vehicle production, which in turn reduces impacts associated with raw material extraction, manufacturing, and end-of-life disposal, along with air pollution. A reduced reliance on motorized transport also translates into lower fuel consumption and environmental degradation. As fewer vehicles are required, the demand for extensive parking infrastructure decreases, freeing valuable urban land that can be repurposed for green spaces (Croeser et al., 2022). While long-distance travel will inevitably continue to rely on motorized transport vehicles, walkable cities can dramatically reduce daily vehicle use, particularly when supported by compact urban form, accessible services, and safe, well-connected pedestrian infrastructure. However, transitioning toward walkability is not without accompanying tradeoffs. Achieving high-density, mixed-use, walkable neighborhoods often requires a fundamental redesign of existing car-centric urban areas, involving large-scale changes to the built environment (Abuwaer et al., 2023), such as modifications to road space, sidewalks, crossings, drainage, lighting, etc. These transformations demand significant additional material inputs, energy use, ongoing maintenance, and associated embodied environmental impacts, particularly when existing infrastructure must be substantially reconstructed or retrofitted (Noland and Hanson, 2015; Zokaei Ashtiani and Muench, 2022). Accordingly, the environmental benefits of walkability should be evaluated alongside the embodied impacts associated with infrastructure construction, replacement, and maintenance using life-cycle approaches. Walkability may also influence travel and shopping behavior by improving access to nearby commercial destinations. Proximity to supermarkets and other daily-goods stores has been associated with greater walking frequency, while recent evidence suggests that proximity to commercial establishments can increase the likelihood of walking for some shopping trips (Cao, 2015; Cao et al., 2009; Liao et al., 2020). Such changes may contribute to reduced reliance on motorized travel and associated emissions, although their sustainability implications depend on whether increased local accessibility translates into fewer or shorter motorized trips. However, existing evidence does not establish that greater proximity necessarily increases shopping frequency or repetitive purchasing. Therefore, changes in shopping behavior should be considered a context-dependent factor when assessing the broader sustainability outcomes of walkability. Walkability and social sustainability Walkability and social sustainability are interlinked, though outcomes depend on planning and implementation. Well-designed pedestrian infrastructure, sidewalks, safe crossings, shaded paths, and plazas require modest public investment yet deliver inclusive, low-cost mobility. By reducing reliance on private vehicles, walkability eases transport burdens, improves air quality, and lowers healthcare costs. Beyond environmental benefits, walkable environments improve mental and physical health by easing congestion, reducing stress, and encouraging daily activity, lowering risks of chronic disease. They also foster social sustainability by creating vibrant cultural and recreational hubs that host markets, performances, and community events. Such spaces strengthen local identity, encourage interaction across diverse groups, and deepen residents’ sense of belonging, enhancing the urban social fabric. Accessibility is central to walkability and social sustainability. Universal design, curb ramps, tactile paving, accessible signals, rest areas, and barrier-free pathways enable people of all ages and abilities to participate in public life, though some groups still require complementary transit for inclusive mobility. Safety is equally vital: lighting, clear sight lines, raised crosswalks, and traffic-calming measures reduce risks, while active street life fosters “eyes on the street,” enhancing both perceived and actual safety (Wekerle, 2000). The built environment also shapes walking behavior; well-maintained sidewalks, shaded paths, and inviting public spaces improve comfort and reinforce neighborhood identity. In hot climates, climate-sensitive design is critical to balance comfort, safety, and environmental impact (Abuwaer et al., 2025). Walkability interventions may also involve opportunity costs because urban road space, parking, municipal budgets, and public right-of-way are finite resources. Reallocating road or parking space to pedestrian infrastructure can reduce the space available for other transport uses and may involve trade-offs among competing mobility priorities and urban-development needs (Chen et al., 2020; González-Guzmán and Robusté, 2011; Douglas et al., 2023). These decisions can affect different population groups unevenly, depending on their mobility needs, accessibility to alternative transport options, and reliance on private vehicles. Therefore, the distribution of these costs and benefits should be considered alongside broader issues of accessibility, inclusion, and equity when evaluating walkability interventions. The magnitude of such opportunity costs depends on local land values, infrastructure conditions, transport demand, and the alternatives forgone, and should therefore be evaluated on a project-specific basis. Without equity safeguards, walkability can reinforce existing inequalities. In low-income areas, it often reflects necessity rather than choice, exposing residents to pollution, heat, and flooding. At the same time, there is growing evidence that both residential and retail gentrification are positively correlated with improvements in walkability, especially those linked to increased neighborhood amenities, improved connectivity, and proximity to transit. Rising property values and commercial rents can cause displacement and disrupt established social networks, ultimately undermining social cohesion, as walkable areas become more appealing to higher-income residents and businesses (Gibbons and Appleyard, 2025). These dynamics demonstrate that walkability is not socially neutral and that different populations experience different advantages and disadvantages. Meanwhile, gentrification tied to walkability projects can drive displacement and erode social cohesion. Only through equitable planning that links affordable housing, inclusive design, and climate-adaptive infrastructure can walkability truly serve all communities. Walkability and economic sustainability Walkability enhances economic sustainability by lowering long-term infrastructure costs, boosting local economies, and increasing resilience to climate and energy shocks. Upfront investments in pedestrian and climate-resilient infrastructure, shaded paths, permeable pavements, and elevated walkways are offset by lower maintenance and reduced damage from extreme weather, protecting both mobility and urban economies (Baobeid et al., 2021). Walkability boosts local economies by increasing foot traffic, supporting tourism, and making cities more attractive to residents and investors. Reduced car dependence lowers household transport costs, while health benefits cut healthcare spending. Pedestrian infrastructure also creates jobs and enhances resilience, and by lowering fossil fuel use, walkable cities protect against energy price shocks and may generate value through carbon credits. On the other hand, sustaining walkability brings some economic challenges. Initial capital requirements can be substantial when walkability improvements involve major reconstruction or retrofitting of existing car-oriented streets. However, costs vary considerably according to the intervention and whether improvements can be incorporated into planned resurfacing or other scheduled infrastructure works. Published assessments show that some road-diet interventions can be implemented at relatively modest additional cost when coordinated with resurfacing, whereas larger pedestrian and complete-street projects can require substantially greater investment (FHA, n.d.; Shapard and Cole, 2013). These variations highlight the importance of project-specific cost–benefit and life-cycle costing analyses rather than treating high upfront costs as an inherent feature of all walkability interventions. Insufficient municipal budgets can lead to neglected maintenance, while climatic and environmental risks may discourage pedestrian activity, limiting economic gains. Resistance from stakeholders, including businesses dependent on cars and policymakers focused on short-term returns, can further impede walkability projects. Transitions away from car-dependent mobility may also generate labor-market adjustments in sectors closely associated with conventional automobile use, including some vehicle manufacturing, maintenance, and fuel supply chains. Evidence from broader low-carbon transport and energy transitions indicates that employment losses can occur in affected automotive and fossil-fuel sectors, while new employment opportunities may emerge elsewhere, creating challenges related to skills mismatches and workforce adjustment (de Ruyter et al., 2024; Wang and Lo, 2021). Thus, rather than assuming that walkability directly causes net job losses, the employment implications should be understood as part of a broader structural transition and assessed in terms of both displaced and newly created employment, including the potential role of retraining and labor-market policies. Tackling these challenges requires integrated urban planning that aligns economic goals with social equity and environmental resilience. By meeting diverse community needs and incorporating climate-adaptive infrastructure, walkability can drive sustainable economic development while promoting inclusivity and long-term urban livability. Walkability quantification via sustainability tools and frameworks The lack of a comprehensive, holistic framework for evaluating walkability through the sustainability lens presents a critical gap in current literature. This limitation restricts our ability to fully assess the walkability’s true value and long-term impact. Nevertheless, there are a variety of already existing tools and metrics that offer a solid foundation from which researchers can begin to quantify walkability’s role in shaping a sustainable urban future. Sustainability assessment evaluates sustainability across environmental, social, and economic dimensions, offering a holistic view of systems, processes, or products (Elegbede et al., 2023). It spans boundaries from cradle-to-gate, gate-to-gate, cradle-to-grave, and cradle-to-cradle for circularity. Standardized under the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), Life cycle assessment (LCA) tracks environmental impacts, such as carbon emissions, ozone depletion, acidification, human toxicity, energy use, resource depletion, and eutrophication, through four iterative stages: (i) defining the goal and system boundaries, including the functional unit; (ii) compiling a life cycle inventory (LCI) of material and energy flows and associated emissions; (iii) conducting life cycle impact assessment (LCIA); and (iv) interpreting results to ensure consistency, robustness, and reliability (Cao, 2017). Complementary approaches, such as social life cycle assessment (S-LCA) and life cycle costing (LCC), extend this life cycle perspective to capture social outcomes and economic performance across the full life cycle. S-LCA assesses the potential social impacts of products, services, or systems across their life cycles, typically considering dimensions such as working conditions, human rights, health and safety, community impacts, and equity among relevant stakeholder groups. LCC, on the other hand, evaluates the costs associated with a product, service, or intervention over its life cycle, including initial investment, operation, maintenance, replacement, and other relevant costs, thereby providing a basis for comparing the long-term economic performance of alternatives. Combined, these tools provide a comprehensive lens to understand and guide sustainable development. Other tools used to assess the environmental impact include the sustainable process index (SPI), an ecological footprint method that calculates the total land area required to sustainably embed all material and energy flows, including emissions to air, water, and soil, throughout the life cycle of a product or service (Kollmann et al., 2016). In the context of walkability, SPI can help evaluate how compact, pedestrian-friendly city layouts reduce ecological demand by minimizing land required for roads, lowering fuel consumption, and decreasing embodied emissions from vehicles. Carbon footprinting tools can also be applied, as they are intended to guide relevant emission reduction efforts and verification processes (Pandey et al., 2011). These tools enable the quantification of GHG emissions avoided through reduced VKT as more people choose to walk instead of driving. Additionally, frameworks, such as the social vulnerability index (SVI) and health impact assessment (HIA), can be used to capture the social benefits of walkability, including improved public health outcomes and increased accessibility for vulnerable populations. Cost–benefit analysis (CBA), on the other hand, can be used to evaluate the economic feasibility of walkable city interventions by comparing implementation costs with long-term savings in healthcare, transportation, and infrastructure. Ultimately, fully understanding the sustainability of walkability requires integrating multiple evaluation tools and frameworks, as illustrated in Figure 2. Comprehensive assessments are essential to generate robust evidence on the environmental, social, and economic impacts of walkable urban environments, revealing both benefits and trade-offs to accurately measure the true sustainable impact of walkability and guide effective decision-making. A practical assessment of a walkability intervention could follow a sequential but iterative framework that links the three sustainability pillars. First, the intervention and its functional unit, spatial boundary, time horizon, and baseline scenario should be defined. LCA and carbon-footprint analysis can then quantify changes in material use, energy demand, emissions, and other environmental impacts associated with infrastructure construction, maintenance, and changes in travel behavior. In parallel, LCC and CBA can assess capital, maintenance, operating, and broader economic costs and benefits. S-LCA, SVI, and HIA can subsequently evaluate distributional effects, accessibility, health outcomes, and potential impacts on vulnerable populations. The results can then be integrated to identify synergies and trade-offs across environmental, social, and economic dimensions of sustainability, followed by sensitivity and uncertainty analyses to test the robustness of the findings. A key methodological challenge is that these tools operate at different spatial and temporal scales and use different outcome measures. For example, LCA may evaluate environmental impacts over the full life cycle of infrastructure, whereas SVI and HIA may focus on present-day or shorter-term population-level outcomes. Similarly, aggregate economic benefits identified through CBA may mask unequal distributions of costs and benefits across social groups. Therefore, the integration of these tools should not rely on a single composite score. Instead, a multi-criteria assessment can retain the individual results while explicitly identifying where environmental benefits, economic efficiency, and social equity reinforce or conflict with one another. This approach would allow walkability interventions to be evaluated not only in terms of whether they increase walking, but also whether the resulting environmental, social, and economic outcomes are sustainable over time. Moving forward, researchers should develop standardized, data-driven frameworks that quantify walkability across all sustainability pillars, enabling rigorous, comparable, and actionable insights. Policymakers can then leverage this evidence to design equitable, climate-adaptive urban policies that maximize the advantages of walkability while addressing material, energy, and social considerations. By combining holistic assessment with thoughtful urban planning, walkable cities can be strategically cultivated to enhance livability, resilience, and long-term sustainability for diverse communities. Developing such standardized, data-driven frameworks also requires careful consideration of data availability and comparability across locations. Environmental assessment may require data on land use, transport type and activity, VKT, energy consumption, emissions, infrastructure materials, and life-cycle impacts. Social assessment may require information on population characteristics, accessibility, health outcomes, mobility patterns, and social vulnerability, while economic assessment may require data on infrastructure costs, maintenance expenditures, travel costs, and associated economic benefits. However, the availability, spatial resolution, temporal coverage, and quality of these data can vary substantially across cities and countries. Differences in monitoring capacity, data collection practices, socioeconomic conditions, and access to high-resolution datasets may be particularly important in lower-income and data-constrained contexts. Therefore, standardized frameworks should define common indicators and methodological procedures while allowing flexibility in data sources and levels of resolution. Transparent reporting of data limitations, uncertainty, and assumptions can also be essential to ensure that comparisons across locations are meaningful and that the resulting assessments do not systematically favor areas with greater data availability.
