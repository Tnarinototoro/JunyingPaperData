This study investigates how climatic variability and land-use dynamics drive hydrological change in the Tawa River Basin, a strategically important tributary of the Narmada River in central India, whose hydrological behaviour underpins regional agricultural productivity, ecosystem functioning, and water supply security. The primary objective is to detect, attribute, and quantify shifts in surface runoff generation, streamflow variability, and water balance partitioning driven by interacting climatic and land-use forcing. Long-term hydro-climatic data were analysed using ordinary least squares regression, coefficient of variation, standardized anomaly, Mann–Kendall trend detection, and Sen’s slope estimation. Basin rainfall exhibits moderate inter-annual variability (CV ≈ 19.7%) with a slight increasing trend, implying potential augmentation of surface runoff and flood frequency. Temperature displays a consistent warming trend of 0.012 °C per year (≈0.24 °C per decade), with persistently positive anomalies characterising the post-2009 period signalling increased evapotranspiration demand, reduced base flow, and altered seasonal streamflow regimes. Concurrently, LULC transitions particularly expansion of agricultural and built-up areas at the expense of natural vegetation have reduced infiltration capacity and amplified direct runoff response. An attribution framework disaggregated observed hydrological change into climatically driven and land-use-driven components, demonstrating that both exert significant and distinguishable influences on the basin’s hydrological regime. These findings carry direct implications for reservoir inflow estimation, flood risk assessment, and long-term water resource planning across the Narmada sub-basin system. Environmental Geography Flooding Earth and Environmental Sciences Drought Environmental Sciences Earth System Sciences 1 Introduction Hydrological systems are highly sensitive to both natural climatic variability and anthropogenic land-use changes [ 1 ]. In recent decades, increasing pressure on land resources due to population growth, agricultural expansion, urbanization, and infrastructure development has significantly altered the natural landscape and its associated hydrological processes (Stephens et al., 2020). The combined effects of land-use changes, climate variability, and climate change have significantly altered watershed hydrology, impacting streamflow regimes, groundwater recharge, evapotranspiration rates, and runoff dynamics [ 2 ]. Understanding the combined and individual impacts of hydro-climatic variability and land use/land cover (LULC) change is therefore essential for sustainable water resource management and environmental planning [ 3 ]. Water resources are fundamental for ecological stability, agricultural productivity, and human livelihoods. However, increasing climatic fluctuations and anthropogenic modifications to land surfaces have disrupted natural hydrological cycles across many regions of the world [ 4 ]. Climate variability, primarily through changes in precipitation patterns, temperature, and evapotranspiration, plays a critical role in determining the temporal and spatial distribution of water resources [ 5 ]. Variations in rainfall intensity, seasonal distribution, and temperature can influence runoff generation, soil moisture conditions, and river discharge patterns [ 6 ]. Consequently, these hydro-climatic variations may lead to increased frequency of extreme events such as floods and droughts, posing significant challenges for water resource planning and management (Xiong & Yang, 2024). Alongside climatic influences, land use and land cover change represent one of the most significant human-induced drivers of hydrological transformation within river basins [ 7 ]. Changes in vegetation cover, deforestation, agricultural intensification, urban expansion, and infrastructure development can substantially modify watershed characteristics [ 8 ]. These modifications influence key hydrological components such as infiltration capacity, evapotranspiration rates, soil moisture dynamics, and groundwater recharge. For instance, conversion of forested land into agricultural or built-up areas typically reduces infiltration and increases surface runoff, thereby altering streamflow regimes and sediment transport processes [ 9 ]. Globally, numerous studies have highlighted that both climate variability and LULC change are major controlling factors of watershed hydrology [ 10 ]. Climate variability primarily affects hydrological processes by altering precipitation and temperature regimes, while land-use change modifies the physical properties of the land surface that regulates water movement within the watershed (Palmate et al., 2023). These two drivers often interact in complex ways, making it difficult to separate their individual impacts on hydrological responses [ 11 ]. Therefore, comprehensive studies that simultaneously evaluate hydro-climatic variability and land-use dynamics are crucial for understanding watershed behaviour and predicting future hydrological conditions. In tropical and subtropical regions, rapid land-use transformation combined with climate variability has intensified hydrological alterations [ 12 ]. These regions have experienced accelerated deforestation, agricultural expansion, and urban growth over the past few decades, which have significantly affected river basin hydrology. Studies across different watersheds have demonstrated that increasing impervious surfaces and reduced vegetation cover lead to higher surface runoff, decreased groundwater recharge, and increased flood risks [ 13 ]. At the same time, changing climatic conditions, particularly variations in rainfall patterns, further amplify these hydrological responses. India, with its diverse climatic conditions and rapidly changing land-use patterns, provides a significant context for examining the interaction between climate variability and watershed hydrology [ 14 ]. Many river basins in India have undergone substantial land-use transformations due to agricultural intensification, population growth, and developmental activities [ 15 ]. These transformations have altered hydrological processes and water availability, affecting both ecological sustainability and socio-economic development. In addition, climate variability across different regions of India has resulted in changes in rainfall distribution and temperature patterns, further influencing the hydrological behaviour of river basins [ 16 ]. Consequently, integrated analysis of climate variability and land-use change has become essential for effective watershed management in the Indian context. The Tawa River Basin, located in central India, represents an important sub-basin of the Narmada River system and plays a significant role in regional water resources and agricultural activities [ 17 ]. The basin is characterized by diverse topography, forested landscapes, agricultural lands, and human settlements. Over the past several decades, the basin has experienced considerable land-use changes, particularly due to agricultural expansion, infrastructure development, and population growth [ 18 ]. At the same time, hydro-climatic variability, including fluctuations in rainfall and temperature, has influenced the hydrological processes within the basin [ 19 ]. These changes have important implications for water availability, flood risk, and ecological sustainability in the region [ 20 ]. The hydrology of the Tawa River Basin is closely linked to monsoonal rainfall patterns, which contribute the majority of annual precipitation in the region. Variability in monsoon intensity and distribution can significantly affect streamflow, groundwater recharge, and soil moisture conditions [ 21 ]. In addition, changes in land use, such as deforestation and conversion of forest land into agricultural fields or built-up areas, can modify watershed hydrological responses. Such changes may lead to increased runoff, reduced infiltration, and altered evapotranspiration dynamics, ultimately affecting the overall water balance of the basin [ 22 ]. Understanding these interactions is essential for developing sustainable water management strategies in the region. Advancements in geospatial technologies, including remote sensing and geographic information systems (GIS), have significantly improved the ability to analyse land-use dynamics and hydrological processes at watershed scales [ 23 ]. Remote sensing provides valuable spatial and temporal information on land cover changes, while GIS-based hydrological models enable researchers to assess the impacts of these changes on watershed hydrology. Additionally, statistical approaches and trend analysis techniques help in identifying hydro-climatic variability and long-term changes in climatic variables such as rainfall and temperature [ 24 ]. These integrated approaches provide a comprehensive framework for evaluating the combined effects of climate variability and land-use change on watershed hydrology. Despite the growing body of research on hydrological impacts of climate variability and land-use change, few studies have focused on the combined effects of these drivers in many Indian river basins, including the Tawa River Basin [ 25 ]. Understanding these interactions is particularly important for developing adaptive strategies for water resource management, flood mitigation, and sustainable land-use planning [ 26 ]. Therefore, a comprehensive analysis of hydro-climatic variability and land-use change is essential to evaluate their influence on hydrological processes within the basin. In this context, the present study aims to evaluate hydro-climatic variability and land-use change effects on the hydrology of the Tawa River Basin in central India [ 27 ]. Specifically, the study seeks to analyse long-term climatic trends, assess spatio-temporal land-use dynamics, and investigate their combined impacts on watershed hydrological processes [ 28 ]. The findings of this study will contribute to improved understanding of the interactions between climate variability, land-use change, and hydrological responses, thereby supporting sustainable water resource management and regional environmental planning. 2 Study area description The Tawa River Basin, a major tributary of the Narmada River in central India, covers an area of approximately 6,455.33 km 2 and is located between 21°45′–22°50′ N latitude and 77°40′–78°45′ E longitude (Fig. 1 ). Situated within the northern Satpura hill ranges, the basin exhibits considerable topographic variation, with elevations ranging from approximately 321 m to 1304 m above mean sea level. The upper catchment is characterized by steep and forested hill slopes, whereas the downstream region consists predominantly of gently sloping valley plains. This physiographic variability strongly influences hydrological processes within the basin, as steeper slopes promote rapid surface runoff and soil erosion, while flatter terrains facilitate infiltration, groundwater recharge, and water retention. The drainage network exhibits a predominantly dendritic to sub-dendritic pattern and is controlled by the underlying geological and geomorphological conditions. The Tawa River and its tributaries form an important hydrological system that contributes significantly to the water resources of the Narmada Basin. The basin experiences a tropical monsoon climate with three distinct seasons: summer (March–June), monsoon (June–September), and winter (October–February). Annual rainfall ranges between approximately 1,000 and 1,500 mm, of which nearly 80–90% is received during the southwest monsoon season. Mean annual temperatures vary from about 20 °C to 32 °C, with maximum temperatures often exceeding 40 °C during summer. The spatial and temporal variability of rainfall plays a crucial role in determining streamflow generation, groundwater recharge, and watershed hydrological responses. Land-use and land-cover (LULC) within the basin are dominated by forests, agricultural land, settlements, water bodies, and barren land. Forests occupy large portions of the upper and middle catchments, while agricultural activities are concentrated in the valley plains and lower reaches. These land-use patterns significantly affect evapotranspiration, infiltration, runoff generation, and sediment transport processes. The basin is characterized by diverse soil types, including black cotton soils (Vertisols), red and yellow soils, and shallow skeletal soils in the hilly regions. Black soils possess high water-holding capacity and support intensive agriculture, whereas shallow upland soils promote rapid runoff and limited infiltration. The combined influence of topography, climate, drainage characteristics, soil properties, and land-use patterns makes the Tawa River Basin a hydrologically significant watershed and an ideal region for investigating the impacts of land-use/land-cover changes on hydrological processes and water resource management. Fig. 1 Full size image Study area location map 3 Data and methods 3.1 Data used This study utilized meteorological, remote sensing, and topographic datasets to assess the impacts of land-use/land-cover (LULC) change on the hydrological processes of the Tawa River Basin. Daily meteorological data, including precipitation, maximum and minimum temperature, were obtained from the India Meteorological Department (IMD) and other available regional sources for the study period. A Digital Elevation Model (DEM) with a spatial resolution of 30 m was acquired from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) and used for watershed delineation, stream network extraction, slope generation, and other terrain analyses (Fig. 2 ). Multi-temporal Landsat satellite imagery was used for LULC mapping, including Landsat 5 TM, Landsat 7 ETM+, and Landsat 8 OLI/TIRS data with a spatial resolution of 30 m. Images corresponding to the years selected for LULC analysis were obtained from the United States Geological Survey (USGS) Earth Explorer platform. All spatial datasets were projected to the Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) coordinate system (WGS 84 datum) and clipped to the basin boundary prior to analysis. Pre-processing of satellite imagery included radiometric correction, atmospheric correction, geometric verification, cloud masking, image mosaicking, and sub setting to the study area. For Landsat 7 ETM+ imagery acquired, data gaps and striping effects were corrected using a gap-filling approach based on neighbouring pixel interpolation and multi-temporal image compositing from temporally adjacent scenes. Areas affected by residual gaps were visually inspected and supplemented with information from auxiliary imagery where necessary. These procedures minimized classification uncertainties and ensured the reliability of the generated LULC maps. The processed images were subsequently classified into major LULC categories, including forest, agriculture, water bodies, settlements, and barren land, and were used as inputs for hydrological modelling and change analysis Tables 1 , 2 . In such environments, increases in temperature can significantly enhance evaporation and evapotranspiration, which in turn influence river discharge, soil moisture dynamics, and overall water availability within the basin. Therefore, understanding the variability and trends in climatic parameters is essential for assessing hydrological responses and watershed behaviour. For the present study, meteorological datasets were obtained from the India Meteorological Department (IMD). The analysis utilized gridded rainfall and temperature data, including minimum and maximum temperature data, covering the period from 1999 to 2019. The rainfall data were available at a spatial resolution of 0.25° × 0.25°, while the temperature data were obtained at a resolution of 1° × 1°. These datasets provide consistent and spatially distributed climatic information suitable for basin-scale hydro-climatic analysis. The IMD gridded datasets were processed and extracted for the Tawa River Basin to evaluate temporal variability and trends in rainfall and temperature. These hydro-climatic variables were subsequently used to assess climate variability, detect trends, and examine their implications for hydrological processes within the basin. The study period was deliberately constrained to 1999–2019 to ensure consistency and comparability across all hydro-climatic and land use/land cover (LULC) datasets employed in the analysis. The selection of this temporal window was governed by the availability of continuous, high-quality, and spatially consistent remote sensing imagery, streamflow records, and meteorological data across the study watershed. Extending the analysis to 2025 would have introduced significant data heterogeneity, as post-2019 satellite products, hydrological monitoring records, and reanalysis climate datasets are subject to ongoing revisions, incomplete archiving, or sensor discontinuities that could compromise the integrity of long-term trend assessments. Furthermore, the 20-year period from 1999 to 2019 captures multiple decadal-scale hydro-climatic cycles, including notable wet and dry phases, as well as substantial anthropogenic LULC transitions, thereby providing a sufficiently robust temporal baseline for detecting meaningful change signals. It is acknowledged that hydro-climatic variability and land use dynamics in the post-2019 period, including potential intensification of extreme events and accelerated land cover change, may modify some of the observed trends and relationships. Incorporating such recent data constitutes a meaningful direction for future research and will be explicitly noted in the limitations section of the manuscript. 3.3 LULC classification accuracy assessment The accuracy of the LULC classification was rigorously evaluated using a standard error matrix (confusion matrix) approach, following well-established protocols in remote sensing-based land cover assessment. A stratified random sampling strategy was adopted to generate a statistically representative set of reference points distributed across all identified land cover classes, thereby minimizing sampling bias and ensuring proportional class representation. Ground truth data for validation were derived from a combination of high-resolution Google Earth imagery, ancillary topographic maps, and field verification records corresponding to each classification epoch. For each classified image, overall accuracy (OA), producer’s accuracy (PA), user’s accuracy (UA), and the Kappa coefficient (κ) were computed as the primary accuracy metrics. The overall classification accuracy across all time periods exceeded 89% with Kappa coefficient values ranging from 0.85 indicating substantial to almost perfect agreement between the classified maps and the reference data, as per the interpretation thresholds established by Landis and Koch (1977). Class-level accuracies were also examined to identify any systematic misclassification between spectrally similar categories, such as sparse vegetation and degraded agricultural land, and appropriate post-classification refinements were applied where necessary to enhance thematic reliability prior to change detection analysis. 3.4 Land Use/land cover classification and change analysis Land use and land cover (LULC) mapping of the study area was carried out using an unsupervised classification technique applied to multi-temporal Landsat satellite imagery (Landsat 5, Landsat 7, and Landsat 8) for the pre-monsoon seasons of 1999, 2009, and 2019. The pre-monsoon images were selected to minimize cloud interference and improve the spectral distinction between different land surface features. Image pre-processing, including geometric correction, layer stacking, and sub-setting of the study area, was performed using ERDAS Imagine and ArcGIS software to prepare the satellite data for classification [ 29 ]. The unsupervised classification algorithm was implemented to automatically group pixels into spectrally similar clusters without prior training samples [ 30 ]. These clusters were then interpreted and labelled into meaningful land-use categories based on visual interpretation (Fig. 3 ). The study area was classified into seven major LULC classes, namely forest, agricultural land, water bodies, urban and rangeland [ 30 ] (Fig. 4 ). Following the classification process, spatial analysis and map preparation were carried out in ArcGIS, which facilitated the generation of LULC maps for each study year. The resulting classified maps were then used to analyse temporal changes in land-use patterns within the Tawa River Basin [ 31 ]. To further examine land-use transitions and spatial dynamics, the Land Change Modeler (LCM) module available in IDRISI Selva was used to assess the magnitude and direction of LULC changes over time. Additional methodological details related to the LULC characteristics of the study area are available [ 32 ]. The rainfall CV of approximately 19.7% indicates moderate inter-annual variability in precipitation patterns. In climatological terms, a CV value ranging between 15 and 30% reflects noticeable year-to-year fluctuations in rainfall. Such variability suggests that the basin experiences considerable differences in annual precipitation, which can significantly influence hydrological processes such as runoff generation, soil moisture availability, and groundwater recharge. Consequently, this level of variability highlights the sensitivity of the basin’s hydrological system to rainfall extremes, including drought and high-rainfall events. In contrast, the temperature CV of approximately 0.74% indicates very low variability, suggesting that temperature remains relatively stable from year to year compared to rainfall. This low variability implies that temperature changes occur in a gradual and systematic manner rather than through abrupt fluctuations. Such behaviour is typical of long-term climatic warming patterns and supports the positive warming trend identified in the earlier trend analysis. The relatively stable but steadily increasing temperature pattern may influence evapotranspiration rates, vegetation dynamics, and overall water balance within the basin over time. These findings confirm that while rainfall variability governs short-term hydrological extremes, rising temperature represents a systematic climatic forcing that may enhance evapotranspiration and alter basin hydrodynamics 4.7 Quantification of climate and land use attribution To evaluate the relative influence of climatic variability and land use/land cover (LULC) change on hydrological responses, a quantitative attribution framework was applied (Table 5 ). This approach separates the individual and combined contributions of climate and land-use drivers by comparing results from different simulation scenarios. The contribution of each driver was calculated using a set of comparative differences among the scenarios. The climate effect was estimated as S₂ − S₁, representing the hydrological change caused solely by variations in climatic conditions while keeping land use constant. Similarly, the LULC effect was calculated as S₃ − S₁, which represents the hydrological changes attributed exclusively to land use and land cover modifications while maintaining constant climatic conditions. In addition to these independent effects, the interaction effect between climate and LULC was determined using the expression S₄ − (S₂ + S₃ − S₁). This term captures the synergistic or combined influence resulting from simultaneous changes in both climate and land use, which may amplify or dampen the individual effects of each driver. The attribution of hydrological changes to climate variability and land use/land cover (LULC) dynamics was conducted using a factorial simulation framework. Four independent model scenarios were implemented to isolate the individual and combined impacts of rainfall–temperature variability and LULC transitions (Table 6 ). The baseline scenario (S1) used constant climate (mean rainfall ≈4.39 mm and temperature ≈25.77 °C for 1999–2019) and baseline LULC conditions. Scenario S2 incorporated observed inter-annual climate variability (rainfall range: 2.97–6.22 mm; temperature range: 25.53–26.20 °C) while keeping LULC constant to quantify climatic influence. Scenario S3 used time-varying LULC maps with constant baseline climate to isolate anthropogenic land transformation effects. Scenario S4 integrated both varying climate and LULC to simulate real-world conditions. The relative contributions were computed by differencing simulation outputs, allowing separation of climate-driven, land-use-driven, and interaction effects. This factorial design ensures robust attribution and avoids conflating hydro-climatic variability with land-use induced hydrological responses. Fig. 7 Full size image Variation in average annual temperature over basin during baseline period 4.8.2 Mann-kendall trend test The Tawa River Basin, an important watershed within the Narmada River system of central India, has been experiencing noticeable hydrological changes influenced by both hydro-climatic variability and land use/land cover change (LULCC). These changes have significant implications for regional water resources, agricultural productivity, and flood management. The results of the Mann–Kendall trend analysis (trend = increasing, p = 0.001, Tau = 0.35, slope = 0.02 mm year −1 ) indicate a statistically significant and moderately strong upward trend in precipitation within the basin. This trend suggests gradually wetter conditions, which may enhance water availability for irrigation and support reservoir storage, particularly in the Tawa Dam system. However, the magnitude of the increase remains relatively modest, corresponding to an estimated rise of approximately 2 mm over a century. Despite the positive precipitation trend, its interaction with ongoing LULC changes has important hydrological consequences. Rapid agricultural expansion, increasing urban development, and localized deforestation have altered the natural hydrological response of the basin. Agricultural intensification often leads to soil compaction and higher irrigation demands, which can increase surface runoff and reduce infiltration capacity. When combined with rising precipitation levels, these factors may accelerate soil erosion and increase sediment transport within the Tawa River system, potentially affecting reservoir storage capacity and water quality. Urbanization within the basin further contributes to hydrological modifications by introducing impervious surfaces such as roads, buildings, and infrastructure. These surfaces reduce natural infiltration and increase the rate and volume of surface runoff, leading to flashier streamflow conditions and a higher risk of flooding during intense monsoon rainfall events. Additionally, deforestation and the degradation of wetlands reduce the natural capacity of the landscape to regulate floods, filter pollutants, and maintain ecological balance. Climate predictors derived from the NCEP dataset (e.g., ncepprgl.dat, R 2 = 0.07) highlight the strong influence of monsoon-driven precipitation variability in the basin. This variability emphasizes the need for adaptive watershed management strategies that consider both climatic and anthropogenic drivers of hydrological change. While the increasing precipitation trend may support agricultural production and water availability, it simultaneously increases the potential for flood hazards, sedimentation, and water quality deterioration caused by enhanced runoff and pollutant transport. Overall, the findings indicate that the hydrological behaviour of the Tawa River Basin is shaped by the combined effects of climatic variability and land-use transformations. Future research should focus on quantifying the rate and spatial distribution of LULC changes, as well as examining long-term temperature trends and extreme rainfall events. Such analyses will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the basin’s evolving hydrological dynamics and support the development of effective water resource management and climate adaptation strategies. 4.8.3 Seasonal variability analysis Figure 8 shows seasonal variability and the changes in weather conditions that occur with the changing seasons. The detected changes in runoff generation can be mechanistically attributed to alterations in surface permeability, infiltration capacity, and evapotranspiration fluxes driven by LULC transitions over the study period. Expansion of impervious surfaces and agricultural land, coupled with a reduction in natural vegetation cover, fundamentally modified the watershed’s hydrological response by reducing interception losses and soil moisture retention capacity, thereby accelerating overland flow generation and reducing baseflow contributions [ 33 , 34 ]. These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in analogous hydro-climatic settings, where deforestation and agricultural encroachment have been reported to increase surface runoff coefficients by 20–40% and significantly reduce lag times between rainfall events and peak discharge [ 35 , 36 ]. The reduction in vegetated land cover further diminishes root-zone water uptake and canopy interception, weakening the watershed’s capacity to buffer against extreme hydrological events and amplifying flood peaks during high-precipitation periods. Conversely, areas retaining dense vegetation or transitioning toward conservation-oriented land uses exhibited comparatively attenuated runoff responses, underscoring the critical role of land surface conditions in modulating hydrological partitioning. These results have significant implications for water resource management, as continued LULC change without appropriate watershed governance is likely to exacerbate runoff extremes, increase sediment yields, and compromise the long-term hydrological resilience of the basin a trajectory increasingly documented across South and Southeast Asian river systems experiencing rapid land use transitions [ 37 , 38 ]. 4.10 Discharge and precipitation analysis of the Tawa River Basin 4.10.1 Annual discharge and rainfall trends Figure 13 presents the average annual river discharge (m 3 /s) and corresponding annual rainfall (mm) at the outlet (near narmadapuram) of the Tawa River Basin over the study period 1999–2019. The Tawa River exhibits a strongly monsoon-driven flow regime, with over 90% of annual discharge occurring during the southwest monsoon season (June–September), driven by intense rainfall in the narmadapuram district. Mean annual discharge over the study period was 46.39 m 3 /s (SD = 31.30 m 3 /s), representing a marked decline from the historical baseline mean of approximately 124 m 3 /s recorded during 1975–1999, reflecting the altered hydrological conditions of the basin. The maximum annual discharge of 123.89 m 3 /s occurred in 2013, coinciding with the peak annual rainfall of 1,797.2 mm, while the minimum discharge of 11.20 m 3 /s was recorded in 2000, the driest year on record (768.7 mm). This strong co-variability is confirmed by a Pearson correlation coefficient of r = 0.910 ( p < 0.0001), with an OLS regression explaining 82.8% of inter-annual discharge variance (R 2 = 0.82), underscoring rainfall as the dominant control on streamflow generation. The Mann–Kendall trend test applied to the annual discharge series yields Z = −0.030 ( p = 0.976), indicating no statistically significant monotonic trend in streamflow over the study period. Similarly, annual rainfall shows a marginal declining tendency (Sen’s slope = −5.77 mm/yr MK Z = −0.393, p = 0.695), which is also statistically non-significant. The coefficient of variation for discharge (CV = 67.5%) is substantially higher than for rainfall (CV = 24.9%), indicating that the rainfall–runoff transformation amplifies inter-annual variability a characteristic indicative of a highly responsive, flashy catchment with reduced infiltration capacity driven by land-use modification. The findings reveal that rainfall variability, increasing temperature, and landscape transformation are important factors associated with changes in basin hydrology [ 39 ]. Annual rainfall exhibited moderate interannual variability (CV = 19.7%) but no statistically significant long-term trend (Mann–Kendall Z = −0.393, p = 0.695; Sen’s slope = −5.77 mm/year) [ 40 ]. Nevertheless, rainfall ranged from 607.6 mm in 2018 to 1,797.2 mm in 2013, demonstrating considerable variability in annual water input. This suggests that the absence of a significant rainfall trend does not necessarily indicate stable hydrological conditions, particularly in monsoon-dependent catchments where individual wet and dry years can strongly influence water availability and flood risk [ 41 ]. Streamflow showed a strong positive relationship with rainfall (Pearson r = 0.910, p < 0.001; R 2 = 0.828), confirming the importance of precipitation in controlling annual discharge. However, the discharge coefficient of variation (67.5%) was substantially higher than the rainfall CV, indicating that streamflow fluctuated more strongly than annual rainfall [ 42 ]. Discharge ranged from 11.20 m 3 /s in 2000 to 123.89 m 3 /s in 2013. This variability may reflect the combined effects of rainfall intensity, antecedent soil moisture, infiltration, catchment storage, and drainage characteristics. Similar rainfall-driven hydrological responses have been reported in monsoon-dominated catchments, including the Narmada basin, where precipitation variability and catchment characteristics influence streamflow patterns. However, annual data cannot fully represent event-scale runoff processes, and reservoir operations and water withdrawals may also affect observed discharge [ 43 ]. 5.2 Temperature trends and regional climate change Temperature increased at approximately 0.012 °C/year, equivalent to about 0.24 °C per decade, with predominantly positive anomalies after 2009. This pattern is broadly consistent with regional evidence of warming across parts of central India and the Narmada basin. Increasing temperature can raise atmospheric evaporative demand and potentially affect soil moisture, vegetation water use, and seasonal water availability [ 44 ]. These effects may become particularly important during dry periods, when reduced soil-water availability can intensify agricultural and ecological stress. The results also align with broader assessments of climate change that identify rising temperatures as an important factor influencing hydrological processes. However, the consequences of warming depend on precipitation, humidity, vegetation, soil properties, and water availability (Stephens et al., 2020). Since actual evapotranspiration and dry-season baseflow were not directly quantified in this study, their potential responses should be investigated using additional observations or calibrated hydrological modelling. Furthermore, the 21-year study period is relatively short for establishing long-term climatic trends, particularly in a region characterised by substantial monsoon variability. Longer records and seasonal analyses would help determine whether the observed temperature and rainfall patterns persist over longer timescales [ 45 ]. 5.3 LULC change and hydrological non-stationarity LULC analysis identified a decline in forest cover of approximately 597 km 2 , accompanied by agricultural expansion of 637 km 2 and urban expansion of 56 km 2 . These changes indicate substantial landscape transformation during the study period [ 46 ]. Forest removal and expansion of cultivated or built-up land can alter interception, surface roughness, soil compaction, infiltration, and runoff generation. The direction and magnitude of these effects depend on soil properties, slope, vegetation condition, and land-management practices (Rojas et al., 2016). The composite Soil Conservation Service curve number increased from 68.2 in 1999 to 72.8 in 2019, indicating greater potential for direct runoff under the assumptions of the curve-number method. This change is consistent with a reduction in the catchment’s capacity to retain rainfall before runoff generation. Similar relationships between LULC transitions and runoff potential have been investigated in hydrological modelling studies of the Narmada basin, including research using integrated climate and land-use scenarios in the Upper Narmada Basin [ 28 ]. Nevertheless, curve number is an empirical indicator rather than a direct measurement of infiltration capacity. Its interpretation depends on the accuracy of LULC classification, hydrological soil-group assignment, and antecedent moisture conditions. The double mass curve analysis revealed that the cumulative rainfall–runoff slope increased from 0.0353 during 1999–2009 to 0.0531 during 2010–2019, representing an increase of approximately 50.5% [ 47 ]. This shift suggests a change in the relationship between rainfall and discharge around 2010. Its timing broadly coincides with the period of substantial landscape transformation, supporting the hypothesis that LULC change may have contributed to altered catchment responses. However, the slope change alone cannot establish causality. Changes in rainfall intensity, reservoir operations, groundwater interactions, and water withdrawals may also influence this relationship [ 48 ]. Therefore, the results indicate hydrological non-stationarity but do not independently quantify the contribution of each driver. 5.4 Regional significance and methodological limitations The combined findings demonstrate that hydrological change can occur even when annual rainfall shows no statistically significant trend. Warming, land-cover transitions, and changes in rainfall–runoff relationships may modify water availability and runoff generation independently of changes in annual precipitation totals [ 49 ]. This interpretation is consistent with broader hydrological research showing that catchment responses depend on interactions between climatic forcing, land cover, soil characteristics, and human water management. Differences between this study and other regional investigations may arise from variations in observation periods, spatial resolution, climate datasets, classification methods, and hydrological modelling approaches [ 50 ]. Studies using calibrated hydrological models can compare climate-only, LULC-only, and combined scenarios, whereas statistical analyses primarily identify observed relationships and changes. The present study provides evidence of changing hydrological behaviour, but stronger attribution requires independent scenario simulations and uncertainty assessment [ 51 ]. The annual temporal resolution also limits the ability to evaluate short-duration rainfall extremes and peak flood responses. 5.5 Limitations and implications for sustainable land-use management Several limitations should be acknowledged. The 1999–2019 period may not capture longer-term climate variability, while LULC maps for three years may overlook intermediate transitions. Uncertainty in land-cover classification and curve-number estimation may affect runoff-potential estimates. Furthermore, the observed rainfall–discharge relationship and double mass curve shift cannot isolate the effects of land-use change from reservoir operations and other human influences. Without calibrated hydrological simulations, the separate contributions of climate and LULC change remain uncertain [ 52 ]. Future research should incorporate longer hydro-meteorological records, higher-frequency rainfall and discharge observations, field-validated LULC maps, and detailed reservoir-operation data. Calibrated hydrological modelling under climate-only, LULC-only, and combined scenarios would improve attribution [ 53 ]. Multi-model CMIP6 projections could also be integrated with alternative land-use scenarios to assess future flood risk, seasonal water availability, and low-flow conditions (Kay et al., 2020). From a management perspective, the findings support forest conservation, restoration of riparian vegetation, soil-conservation practices, and improved regulation of agricultural and urban expansion. Urban development should incorporate storm water detention, rainwater harvesting, and permeable surfaces, while agricultural planning should promote practices that maintain soil structure and infiltration [ 54 ]. These measures should be targeted to local soil, slope, drainage, and land-cover conditions. Overall, integrating hydro-climatic monitoring with spatial land-use planning can strengthen climate-resilient watershed management and support sustainable water-resource use in the Tawa River Basin and other monsoon-dependent catchments of central India. 6 Conclusion This study assessed the combined influence of hydro-climatic variability and land use/land cover (LULC) change on the hydrological regime of the Tawa River Basin during 1999–2019 using climatic trend and anomaly analysis, streamflow assessment, double mass curve analysis, LULC change detection, and a hydrological attribution framework. Rainfall showed substantial interannual variability (CV = 19.7%) but no statistically significant long-term trend, whereas temperature exhibited a warming trend of approximately 0.012 °C/year. Streamflow was strongly correlated with rainfall ( r = 0.910, p < 0.001), while double mass curve analysis indicated a 50.5% increase in the cumulative rainfall–runoff slope after approximately 2010. Concurrently, forest cover declined by 597 km 2 , accompanied by expansion of agricultural and urban areas, and the composite SCS curve number increased from 68.2 to 72.8, indicating greater potential for runoff generation. These findings suggest that hydro-climatic variability and LULC transformation jointly influence basin hydrology, although their individual contributions require further validation through calibrated hydrological modelling. Integrated watershed management, forest conservation, and improved land-use planning are therefore recommended to reduce runoff-related risks and support sustainable water-resource management. Future studies should incorporate calibrated hydrological models, detailed reservoir-operation data, and CMIP6 climate projections to strengthen attribution and assess future hydrological changes.
