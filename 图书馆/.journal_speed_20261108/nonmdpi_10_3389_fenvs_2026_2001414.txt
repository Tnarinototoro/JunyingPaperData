School of Economics and Management, Nanjing Forestry University, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China 2. School of Tourism, Taishan University, Tai’an, Shandong, China Article metrics View details Abstract Introduction: Marine development programs often pursue resource conservation and production objectives simultaneously, yet their broader implications for sustainable coastal development remain insufficiently understood. This study examines whether China’s National Marine Ranching Demonstration Zone (NMRDZ) program improves inclusive green growth (IGG) in coastal cities. Methods: IGG is measured using an entropy-weighted composite index covering economic growth, social inclusion, and environmental sustainability. Treating staggered NMRDZ designation as a quasi-natural experiment, this study applies a staggered difference-in-differences (DID) model to panel data for 37 Chinese coastal cities from 2011 to 2024. The analysis examines the program’s overall effect, transmission mechanisms, heterogeneity, and moderating conditions. Results: The results show that: (1) the program increases IGG by approximately 0.174 standard deviations, and the finding remains robust; (2) it increases mariculture output per unit area, generating a positive transmission effect, but reduces marine capture production, creating an offsetting effect; (3) it significantly promotes IGG both within and outside the Northern Marine Economic Circle and across initial and subsequent designation batches, while its gains are statistically evident in larger-population cities but not in smaller-population cities; and (4) the estimated program effect is larger in cities with higher levels of green technological innovation or a more advanced industrial structure. Discussion: These findings reveal a dual production dynamic in marine ranching development: higher mariculture output per unit area reinforces IGG, whereas contraction in marine capture production partially offsets the overall improvement. By evaluating marine ranching through an integrated economic, social, and environmental framework, this study moves beyond separate ecological or economic outcomes and provides evidence for refining the NMRDZ program, guiding fisheries production restructuring, and advancing inclusive and sustainable development in coastal cities. 1 Introduction Marine ecosystems and living resources make important contributions to aquatic food supply, fisheries production, and the economic development of coastal regions (Bene et al., 2016). However, overexploitation of fishery resources, degradation of marine habitats, environmental pollution, and increasing competition for marine space have placed growing constraints on conventional fisheries development. In China, relatively stable aggregate marine catch has coexisted with deteriorating resource conditions. A historical assessment summarized by Cao et al. (2017) classified approximately 57% of the assessed fish stocks in Chinese seas as overexploited or collapsed. This illustrates the tension between maintaining fishery output and conserving the resource base on which production depends. Measures to reduce fishing pressure can also require fishing communities to adjust their employment and income sources. For example, a survey in Zhoushan, Zhejiang Province, found that fishers who had changed occupations under vessel buyback and transfer programs faced livelihood vulnerability associated with limited adaptive capacity (Chen et al., 2020). Fisheries transformation therefore involves balancing the economic contribution of production, the protection of marine resources, and support for affected livelihoods. Evaluating marine development policies requires attention to these interconnected economic, social, and environmental outcomes (Elegbede et al., 2025; McKinley et al., 2019). Inclusive green growth (IGG) provides a useful framework for evaluating these combined outcomes. It emphasizes the coordination of economic growth, social inclusion, and environmental sustainability under resource and environmental constraints (Kamguia et al., 2025). Existing research on marine ranching and related marine development initiatives has examined ecological efficiency, fishery production, enterprise performance, and broader development outcomes (Kitada, 2018; Zhong et al., 2024). Nevertheless, these outcomes are often evaluated separately. Consequently, it remains unclear whether a marine development program that combines resource-conservation objectives with changes in fishery production can improve a city-level indicator encompassing economic, social, and environmental dimensions. Moreover, changes in different types of fishery production may not contribute to the overall outcome in the same direction. An increase in mariculture output per unit area and a decline in marine capture production, for example, represent different adjustments in coastal fishery production and may have different relationships with city-level IGG. China’s National Marine Ranching Demonstration Zone (NMRDZ) program provides an appropriate setting in which to examine this question. Since 2015, NMRDZs have been designated in successive batches across China’s coastal regions. The program includes policy instruments and construction activities related to artificial reefs, stock enhancement, habitat management, mariculture facilities, monitoring equipment, and the development of supporting fishery activities (Yu and Zhang, 2020). It is intended to combine marine resource conservation, fisheries production restructuring, and coastal economic development. However, the existence of these multiple policy objectives does not in itself establish that NMRDZ designation improves the broader development performance of host cities. Its effects need to be evaluated using an outcome measure that extends beyond individual project performance or a single ecological or economic indicator. The sample covers 37 Chinese coastal cities annually from 2011 to 2024. Because NMRDZs entered the program in different years, a staggered DID specification is used to estimate the effect on city-level IGG. The IGG index is constructed from indicators covering economic growth, social inclusion, and environmental sustainability. The analysis first estimates the overall effect of NMRDZ designation and then examines two production-side transmission channels: mariculture output per unit area and marine capture production. It further investigates whether the estimated effect varies across marine economic circles, city population groups, and designation batches. Finally, green patent applications and industrial structure advancement are used to examine whether local green innovation activity and industrial structure moderate the estimated policy effect. The study advances the literature in three respects. First, it extends research on marine ranching from project-level performance and individual ecological or economic outcomes to a city-level IGG framework. This makes it possible to evaluate NMRDZ designation using a composite indicator that incorporates economic, social, and environmental considerations. Second, it distinguishes between changes in mariculture output per unit area and marine capture production and examines their different transmission relationships with IGG. This distinction helps identify how changes in separate components of fishery production are associated with the overall policy effect. Third, the study examines variation in the estimated effect across cities and evaluates the moderating roles of green innovation activity and industrial structure. Although the institutional design of the NMRDZ program is specific to China, the analysis provides evidence relevant to the evaluation of place-based marine development programs that pursue resource-conservation and production-development objectives simultaneously. 2 Literature review The IGG perspective brings the economic, social, and environmental dimensions of development into a single evaluative framework. One strand of research focuses on its measurement and spatial evolution. Composite indices have been constructed to examine regional disparities, convergence, spatial dependence, and structural drivers of IGG across countries, regions, and cities (Chen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). A second strand investigates factors associated with IGG, including digital intelligence, technological change, and industrial restructuring (Chen et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). A third strand evaluates the effects of place-based policies on urban IGG. Studies have examined horizontal ecological compensation, green finance reform, climate-resilient city construction, and national big data pilot zones using quasi-experimental methods (Wang H. et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Zhang Y. et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026). These studies demonstrate the usefulness of city-level IGG indicators for evaluating policies with economic, social, and environmental implications. Related city-level policy research also examines specific environmental outcomes. Deng and Guo (2026), for example, find that low-carbon city pilots improve air quality through enhanced green economic efficiency and industrial structure upgrading. Far less is known about the IGG implications of marine development programs that jointly involve resource governance and fisheries production restructuring. Research on marine ranching initially concentrated on its construction, production functions, and ecological performance. Marine ranching commonly involves artificial reefs, stock enhancement, habitat management, and supporting production facilities, although its construction and management models vary across regions and projects (Zhou et al., 2019). Subsequent studies have examined how government support, enterprise participation, project management, technological conditions, and stakeholder involvement affect marine ranching development and project performance (Liu et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2021). A substantial body of research has also evaluated ecological efficiency at the project or regional level (Du et al., 2021; Qin and Sun, 2021; Zhang et al., 2021). Other studies have investigated biological community structures within individual marine ranches, the effects of demonstration-zone designation on regional ecological efficiency, and its relationship with energy-saving and emission-reduction potential in coastal cities (Huang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2022; Qin and Nie, 2024). Related research has also applied an integrated economic-environmental sustainability index to an offshore aquaculture platform in the South China Sea (Chen S. et al., 2024). This literature provides important evidence concerning the ecological and management dimensions of marine ranching, but most studies focus on individual projects, particular ecological outcomes, or specialized measures of environmental performance. A growing body of research has extended the evaluation of marine ranching to economic, social, and broader regional outcomes. Case studies of marine ranching enterprises have documented economic, environmental, and social dimensions of development practices (Wang et al., 2023). City-level policy evaluations suggest that marine ranching demonstration zones are associated with fishery economic development and the high-quality development of coastal cities (Qin and Guo, 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Research has also examined regional differences in the economic, ecological, and social benefits of national marine ranches, as well as fishers’ organizational participation and perceived benefit distribution in marine ranching governance (Yu and Hou, 2026; Zhong et al., 2024). These studies show that the consequences of marine ranching extend beyond project construction and ecological performance. At the city level, however, existing evaluations primarily focus on aggregate outcomes such as fishery economic development or high-quality urban development. They provide limited evidence on whether NMRDZ designation is associated with different changes in mariculture and marine capture production. Moreover, although previous studies recognize regional differences in marine ranching outcomes and the relevance of technological and development conditions, their roles in shaping the effect of NMRDZ designation on city-level IGG remain insufficiently examined. Taken together, the IGG literature provides a multidimensional framework for evaluating urban development, but its application to marine-ranching policy remains insufficiently developed. Existing city-level evaluations of marine ranching have primarily examined fisheries economic performance or aggregate urban development outcomes, providing limited evidence on how mariculture and marine capture production separately transmit policy effects to IGG. Distinguishing these two production channels is important because changes in different fisheries activities may contribute differently to the overall development outcome. This study therefore connects the IGG framework with marine-ranching policy evaluation by examining the overall effect of NMRDZ designation and separately assessing the transmission relationships associated with mariculture output per unit area and marine capture production. It further investigates whether the estimated policy effect varies across city groups and with local innovation and industrial conditions. 3 Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses 3.1 The NMRDZ program and inclusive green growth The NMRDZ program combines marine resource management with fishery production and related development activities. From a city-level perspective, its potential effect on IGG can be understood in relation to the economic, social, and environmental dimensions incorporated in the composite index. The program does not target these dimensions through a single instrument. Instead, NMRDZ designation is accompanied by a combination of artificial reef construction, stock enhancement, habitat management, production facilities, monitoring equipment, and supporting fishery activities (Qin et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2019). These instruments provide several possible channels through which the program may affect the broader development performance of coastal cities. Economically, the construction of production and monitoring facilities may improve the conditions under which marine fishery activities are organized. The combination of marine space, production facilities, technology, and other inputs may support more specialized and facility-based production and contribute to fishery output (Wang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2019). Marine ranching may also be connected with seafood processing, cold-chain logistics, recreational fisheries, and marine tourism. These related activities can extend the ways in which marine resources contribute to local economic activity (Chong et al., 2024). Where such activities broaden income-generating opportunities in coastal areas, they may also contribute to the social inclusion dimension of IGG. However, the extent to which these potential benefits are realized may vary across cities and does not necessarily imply that all groups benefit equally. Environmentally, artificial reefs, stock enhancement, and habitat management are intended to support fishery resource conservation and improve the management of marine habitats (Kitada, 2018). NMRDZ development may also encourage coordination among local governments, enterprises, research institutions, and fishery operators in the use and management of marine resources (Wan et al., 2023). These activities may contribute to the environmental dimension of city-level IGG, although their effects on broader coastal environmental conditions depend on policy implementation and local circumstances. Taken together, the combination of production-related, development-related, and environmental objectives provides a theoretical basis for expecting the NMRDZ program to improve the composite IGG performance of coastal cities, even if the individual dimensions do not necessarily respond to the same extent. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H1The NMRDZ program has a positive effect on IGG in coastal cities. 3.2 Transmission channels through changes in fisheries production 3.2.1 Mariculture output per unit area as a positive transmission channel Mariculture contributes to aquatic food supply and the fishery economies of coastal regions (Greenfeld et al., 2024). However, its expansion is constrained by the availability of suitable marine space and by competition with ecosystem conservation, shipping, ports, and other uses of coastal waters (Strand et al., 2026). Under these spatial constraints, increasing output from a given farming area provides an important means of supporting mariculture production without relying entirely on the continued expansion of farming space (Del Valle et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023). The NMRDZ program may increase mariculture output per unit area by improving production facilities, production organization, and access to relevant technologies. Marine ranching development can be accompanied by upgraded facilities, monitoring equipment, improved seed varieties, precision feeding, and disease-prevention technologies (Chen Y. et al., 2024; Henriksson et al., 2021). The program may also facilitate the combination of marine space with capital, technology, labor, and managerial inputs, thereby improving the organization of mariculture production (Yu and Zhang, 2020). Higher mariculture output per unit area can contribute positively to city-level IGG. Producing more output from a given farming area can support gross fishery output and the economic performance of coastal cities. A larger and more stable supply of mariculture products may also support seafood processing, distribution, and market activities, thereby extending the economic contribution of mariculture beyond the production stage (Ababouch et al., 2023). The NMRDZ program may therefore promote IGG by increasing mariculture output per unit area. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H2The NMRDZ program increases mariculture output per unit area, and mariculture output per unit area is positively related to IGG in coastal cities. 3.2.2 Marine capture production as an offsetting transmission channel Marine capture fisheries depend on renewable biological resources whose capacity for replenishment is affected by fish stocks, ecosystem conditions, and fishing pressure (Hilborn et al., 2020; Worm et al., 2009). When fishing pressure exceeds the regenerative capacity of fishery resources, continued capture production can place additional pressure on nearshore ecosystems (Cao et al., 2017). Marine resource management therefore commonly involves adjustments to the scale and organization of conventional capture activities. The NMRDZ program incorporates fishery resource-conservation objectives and operates within a policy context involving fishing restrictions, fishing-vessel management, stock enhancement, and adjustments in fishery production (Ministry of Agriculture of the People’s Republic of China, 2017b; Yu and Zhang, 2020). The construction and management of marine ranching areas may restrict some conventional capture activities, strengthen the management of fishery resources, and increase the relative emphasis placed on mariculture and other marine-ranching-related activities. The program may consequently reduce marine capture production. Although reducing marine capture production is consistent with the resource-conservation orientation of the NMRDZ program, capture fisheries remain an established source of fishery output and related economic activity in coastal areas (Bene et al., 2016). A contraction in capture production may therefore reduce the current economic contribution of traditional fisheries to coastal development. It may also create short-term pressures on fishers’ employment and incomes, particularly when moving to alternative activities requires skills and productive assets that are not readily available (Chen et al., 2020). Where affected households have limited access to alternative income-generating opportunities, these adjustment pressures may also weaken the social inclusion dimension of coastal development. Meanwhile, the benefits associated with resource conservation and the development of alternative fishery activities may require more time to materialize (Kitada, 2018). Potential economic and social adjustment costs may therefore partially offset the positive effect of the NMRDZ program on IGG before these benefits are fully realized. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H3The NMRDZ program generates an offsetting effect on IGG in coastal cities by reducing marine capture production. 3.3 Moderating effects of local conditions The effects of a place-based development program may depend on the conditions under which it is implemented. Although the NMRDZ program provides policy support and construction inputs, cities differ in their ability to combine these inputs with local technologies and industrial activities. Green technological innovation and industrial structure advancement may therefore influence the extent to which the program contributes to IGG. 3.3.1 The moderating role of green technological innovation Green technological innovation can support improvements in fishery production and marine resource management. Technological knowledge related to intelligent monitoring, precision feeding, disease prevention, water-quality management, and production control can improve the collection and use of information in mariculture and marine ranching operations (Fore et al., 2018; Henriksson et al., 2021). Previous studies have also identified technological support and collaborative innovation as important conditions affecting the ecological efficiency and project performance of marine ranching (Du et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2021; Wang W. et al., 2024). City-level evidence also links green technological innovation with multidimensional high-quality development (Zhang M. et al., 2026). A stronger local green innovation base can facilitate the development, adaptation, and diffusion of technologies relevant to marine ranching. It can improve the connection between research institutions, enterprises, and fishery operators and support the application of technological knowledge in production management and resource monitoring. Green technological innovation can also facilitate the extension of information management and quality-control practices from mariculture production to processing, distribution, and other stages of the fishery value chain (Rowan, 2023). These conditions can strengthen the conversion of NMRDZ-related facilities and policy support into broader improvements in production and resource management. The positive effect of the NMRDZ program on IGG is therefore expected to be stronger in cities with higher levels of green technological innovation. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H4Green technological innovation positively moderates the effect of the NMRDZ program on IGG in coastal cities. 3.3.2 The moderating role of industrial structure advancement Marine ranching is connected not only with primary fishery production but also with seafood processing, cold-chain logistics, product distribution, recreational fisheries, marine tourism, and related services. The broader effects of the NMRDZ program therefore depend partly on whether changes in fishery production can be connected with secondary and tertiary activities. A more advanced industrial structure can provide stronger links among production, processing, distribution, and services, allowing marine-ranching-related activities to contribute to a wider range of local economic activities (Ababouch et al., 2023; Qian, 2026; Shi et al., 2023). Industrial structure advancement can also provide more diversified channels for reallocating production factors when the composition of fishery production changes. In cities with stronger secondary and tertiary sectors, increases in mariculture output can be more readily linked with processing and market activities, while adjustments in marine capture production can be accommodated through a broader range of related activities. These industrial linkages can strengthen the overall contribution of the NMRDZ program to city-level IGG. The positive effect of the program is therefore expected to increase with the advancement of the local industrial structure. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed: H5Industrial structure advancement positively moderates the effect of the NMRDZ program on IGG in coastal cities. (2025), we adapt the measurement framework to the characteristics of coastal cities and the availability of data. IGG is evaluated along three dimensions: economic growth, social inclusion, and environmental sustainability. Economic growth is assessed using gross fishery output value and GDP per capita. The former measures the size of the fishery economy, whereas the latter reflects the city’s overall economic development. Three indicators are used to assess social inclusion: per capita disposable income of rural residents, the urban-rural income ratio, and the minimum living allowance recipient rate. They capture rural income, disparities between urban and rural incomes, and dependence on basic social assistance, respectively. The environmental sustainability dimension comprises the coastal water quality rating and wastewater discharge. These indicators are prioritized because they capture two complementary aspects of environmental sustainability relevant to coastal development. Coastal water quality characterizes the environmental conditions in which marine fisheries operate, while wastewater discharge serves as a proxy for land-based pollution pressure associated with urban activities. Their selection also takes into account the availability of comparable annual data at the city level. The environmental component of the index therefore focuses on coastal water conditions and land-based pollution pressure. Other potential indicators, such as carbon emissions and carbon sequestration, would extend the assessment to climate-related outcomes beyond this measurement scope. Indicator weights and the composite IGG index are derived using the entropy-weighting method. Larger composite scores represent higher IGG. Table 1 reports all indicators included in the index. The China Coastal Marine Environmental Quality Bulletin provided coastal water quality records for 2011–2016. The China Seawater Quality Monitoring Information Disclosure System supplied the corresponding observations for 2017–2024. To ensure comparability across the two data sources, coastal water quality observations were coded consistently according to the five-category classification and scoring method described in the note to Table 1. The China Urban Construction Statistical Yearbook provided the wastewater discharge figures. For cities in Guangdong and Jiangsu, gaps in mariculture and marine capture production were filled with information from the Guangdong Rural Statistical Yearbook and Jiangsu Rural Statistical Yearbook, respectively. The China National Intellectual Property Administration supplied data on green patent applications. We derived the environmental regulation measure from work reports issued by the sampled city governments. Cities were excluded because data required for the analysis were unavailable or had extensive gaps. These data limitations included missing coastal water quality records in cities such as Hangzhou, Shaoxing, Sansha, and Danzhou; incomplete marine fisheries production or mariculture area data in cities such as Jinzhou, Yingkou, and Huludao; and unavailable rural residents’ disposable income data in Shenzhen. The final sample comprises 37 coastal prefecture-level cities observed annually from 2011 to 2024, yielding 518 city-year observations. Before estimation, we interpolated a limited number of remaining missing values within the retained sample to obtain a balanced panel. This estimate accords with evidence that the program enhances regional ecological efficiency (Qin and Nie, 2024), stimulates fisheries economic development (Wu et al., 2025), and supports high-quality development in coastal cities (Qin and Guo, 2025). Whereas these studies examine particular economic, ecological, or urban-development outcomes, the present study evaluates the program through a city-level IGG index that incorporates economic development, social inclusion, and environmental sustainability. The positive estimate indicates an improvement in the overall multidimensional development performance captured by the index. By adopting this integrated perspective, the analysis extends existing evaluations of marine ranching from individual sectoral outcomes to its broader relevance for inclusive and environmentally sustainable development in coastal cities. The event-time estimates further show that the improvement in IGG does not emerge immediately. The coefficients for the year of designation and the first year after designation are statistically insignificant, while the estimated effect becomes positive and statistically significant in the second year after designation. This delayed pattern suggests that the city-level effects of the NMRDZ program require time to materialize. Previous studies have shown that the economic and ecological outcomes of stock enhancement, artificial reefs, and fisheries resource conservation depend on sustained management, resource recovery processes, and project operating cycles (Hilborn et al., 2020; Kitada, 2018). Consistent with this evidence, marine ranching requires time to progress from infrastructure construction and technology adoption to the formation of aquaculture capacity, extension of industrial chains, and creation of employment opportunities. Meanwhile, the output and livelihood adjustments associated with the contraction of traditional capture fisheries may occur before the benefits of resource recovery and alternative industries are fully realized. 6.2 Two transmission channels through changes in fisheries production The mechanism estimates identify two production-side changes with different relationships to IGG. In the DID specifications, NMRDZ designation is followed by an estimated increase in MOPA and an estimated decline in MCP. Both production measures are positively associated with IGG. These results support a reinforcing role for higher MOPA and an offsetting role for lower MCP. The positive MOPA channel is consistent with previous research linking marine ranching development to improvements in fishery production conditions and eco-efficiency (Chen Y. et al., 2024), as well as research identifying productivity improvement as an important component of sustainable aquaculture development (Henriksson et al., 2021). In this study, MOPA measures the amount of mariculture output obtained per unit of recorded farming area. Its positive association with IGG indicates that the increase in mariculture output relative to farming area constitutes one channel through which the NMRDZ program contributes to the broader development performance of coastal cities. Research on aquaculture value chains further suggests that production at the farming stage can support downstream processing and market access (Ababouch et al., 2023), providing a broader economic context for the positive relationship between MOPA and city-level IGG. The MCP channel reflects a different aspect of the adjustment in fisheries production. Although fisheries management is important for improving the status of fish stocks (Hilborn et al., 2020), marine capture fisheries continue to contribute to production and livelihoods in coastal areas. Previous studies show that fishers’ responses to fishing-exit policies and coastal livelihood transitions depend on their skills, assets, and access to alternative economic opportunities (Chen et al., 2020; Dong et al., 2025; Fabinyi et al., 2022). Against this background, a contraction in marine capture production may weaken part of the contemporaneous contribution of capture fisheries to coastal development. This helps explain why the reduction in MCP partially offsets, rather than reinforces, the positive effect of the NMRDZ program on IGG. Taken together, the two channels reveal that the overall effect of the NMRDZ program involves both a reinforcing contribution from higher mariculture output per unit area and an offsetting contribution from lower marine capture production. The positive baseline estimate indicates that the overall policy effect remains positive within the study period, while the offsetting MCP channel highlights the importance of managing adjustments in capture fisheries. 6.3 Heterogeneous policy effects across cities The heterogeneity analysis by marine economic circle shows that the NMRDZ program significantly increases IGG both within and outside the Northern Marine Economic Circle, with a numerically larger coefficient for northern cities. This pattern differs from Wu et al. (2025), who find that the program mainly benefits the fisheries economy in the East and South China Sea regions. The difference may reflect the use of different outcome variables and regional groupings: the present study examines the multidimensional outcome of IGG and combines the eastern and southern circles into a single non-northern group. Together with the regional variation documented by Zhong et al. (2024), the result suggests that the observed regional pattern depends partly on the development outcome being evaluated. The estimated policy effect is positive in both population groups, but statistically significant only in cities above the 2014 population median. The evidence therefore points more clearly to IGG gains in more populous cities. Several factors may help explain this pattern. More populous cities may provide broader market demand and stronger links with supporting economic activities, consistent with the importance of value chains and market access identified in the aquaculture literature (Ababouch et al., 2023). Where smaller-population cities face fiscal constraints, investment in supporting infrastructure and sustained project operation may be restricted. Shortages of specialized personnel and technical support could also hinder the adoption and maintenance of marine-ranching technologies. Where projects are relatively small and have limited connections with local employment and supporting industries, their benefits may be less visible in a city-level composite index. The positive but statistically insignificant estimate leaves the aggregate IGG effect in smaller-population cities uncertain and does not establish an absence of policy benefits. The analysis by designation batch shows positive and statistically significant effects for both the initial and subsequent designation batches, with a numerically larger coefficient for the initial batch. This extends previous studies focusing on the average effects of the NMRDZ program (Qin and Guo, 2025; Qin and Nie, 2024) by showing that the positive IGG effect is not confined to the initial batch. The larger initial-batch estimate is consistent with the dynamic finding that the program effect takes time to emerge, because these cities have experienced longer policy exposure within the sample period. Nevertheless, differences in treatment timing and city composition mean that the coefficient gap should be interpreted descriptively rather than as evidence of policy learning or stronger implementation capacity. 6.4 Moderating roles of green technological innovation and industrial structure advancement The moderation results indicate that the estimated effect of the NMRDZ program on IGG is larger in cities with higher levels of green technological innovation. Green technological innovation is measured by the natural logarithm of annual green patent applications and captures the level of environmentally oriented innovation activity in a city. The positive interaction coefficient indicates that the estimated IGG effect of the program increases with the level of green technological innovation. This finding is consistent with previous studies identifying technological support and innovation as important conditions for the ecological efficiency and performance of marine ranching (Du et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2021; Wang W. et al., 2024; Zhang M. et al., 2026). Greater accumulation of green technological knowledge may provide a more supportive environment for adapting and applying environmentally oriented production and management practices, thereby enhancing the contribution of the NMRDZ program to IGG. The estimated effect of the NMRDZ program on IGG is also larger in cities with a more advanced industrial structure. The ISA index is constructed from the GDP shares of the primary, secondary, and tertiary sectors, with higher scores representing a more advanced sectoral composition. The positive interaction coefficient indicates that the estimated IGG gains from the program are larger in cities with higher levels of industrial structure advancement. This result is consistent with studies emphasizing industrial integration and value-chain development in realizing the broader benefits of marine ranching (Qian, 2026; Shi et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025). A more advanced sectoral composition may provide a supportive economic context for connecting changes in fisheries production with broader urban development, thereby strengthening the contribution of the NMRDZ program to IGG. 6.5 Theoretical contributions, limitations, and future research First, this study connects the literature on IGG with research evaluating marine ranching policies. Previous evaluations of the NMRDZ program have mainly examined ecological efficiency, fisheries economic development, or the high-quality development of coastal cities as separate outcomes (Qin and Guo, 2025; Qin and Nie, 2024; Wu et al., 2025). By introducing a city-level IGG index, this study evaluates the program from an integrated perspective that incorporates economic development, social inclusion, and environmental sustainability. It thereby extends the evaluation of marine ranching beyond individual sectoral outcomes and demonstrates its relevance to the broader sustainable development of coastal cities. Second, this study contributes to understanding fisheries production restructuring by distinguishing two observable production-side changes: higher mariculture output per unit area and lower marine capture production. The empirical results distinguish between the reinforcing transmission effect associated with higher mariculture output per unit area and the offsetting transmission effect associated with lower marine capture production. These opposing channels show that different production changes induced by the same program need not contribute to IGG in the same direction. This distinction provides a more differentiated understanding of how marine-ranching development is related to the overall balance between fisheries production and inclusive green development. Third, this study adds a context-dependent perspective to the evaluation of the NMRDZ program. The subgroup results show that the estimated effects vary across marine economic circles, population groups, and designation batches. The moderation results further indicate that the estimated policy effect is larger in cities with higher levels of green technological innovation or a more advanced industrial structure. These findings extend previous research emphasizing the importance of regional conditions, technological support, and industrial integration in shaping marine-ranching performance (Du et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2023). They also show that the contribution of the NMRDZ program to IGG depends partly on the local context in which it is implemented. Despite these contributions, this study has limitations that suggest directions for future research. First, in terms of measurement scope, the IGG index represents environmental performance through indicators of coastal water quality and land-based pollution pressure. A broader assessment could also include climate-related outcomes, such as carbon emissions and carbon sequestration. Tan et al. (2025) incorporate carbon footprints and carbon sequestration into mariculture structure optimization alongside water use and production objectives. This provides a useful extension for future evaluations seeking to examine both water-related environmental conditions and net carbon outcomes. Second, in terms of empirical scope, the analysis focuses on city-level outcomes under China’s NMRDZ program in 37 coastal cities during 2011–2024. Potential sample-selection bias associated with data availability should be considered when extending the findings to other coastal cities. At the city level, the estimates capture aggregate changes, while differences in fishers’ employment and income adjustments require more detailed household and project-level evidence. Future research could expand city coverage as data availability improves, combine household and project-level evidence with city-level evaluations, and extend the observation period to examine the distribution of policy benefits and longer-term ecological and livelihood changes. 7 Conclusions and policy implications 7.1 Main conclusions Based on the staggered timing of NMRDZ designation in 37 Chinese coastal cities over 2011–2024, the analysis evaluates the program’s overall effect on IGG, its two production-side transmission channels, and variation across city groups and local conditions. Four central findings emerge from the empirical results. First, the NMRDZ program significantly improves IGG in coastal cities, with an estimated average increase of 0.174 standard deviations. The event-time estimates show that the effect is statistically insignificant in the year of designation and the first year thereafter, becomes positive and statistically significant in the second year after designation, and remains positive in subsequent years, although its magnitude and statistical precision vary. The main result remains robust to heterogeneity-robust staggered DID estimation, placebo tests, an alternative construction of the IGG index, the exclusion of higher-administrative-status cities, and separate controls for concurrent marine policies. Second, the mechanism estimates identify two production-side transmission channels operating in opposite directions. The NMRDZ program increases mariculture output per unit area, and the increase constitutes a positive transmission channel for IGG. The program also reduces marine capture production. Because marine capture production is positively associated with IGG, this reduction produces an offsetting transmission effect. The positive baseline estimate indicates that the overall effect of the program remains positive despite the offsetting MCP channel. Third, the subgroup estimates show that the positive effect of the NMRDZ program is statistically significant both within and outside the Northern Marine Economic Circle, with a numerically larger coefficient for northern cities. When the sample is divided according to the median permanent resident population in 2014, the estimate is positive and statistically significant for larger-population cities but positive and statistically insignificant for smaller-population cities. The estimated effects are also positive and statistically significant for both the initial and subsequent designation batches, with a numerically larger coefficient for the initial batch. Fourth, green technological innovation and industrial structure advancement positively moderate the effect of the NMRDZ program on IGG. The estimated effect of the program is larger in cities with higher levels of green patent application activity and a more advanced sectoral composition. These results indicate that the contribution of the NMRDZ program to IGG varies with the local innovation and industrial context. 7.2 Policy implications First, the NMRDZ program should be implemented with policy continuity and evaluated over an appropriate time horizon. The dynamic estimates show that the improvement in IGG does not become statistically evident until the second year after designation. Evaluations conducted only in the designation year or the first year thereafter may therefore provide an incomplete assessment of program performance. A phased monitoring framework should be established to track changes over multiple years and to support periodic adjustment of implementation arrangements. Because IGG represents a multidimensional development outcome, program evaluation should consider economic development, social inclusion, and environmental sustainability alongside conventional indicators of fisheries production. This would enable the NMRDZ program to be assessed in terms of its broader contribution to sustainable coastal development rather than only its short-term production outcomes. Second, policy implementation should coordinate the two opposing transmission channels associated with mariculture and marine capture fisheries. The positive MOPA channel suggests that greater attention should be given to improving mariculture output relative to farming area through appropriate production services, management support, and locally suitable technological applications. At the same time, reductions in marine capture production should be accompanied by support for affected fishery participants and communities. Local fisheries and employment departments could jointly establish a transition-support mechanism combining skills training, job placement, and temporary livelihood assistance where adjustment needs are identified. Training should be linked to locally available opportunities in mariculture, seafood processing, recreational fisheries, and related services. Cooperation with project operators and local employers could help connect training with actual employment opportunities. Program monitoring should track both MOPA and MCP, together with employment and income changes among affected participants, to inform adjustments to production and transition-support measures. Third, implementation strategies should reflect local development conditions. For smaller-population cities with limited supporting capacity, local authorities could prioritize phased development of small- and medium-sized marine-ranching projects. Implementation plans could identify opportunities for local fishers to participate in production, maintenance, and related services, with subsequent expansion guided by operational performance and local participation. Across marine economic circles, supporting measures should reflect local fishery resources, ecological conditions, and existing value-chain infrastructure. Cities with established processing and distribution networks could strengthen links between marine-ranching production and downstream activities, while cities with limited supporting infrastructure could access existing regional processing and cold-chain services through cooperation with nearby cities. The GTI and ISA moderation results also suggest coordinating marine-ranching development with local innovation and industrial development strategies. Technical demonstrations, operator training, and access to technical advice could support the application of suitable green technologies. Cooperation among producers, processors, distributors, and service providers could strengthen the connections through which marine-ranching production contributes to the local economy.
