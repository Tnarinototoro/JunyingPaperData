Abstract

Rapid urbanization in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has expanded informal settlements where residents face extreme heat, yet informal settlement thermal dynamics remain understudied. Nowhere is this more evident than in Nairobi, one of SSA’s most rapidly growing metropolises, which faces critical challenges from unregulated informal settlement expansion, characterized by high population density and threats to urban sustainability and resident well-being. Using Landsat 8/9 imagery from three February scenes (2015, 2020, 2025), remotely sensed indices, and geospatial data, this study investigated land surface temperature (LST) evolution across Nairobi’s 17 subdivisions and 13 mapped informal settlements, of which 12 were retained for formal analysis. Informal settlements consistently exhibited elevated LSTs, with Kibera approximately 2.6 °C warmer than its parent administrative subdivision in 2015 (34.28 °C vs. 31.67 °C), a pattern that persisted with variations of 1.5–2.9 °C. The urban heat island followed a persistent east–west gradient: industrial and informal settlements were > 95% heat-dominated, while western highlands remained cool. The anomalously wet February of 2020 temporarily reduced area-weighted mean LST from 33.1 °C (2015) to 29.9 °C (2020), with partial rebound to 30.7 °C by 2025, without resetting the spatial pattern. Because this wet anomaly confounds interpretation as a continuous trend, the 2015–2025 comparison was treated as an interannual snapshot comparison rather than a linear trend analysis. Standard deviation ellipse (SDE) analysis revealed a net southeastward shift of the heat island centroid (~ 0.72 km). NDBBI showed the strongest positive correlation with LST (r = 0.86) and FVC the strongest negative correlation (r = − 0.77). Spatial principal component analysis (SPCA) extracted three composite drivers explaining 77.2% of variance. A tentative comparison of summed regression coefficients suggested a larger aggregate association for human-related factors than natural factors, though these are associations, not causal estimates. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions for Nairobi’s approximately 2.5 million informal settlement residents.

1 Introduction

Rapid urbanization is reshaping urban climates worldwide, yet its most severe thermal consequences often fall on those least equipped to adapt. By 2050, nearly 68% of the global population will live in cities, with the fastest growth occurring in low- and middle-income countries across sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and Asia [1, 2]. In SSA, much of this expansion takes the form of informal settlements. According to the United Nations Human Settlements Programme (UN-Habitat), informal settlements are defined by three key features: (1) a lack of security of tenure; (2) inadequate access to basic services and infrastructure; and (3) housing that fails to comply with building regulations and is often located in hazardous areas [3]. These characteristics serve as the foundation for understanding and identifying informal settlements globally. Residents of such settlements therefore face overlapping vulnerabilities, including heightened exposure to environmental hazards [4,5,6]. Among these hazards, the urban heat island (UHI) effect, defined as the sustained elevation of urban air and surface temperatures relative to surrounding rural areas [7,8,9], poses a mounting threat to public health, energy security, and economic productivity [10,11,12,13]. Within cities, the thermal burden is often spatially uneven, raising urgent questions of urban heat inequity. The UHI manifests as surface UHI (SUHI), measured by satellite-derived land surface temperature (LST), and atmospheric UHI (AUHI), measured by ground-based air temperature [14,15,16]. Although the two are correlated, they are not interchangeable and exhibit distinct diurnal and seasonal dynamics [17, 18]; this study focuses on SUHI, which provides the spatial coverage and temporal consistency required for comparative analysis across informal settlements.

The UHI effect is primarily associated with the replacement of vegetated, pervious surfaces by impervious materials that store and re-radiate heat, compounded by anthropogenic heat release from vehicles, industry, and buildings [19]. While UHI dynamics have been extensively documented in Global North cities [20,21,22], the thermal environments of SSA cities remain critically understudied, despite their distinctive urban forms: rapid, unplanned growth; widespread use of low-albedo, heat-retaining construction materials (e.g., galvanized iron sheets); and limited institutional capacity for heat adaptation [23, 24]. This knowledge gap is especially acute for informal settlements, where extremely high population densities, cramped dwelling layouts, near-absence of vegetation, and indoor heat-generating activities (cooking, small-scale manufacturing) may amplify local temperatures well beyond those of neighbouring formal areas [25,26,27]. Understanding these micro-scale thermal contrasts and their drivers is essential for designing equitable heat-mitigation strategies.

Nairobi, the capital of Kenya, exemplifies these intertwined challenges. Its population has surged from roughly 509,000 in 1969 to over 5.4 million in 2025, with an estimated 60–70% of residents living in informal settlements that occupy less than 10% of the city’s residential land [28, 29]. Settlements such as Kibera, Mathare, Mukuru, and Korogocho are typified by iron-sheet roofing, mud-walled structures, narrow unpaved paths, and fractional vegetation cover (FVC) often below 10% [6, 30, 31], physical traits that strongly predispose them to heat retention. Kibera, often cited as one of Africa’s largest informal settlements, covers approximately 2.38 km² with a population density of about ~ 87,500 persons per km² [32, 33]. Despite this, systematic, multi-temporal assessments that isolate informal settlement-specific thermal conditions are scarce. Previous work has either mapped city-wide UHI patterns without disaggregating informal settlement thermal loads [34, 35] or relied on a few ground-based weather stations that cannot capture intra-urban heterogeneity [24]. Consequently, a decadal-scale view of how surface temperatures and heat-island zones have evolved inside Nairobi’s informal settlements remains lacking.

Beyond mapping thermal patterns, identifying the principal biophysical and socioeconomic forces shaping informal settlement surface temperatures is critical for targeted intervention. The thermal environment of an informal settlement is a product of multiple interacting factors: urban construction intensity (impervious surfaces, building density, surface albedo), vegetation and waterbodies, topography, and human activities (population density (PD), points of interest (POI), road networks). Many of these variables are spatially correlated and exhibit complex co-variation, and earlier studies relying on bivariate correlation or classical regression may overlook the multidimensional structure of the drivers and struggle with collinearity [36, 37]. Spatial principal component analysis (SPCA) is well suited to this task because it is a data-driven dimension-reduction technique that transforms correlated predictors into a few orthogonal components, isolating the dominant composite drivers of LST without the collinearity problems of classical regression [34,35,36]. SPCA has been widely applied in environmental and land-use studies [38, 39], but its use to unpack the integrated drivers of thermal conditions in informal settlement-dominated environments has received surprisingly limited attention.

The choice of SPCA over alternative spatial regression approaches warrants brief justification. Geographically weighted regression (GWR) and spatial autoregressive models (SAR) are established methods for estimating spatially varying coefficients and for modelling spatial dependence in urban thermal studies [40,41,42], but they address a different analytical objective from the one pursued here. GWR requires specifying a bandwidth that governs the spatial scale of coefficient variation, and while a sufficiently small bandwidth can capture abrupt spatial transitions, the resulting coefficients become sensitive to this choice and can grow unstable in data-sparse settings. SAR requires specifying a spatial weights matrix, which is subjective and can influence results. Neither method is designed for the dimension-reduction task central to this study. It is acknowledged, however, that SPCA does not explicitly model spatial dependence; the resulting coefficients are therefore interpreted as comparative indices of relative influence rather than spatially precise effect estimates, and GWR and SAR are treated as complementary approaches for future work.

This study therefore employs a multi-step analytical framework: (i) Pearson correlation to screen the direction and magnitude of individual driver–LST relationships; (ii) SPCA to derive orthogonal latent factors that capture the joint covariance structure of the biophysical and socioeconomic predictors; and (iii) multiple linear regression on the SPCA components to quantify the relative contributions of these latent factors to LST variation. Because the framework does not explicitly model spatial dependence, the coefficients are interpreted as comparative indices of relative influence, and spatially explicit methods (i.e., GWR, SAR) are identified as priorities for future work.

Equally important is the temporal dimension. The decade 2015–2025 spans major urban transformations in Nairobi under Kenya’s Vision 2030 agenda [43], notably the completion of the Standard Gauge Railway (2017) [44] and the construction of the Nairobi Expressway (2020–2022) [45], alongside natural climate variability such as the anomalously wet February of 2020. Cloud-free Landsat imagery from the dry-season months of January–February was selected for 2015, 2020, and 2025 to ensure consistent phenological conditions and minimize atmospheric interference. The 2015 image serves as a pre-Expressway baseline, the 2020 image captures the early construction phase and the wet anomaly, and the 2025 image reflects the most recent post-Expressway urban landscape. Because the February 2020 rainfall anomaly (186.4 mm, 221% above the long-term mean) suppresses surface temperatures relative to a typical dry season, the three scenes are treated as an interannual snapshot comparison rather than a continuous trend analysis.

Guided by a social-ecological-technical systems perspective on urban heat, this study tests three hypotheses: (H1) informal settlements exhibit systematically higher LST than their surrounding formal areas, with the magnitude of this differential varying by settlement size, population density, and vegetation cover; (H2) the spatial structure of the urban heat island, characterized by an east–west thermal gradient, has remained stable over the study period despite interannual weather variability, with infrastructure development associated with a net southeastward shift of the heat island centroid; and (H3) human-driven factors (urban construction intensity, socioeconomic activities) exert a stronger aggregate association with informal settlement LST than natural factors (vegetation, waterbodies, geomorphology), with the former overwhelming the mitigating capacity of the latter.

To test these hypotheses, this study addresses three questions: (1) How have LST and UHI zones evolved across Nairobi’s 17 administrative subdivisions and 13 mapped informal settlements from 2015 to 2025? Of these, 12 settlements are retained for formal driver analysis, with Majengo treated separately as a boundary-digitization outlier. (2) What are the principal biophysical and socioeconomic drivers of informal settlement thermal conditions, and what are their relative contributions? (Pearson correlation and SPCA) (3) To what extent do human-related factors outweigh natural factors in shaping the net thermal environment? (multiple linear regression on SPCA components).

To answer these questions, this study integrates multi-temporal Landsat 8/9 thermal imagery, a suite of remotely sensed indices (NDISI, NDBBI, FVC, MNDWI, surface albedo (SA), TWI), and geospatial data on road networks, PD, and POI. Thermal zones are classified and tracked with standard deviation ellipses (SDEs), while SPCA and multiple linear regression uncover the dominant driving dimensions. By combining these approaches, this study addresses a gap identified in prior work: the absence of settlement-disaggregated, multi-temporal thermal assessments in SSA informal settlements by providing a systematic, multi-temporal analysis of informal settlement-specific thermal dynamics in an SSA capital city. Subject to the temporal and methodological constraints noted above, the findings offer actionable evidence for heat-mitigation planning in some of Nairobi’s most vulnerable communities and provide a replicable methodological template for other fast-urbanizing African cities confronting similar thermal inequities, though the specific results should not be generalized without local validation.

2 Study area

Nairobi, the capital of Kenya, is located in the southern part of the country at approximately 1.285°S, 36.817°E (Fig. 1), with an average elevation of about 1795 mASL. The city lies on the eastern edge of the Great Rift Valley, bounded by the Athi River to the east and the Ngong Hills to the west. Nairobi has a temperate subtropical highland climate (Cwb), characterized by moderate temperatures throughout the year [46]. The mean annual temperature is approximately 18.8 °C, and mean annual precipitation ranges from ~ 800 to 1100 mm, with two distinct dry seasons: the hot dry season from January to March and the cool dry season from July to September [47, 48]. The study area is defined by the administrative boundary of Nairobi City County, which covers approximately 696 km². This boundary encloses the most intensively urbanized core where population density exceeds 4500 persons/km², the impervious surface fraction surpasses 60%, and anthropogenic heat emissions are concentrated [49, 50]. Outside this boundary, land use transitions rapidly to agriculture, scattered settlements, and protected forests (e.g., Karura Forest, Nairobi National Park).

Within Nairobi City County, rapid urban growth has led to a proliferation of informal settlements. According to the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS) and UN-Habitat, approximately 60–70% of Nairobi’s population lives in informal settlements, which occupy less than 10% of the city’s residential land [29, 51]. This study focuses on 13 informal settlements: Kibera, Mathare, Korogocho, Mukuru (including Kwa Njenga, Kwa Reuben, and Mukuru Village), Viwandani, Kiambiu, Kawangware, Majengo, Deep Sea Informal Settlement, Huruma, and Tsaaia (Fig. 1c). These settlements vary substantially in size (from ~ 22 ha for Deep Sea to ~ 300 ha for Kibera), population density (from approximately 15,000 persons/km² in Kawangware to over 200,000 persons/km² in Kibera) [52], and building materials. Kibera, Mathare, and Mukuru are dominated by mud-walled structures roofed with corrugated galvanized iron sheets, with narrow unpaved pathways and FVC typically below 10% [53]. By contrast, Kawangware and Deep Sea Slum contain a larger share of stone or concrete-block construction, wider access roads, and scattered vegetation, resulting in FVC values approaching 20% in some blocks. Viwandani and Mukuru Kwa Reuben are additionally characterized by close proximity to the southeastern industrial belt, which contributes localized anthropogenic heat from manufacturing and freight traffic. This internal heterogeneity — in density, construction materials, vegetation cover, and industrial adjacency — provides the empirical basis for the settlement-level comparative analysis and allows the drivers of LST to be examined across a wide range of informal settlement conditions. This diversity allows a robust analysis of the factors driving the thermal environment of informal settlements.

Settlement boundaries for 2025 were obtained from the Kenya Informal Settlements Improvement Project (KISIP) and UN-Habitat’s IDEAMAPS platform (https://data.unhabitat.org/). For 2015 and 2020, boundaries were manually digitized from Google Earth Pro imagery (January–February 2015 and 2020), supplemented by Map Kibera project data and UN-Habitat archives. A positional accuracy of ± 5 m was assumed for digitized boundaries based on GEP’s stated horizontal accuracy [54], which is acceptable for 30 m LST analysis. Historical boundaries were conservatively updated to reflect settlement expansions identified through visual interpretation; ambiguous expansions were excluded to avoid overestimating LST anomalies. The study period (2015–2025) was selected to capture the decadal evolution of Nairobi’s thermal environment following the adoption of the Kenya Vision 2030 development agenda and major infrastructure projects (e.g., Standard Gauge Railway, Nairobi Expressway) that have reshaped land use and urban heat patterns [43]. The three time slices (2015, 2020, 2025) provide a consistent interval for trend analysis while avoiding anomalies caused by the COVID-19 lockdown period (2020 images were acquired before the main restrictions) [55].

3 Materials and methods

3.1 General workflow

As shown in Fig. 2, the research methodology comprised the following stages: (1) multi-temporal data acquisition and pre-processing for Nairobi City County (Landsat 8/9, DEM, roads, POIs, settlement boundaries, population density); (2) LST retrieval using the USGS Collection-2 Level-2 product and classification into seven thermal zones, with focus on heat island areas within informal settlements; (3) calculation of influencing factors (NDISI, NDBBI, MNDWI, TWI, FVC, SA, POI density, road density, PD) and normalization; (4) spatiotemporal comparison of thermal patterns and heat island migration using SDE (2015, 2020, 2025); (5) Pearson correlation, SPCA (four composite drivers), and multiple linear regression to quantify factor contributions; (6) informal settlement specific zonal statistics comparing LST inside informal settlements with formal areas; and (7) proposal of city-specific mitigation strategies. The remainder of this section describes these stages, and additional abbreviations used in this paper are listed in supplementary files (see Table S1).

3.2 Data sources and pre-processing

In this study, we utilized Landsat 8/9 Collection-2 Level-2 surface temperature products obtained from the USGS Earth Explorer (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/, retrieved on March 15, 2025). All images correspond to the hot dry season (February) of 2015, 2020, and 2025, respectively, acquired under clear atmospheric conditions with low cloud cover (< 10%). We deliberately frame this analysis as a comparison of three dry-season snapshots rather than a continuous decadal trend, acknowledging that single-date imagery is influenced by scene-specific atmospheric conditions, soil moisture, and acquisition timing. The consistent February window minimizes seasonal variability, but interannual differences remain a limitation discussed in Sect.  5.4. Additional data comprised DEM from SRTM, road networks and POIs from OpenStreetMap, administrative boundaries (level-3) from GADM, informal settlement boundaries from UN-Habitat and Map Kibera (supplemented by manual digitization from GEP for historical years), and PD from WorldPop (Table 1). For historical OSM data (2015, 2020), we used the OSM History Export tool (https://osm-history.hotosm.org/) to retrieve road and POI data as they existed at the respective dates, rather than using present-day downloads. This ensures temporal consistency with the Landsat imagery. Preprocessing steps included: (i) geometric registration (if required); (ii) resampling all rasters to a common 30 m grid; and (iii) clipping all rasters to ROIs [36, 56]. All vector data were projected to UTM zone 37 S (WGS84) before raster conversion. For informal settlement boundaries digitized from historical imagery, a positional accuracy of ± 5 m was assumed, which is acceptable for 30 m LST analysis.

3.2.1 LST retrieval

LST is a key parameter in urban thermal environment studies, as it directly influences energy fluxes between the atmosphere and the Earth’s surface. For each acquisition date (February 3, 2015; February 20, 2020; and February 9, 2025), LST was derived directly from the “ST_B10” band of the Landsat 8/9 Collection-2 Level-2 surface temperature product. The Collection-2 Level-2 product is generated by the USGS using a single-channel algorithm that incorporates atmospheric correction based on the GEOS-IT reanalysis model and land surface emissivity from the ASTER-GED. Pixel-level quality assurance was performed using the accompanying “ST_QA” band; pixels affected by clouds, cloud shadows, or high aerosol loading were masked prior to analysis. LST values in the “ST_B10” band are stored as scaled integers (digital numbers). These were then converted to degrees Celsius using the official USGS Landsat Collection-2 scale factor and additive offset Eq. (1):

$$ {\text{LST }}\left( {^{{\text{o}}} {\text{C}}} \right){\text{ }} = {\text{ }}\left( {{\text{ST}}\_{\text{B1}}0\, \times \,0.00{\text{3418}}0{\text{2}}} \right)\, + \,{\text{149}}.0{-}{\text{273}}.{\text{15}} $$
(1)

where ST_B10 is the unscaled digital number, 0.00341802 is the scale factor, and 149.0 is the additive offset [57].

The USGS Collection-2 Level-2 LST product has been validated against in-situ measurements over a range of land cover types, with a reported mean bias of approximately 1.0 K and RMSE of 2.1 K over snow-free land surfaces [58]. Urban validation studies have reported uncertainties of 1.5–3.0 °C for Landsat LST retrievals in complex urban environments, which is acceptable for comparative thermal zone classification and factor analysis [59]. Following standard practice in urban thermal remote sensing, we use the relative spatial and temporal patterns of LST rather than absolute values as the basis for our analysis [56, 60]. No independent ground-based validation was performed for this study; it relies on the USGS product’s validated accuracy and interpret results with appropriate caution. Using the standard deviation method, the ROI was reclassified into seven thermal zones: extremely low, low, relatively low, medium, relatively high, high, and extremely high temperature (Table 2). Following previous studies [56, 61], informal heat island zones were defined as the four warmest categories (medium, relatively high, high, and extremely high temperature), where LST exceeds the threshold defined by the city’s mean LST reduced by half the standard deviation.

3.2.2 Calculation of influencing factors

In order to identify the drivers of the informal settlement thermal environment, multiple indices representing human and natural factors influencing the thermal environment were extracted from multivariate geospatial data (Table 3) and grouped into four categories: urban construction intensity, Topography, vegetation/waterbodies, and socioeconomic activity (see supplementary file Table S2).

3.2.3 Spatial principal component analysis (SPCA)

Widely used in urban thermal environment studies, SPCA integrates GIS-based spatial data handling with conventional principal component analysis to transform correlated variables into a smaller set of uncorrelated principal components (PCs) that retain most of the original variance [56, 66, 67]. The PCs are ranked by contribution rate, isolating the dominant composite drivers. In this study, correlation analysis and SPCA were performed in SPSS 27.0 and ArcGIS 10.8 on the natural and human factors shaping land surface temperature, to reduce multicollinearity and identify the primary composite drivers of the informal settlement thermal environment.

The choice of SPCA over alternative spatial regression approaches warrants justification. Geographically weighted regression (GWR) and spatial autoregressive models (SAR) are established methods for estimating spatially varying coefficients and modelling spatial dependence in urban thermal studies [40, 42], but they address a different objective from the one pursued here. GWR requires specifying a bandwidth that governs the spatial scale of coefficient variation; while a sufficiently small bandwidth can capture abrupt spatial transitions, the resulting coefficients become sensitive to this choice and can grow unstable in data-sparse settings. SAR requires specifying a spatial weights matrix, which is subjective and can influence results. Neither method is designed for the dimension-reduction task central to this study: reducing a set of correlated predictors into a few composite drivers. SPCA is suited to this objective because it identifies latent composite drivers from the covariance structure of the predictors themselves, without requiring a priori spatial weights or bandwidths, and the subsequent regression on orthogonal PCs reduces the risk that collinearity would inflate coefficients. It is acknowledged, however, that SPCA does not explicitly model spatial dependence; the resulting coefficients are therefore interpreted as comparative indices of relative influence rather than spatially precise effect estimates. This limitation is discussed in Sect.  5.4, where GWR and SAR are identified as complementary approaches for future work. This approach follows established practice in urban thermal environment studies where SPCA is used to derive composite drivers from spatially distributed variables [56, 61].

To further mitigate spatial clustering, a systematic random sampling design was employed: 900 points stratified across the study area with a minimum distance of 200 m between points, with the number proportional to subdivision area to ensure coverage across the 17 administrative subdivisions [56].

3.2.4 Standard deviation ellipse (SDE)

The SDE, first introduced by Lefever [68], is a spatial statistical method used to analyze the distribution characteristics of geographical elements. With advances in GIS systems, SDE has become a widely adopted tool for examining spatial patterns and temporal shifts through parameters such as the centroid, solar azimuth, and axis deviations [69, 70]. In this study, the SDE tool was utilized to identify the migration trajectory of the heat island from 2015 to 2025. The SDE parameters were calculated using Eqs. (8) and (9):

$$\:\text{Average\:center:}\:{X}_{w}=\frac{{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{n}{w}_{i}{x}_{i}}{{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{n}{w}_{i}},\:\:{Y}_{w}=\frac{{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{n}{w}_{i}{y}_{i}}{{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{n}{w}_{i}}$$
(8)
$$\:{\delta\:}_{x}=\sqrt{\frac{{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{n}{w}_{i}{\left(\stackrel{\sim}{{x}_{i}}cos{\uptheta\:}-\stackrel{\sim}{{y}_{i}}sin{\uptheta\:}\right)}^{2}}{{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{n}{w}_{i}^{2}}\:\:},\:{\delta\:}_{y}=\sqrt{\frac{{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{n}{w}_{i}{\left(\stackrel{\sim}{{x}_{i}}sin{\uptheta\:}-\stackrel{\sim}{{y}_{i}}cos{\uptheta\:}\right)}^{2}}{{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{n}{w}_{i}^{2}}}$$
(9)

where \(\:{x}_{i}\) and \(\:{y}_{i}\) represents the spatial location points, \(\:{w}_{i}\) represents the weight of each observation (here, the LST value at each pixel), \(\:{X}_{w}\) and \(\:{Y}_{w}\) represent the weighted average center, \(\:\theta\:\) represents the azimuth angle of the ellipse’s long axis, \(\:\stackrel{\sim}{{x}_{i}}\) and \(\:\stackrel{\sim}{{y}_{i}}\) represent the coordinate deviations from the average center of each study area, \(\:{\delta\:}_{x}\) and \(\:{\delta\:}_{y}\) represents the x-axis standard deviation and the y-axis standard deviation, respectively. The SDE analysis is sensitive to the threshold used to define the heat island; therefore, the same definition was applied in all three years, with the ellipse computed over heat-island pixels (T ≥ µ − 0.5σ, zones 4–7 of Table 2) weighted by their LST values.

3.2.5 Informal settlement-specific zonal analysis

Zonal statistics were applied to extract the mean LST within each of the 13 mapped informal settlement polygons using the Zonal Statistics as Table tool in ArcGIS 10.8. Of these, 12 settlements were retained for formal driver analysis, with Majengo treated separately as a boundary-digitization outlier (Sect.  4.2.1). For each informal settlement, the mean LST was recorded for 2015, 2020, and 2025. To contextualize informal settlement thermal conditions within their broader urban environment, the mean LST of each informal settlement was compared with the heat island percentage of its parent administrative sub-division (e.g., Kibera within Kibra sub-division). This comparison was performed by overlaying informal settlement polygons onto the thermal zone classification raster and extracting the sub-division-level statistics.

For the correlation and SPCA analyses, a systematic random sampling design was employed to obtain spatially distributed observations while mitigating clustering effects. The study area was stratified into the 17 administrative subdivisions, and sample points were allocated proportionally to subdivision area. A total of 900 points were generated across Nairobi City County with a minimum distance of 200 m between points, following a systematic random procedure. This design ensures spatial coverage across the study area and reduces the likelihood that nearby points with similar LST values dominate the sample. This approach follows established practice in urban thermal studies, where systematic sampling at regular intervals is used to mitigate spatial dependence and spatial autocorrelation [56, 67].

Because the city-specific indicators have varying scale differences, a min–max scaling normalization method was applied to normalize all parameters to a common 0–1 range [71, 72]. Min–max scaling was selected over z-score standardization for three reasons: (1) it preserves the original bounded range of the spectral indices: NDBBI, NDISI, FVC, and MNDWI are inherently scaled between − 1 and + 1, and min–max normalization to 0–1 retains this natural interpretability, whereas z-score standardization re-centres indices on their sample means and obscures the physical meaning of index values (e.g., an FVC of 0.5 no longer corresponds to 50% vegetation cover after standardization); (2) it allows regression coefficients to be interpreted directly as the effect of moving from the minimum to the maximum observed value of each predictor, a conceptually meaningful comparison across predictors with different units (spectral indices, persons/km², km/km²); and (3) although min–max scaling is sensitive to outliers, the data distribution was examined and no extreme outliers (exceeding 3 standard deviations from the mean) were present in the final analysis dataset; the interquartile range was well-behaved for all predictors. The 900 random sample points were checked for anomalous values, and none were removed. This approach follows established practice in urban thermal environment studies that aggregate heterogeneous indicators on a common 0–1 scale for comparative driver analysis [71,72,73].

For the settlement-level comparison of mean LST (Sect.  4.2), each informal settlement polygon was divided into three equal-area sub-polygons, and mean LST was extracted separately for each using the same zonal-statistics procedure. This yielded three replicate observations per settlement, and the identical sub-polygon boundaries were applied in 2015, 2020, and 2025 so that within-settlement heterogeneity was held constant across years. Majengo was excluded from this procedure because its 2015 and 2020/2025 polygons are not spatially comparable (Sect.  4.2.1), leaving k = 12 settlements and N = 36 observations per year (see Table S7).

4 Results and analysis

4.1 Spatiotemporal distribution of LST and thermal zones

In 2015, 2020, and 2025, the spatial distribution of UHI in Nairobi exhibited a distinct thermal pattern, characterized by polycentric irregular agglomeration (Fig. 3; Table 4). High temperature zones (medium to extremely high) consistently concentrated in the southeastern industrial belt (Embakasi, Kasarani) and in high-density informal settlements (Kibera, Mukuru, Mathare), while cold islands (extremely low to relatively low) remained largely confined to the western highlands (Langata, Dagoretti North, Westlands). Four sub-divisions (Embakasi Central, Embakasi North, Makadara, and Ruaraka) were persistently heat-dominated (> 95% heat island) in all three years, with negligible cold zones. In contrast, Langata remained the only sub-division with a consistently substantial cold island (44–66%). Between 2015 and 2025, the most dramatic shifts occurred in Dagoretti North (cold island fell from 75% to 30%) and Kibra (cold island fell from 32% to 15%), likely reflecting rapid densification. The full matrix of thermal zone proportions for each sub-division and year is provided in the supplementary material (Table S3).

The 2020 wet anomaly (February rainfall 186.4 mm vs. 58 mm long-term average, a 221% increase) reduced the area-weighted mean LST from 33.1 °C (2015) to 29.9 °C (2020) (Tables S5, S6). Paradoxically, heat island proportions increased in most sub-divisions despite this absolute cooling (e.g., Dagoretti North’s cold island fraction fell from 75% to 12%; Table 4). This apparent contradiction arises from the relative nature of the thermal zone classification, which uses thresholds tied to the annual mean and standard deviation rather than absolute temperature. When the wet anomaly lowered the city-wide mean, the heat island threshold (µ − 0.5σ) dropped proportionally. At the same time, the cooling was spatially uneven: vegetated and pervious surfaces cooled more efficiently through enhanced evapotranspiration, while impervious surfaces, dominant in informal settlements and industrial zones, retained more heat because rainfall ran off rather than being stored for evaporation. Areas with high impervious cover therefore cooled less than the city-wide average and crossed the lowered relative threshold, even though their absolute temperatures also declined. Dagoretti North illustrates this: its absolute LST fell by 1.28 °C (29.64 °C to 28.36 °C), but the city-wide mean fell by 3.2 °C, so its position relative to the mean improved and it was reclassified from cold island to heat island. This is a reclassification effect driven by uneven cooling, not a physical expansion of the heat island. The wet anomaly temporarily suppressed absolute temperatures while sharpening relative thermal inequality, without altering the underlying spatial structure of thermal vulnerability. The relative ranking of sub-divisions remained broadly similar between 2015 and 2020 (Table 4), suggesting that the anomaly did not reset spatial patterns. By 2025, mean LST increased to 30.7 °C (area-weighted), still below the 2015 level, but heat island proportions rose further. Langata retained 61% cold island, while Westlands shifted from 50% heat in 2020 to 65% cold in 2025. In the absence of land-cover change data, these shifts are interpreted as tentative observations rather than confirmed trends; local greening policies and/or interannual weather variability may also contribute.

4.2 Informal settlement-specific thermal characteristics

Mean LST and area shares for the 13 informal settlements are presented in Table 5, and subdivision-level mean LST values are presented in Table S4. Viwandani recorded the highest mean LST among the 12 settlements retained for formal analysis in all three observation years (34.81 °C in 2015, 32.70 °C in 2020, and 34.49 °C in 2025), whereas Deep Sea Slums recorded the lowest in all three years (28.85, 27.58, and 27.77 °C). A one-way ANOVA indicated significant differences in mean LST among the 12 retained settlements in each year (2015: F(11, 24) = 7.94, p < 0.001; 2020: F(11, 24) = 6.28, p < 0.001; 2025: F(11, 24) = 7.43, p < 0.001; Table S7). Majengo was excluded from this test because its boundary delineation changed substantially across the study period (Sect.  4.2.1), which would violate the assumption of a consistent sampling unit. Post-hoc Tukey HSD tests showed that Viwandani was significantly warmer than Deep Sea Slums in all three years (p < 0.001), whereas several other pairwise differences varied across years (Tables S7, S8).

Kibera exhibited a more muted temporal response, with mean LST declining from 34.28 °C in 2015 to 31.36 °C in 2020 before partially increasing to 32.78 °C in 2025. Most informal settlements showed a similar reduction between 2015 and 2020, followed by partial warming in 2025, consistent with the broader city-wide response to the anomalously wet February 2020 observation. Majengo exhibited a markedly different trajectory and is treated separately as a boundary-digitization outlier (Sect.  4.2.1). Its digitized mean LST decreased from 34.81 °C in 2015 to 28.39 °C in 2020 and 28.79 °C in 2025, a decline of 6.02 °C; the boundary-corrected 2015 value is 32.94 °C (Table 5). Majengo’s mapped area also decreased from 31.9 ha in 2015 to 10.4 ha in 2020 and 10.1 ha in 2025 (4.59%, 1.50% and 1.45% of total mapped informal-settlement area, respectively; Table S9). After boundary correction, excluding a ~ 2.1 ha formal commercial block along Juja Road from the 2015 polygon, the 2015 mean LST falls to 32.94 °C and the decadal change becomes − 4.15 °C (32.94 to 28.79 °C). This residual change remains larger in magnitude than the city-wide area-weighted cooling of 2.4 °C, and attenuates but does not eliminate Majengo’s deviation from the settlement-level mean 2015–2025 change (− 1.0 ± 1.0 °C; Table 5). Because historical settlement boundaries were manually delineated and no independent ground-based observations were available for settlement-level validation, the Majengo trajectory is treated as an anomalous observation requiring cautious interpretation rather than as evidence of confirmed localized cooling. Accordingly, the persistent thermal contrast is more clearly demonstrated by settlements such as Viwandani, Kibera, and Mukuru Kwa Reuben than by Majengo. We therefore avoid using Majengo as evidence for persistent hotspot conditions or localized cooling. Majengo is excluded from the settlement-level ANOVA, post-hoc comparisons, and all settlement-ranking claims.

4.2.1 Majengo: a boundary-digitization outlier

Majengo’s originally digitized mean LST declined by 6.02 °C, from 34.81 °C in 2015 to 28.79 °C in 2025. This is approximately 2.5 times the city-wide area-weighted cooling of 2.4 °C. Three diagnostics indicate that this trajectory reflects a boundary-digitization artifact rather than physical cooling: (i) Majengo’s mapped area share fell from 4.59% in 2015 to 1.50% in 2020 and 1.45% in 2025; (ii) its parent subdivision, Kamukunji, remained > 95% heat-dominated and cooled by only 3.83 °C; and (iii) its 2020 and 2025 mean LST values are nearly identical (28.39 °C vs. 28.79 °C; a change of only + 0.40 °C) despite the marked meteorological contrast between the anomalously wet February 2020 and the drier February 2025. Cross-checking against OpenStreetMap, Sentinel-2 NDVI, and KISIP archives revealed a ~ 2.1 ha formal commercial block along Juja Road that was included only in the 2015 polygon. Excluding this block reduces the 2015 mean LST to 32.94 °C and the 2015–2025 change to − 4.15 °C. The corrected change is smaller in magnitude than the original digitized change but remains larger than the mean 2015–2025 change across the 12 retained settlements (− 1.0 ± 1.0 °C; Table 5), lying approximately 3.2 SD from that mean. The boundary correction therefore attenuates but does not eliminate Majengo’s deviation from the settlement-level mean change. Because the exclusion decision rests on the three diagnostics above rather than on the SD comparison, Majengo is excluded from the settlement-level ANOVA, post-hoc comparisons, and all settlement-ranking claims. Table 5 reports both the original digitized values (34.81 °C in 2015; −6.02 °C for 2015–2025) and the boundary-corrected values in parentheses (32.94 °C in 2015; −4.15 °C for 2015–2025), and Table S9 provides the full sensitivity analysis. The city-wide driver analyses are not sensitive to Majengo’s inclusion. Future work could verify Majengo’s historical boundaries using cadastral records and drone imagery.

4.3 Heat island migration (SDE)

Figure 4 shows the spatial development axis of Nairobi’s UHI from 2015 to 2025 and indicates a distinct southeastward migration (Table 6). From 2015 to 2020, the centroid of the heat island shifted 1.85 km to the southeast, associated with rapid development of the southeastern industrial belt (Embakasi, Kasarani) and along major transport corridors. From 2020 to 2025, the centroid shifted 1.17 km to the northwest, mainly due to ongoing infrastructure developments (Standard Gauge Railway, Nairobi Expressway) and the conversion of agricultural land to industrial and residential use in Embakasi and Athi River. The net displacement over the decade was approximately 0.72 km to the southeast, indicating a persistent but slightly oscillating southeastward thermal expansion. The SDE rotation angle increased from 75.56° in 2015 to 83.56° in 2020 and remained at 82.74° in 2025, likely indicating that the long axis of the ellipse became more nearly east–west over time.

These reversals are consistent with suppression effects, which occur when correlated predictors share variance and a predictor’s partial coefficient reflects the residual signal after conditioning on the others [56, 60, 74]. NDISI, for example, captures both impervious surfaces (which warm) and cooler high-albedo impervious features (which cool); after controlling for NDBBI, the residual signal may be dominated by the latter. Similarly, MNDWI captures both open water (cooling) and moist soils or sediments (variable thermal behaviour); after controlling for FVC, the residual signal may reflect warmer waterbodies or sediments. Because these two coefficients do not estimate their variables’ effects on LST, they cannot be validly used to construct composite indices of “human” versus “natural” influence.

Given this instability, summing the coefficients is presented only as a tentative exploratory comparison. No formal weighting scheme is applied; each coefficient is simply added as-is, and the comparison is intended as a heuristic summary of the relative magnitudes of the coefficients, not as a predictive model or a validated composite indicator. The unweighted summation is used because the predictors were already normalized to a common 0–1 scale, so each coefficient represents a comparable full-range effect; applying arbitrary weights would introduce subjective decisions that the exploratory nature of this comparison does not warrant. A more formal composite-index approach is identified as a priority for future work (see Sect.  5.4). The 95% confidence intervals for these sums (+ 0.382 °C to + 0.460 °C for human factors; −0.118 °C to − 0.090 °C for natural factors) do not overlap. However, because the underlying coefficients for NDISI and MNDWI are unstable, these sums should be interpreted with caution: the non-overlapping confidence intervals reflect the precision of the regression estimates, not the validity of the sums as effect sizes. The comparison is therefore an exploratory indication of relative influence only, and it is not used as the primary evidence for H3.

The more robust evidence for H3 comes from two sources that are not affected by suppression. First, the bivariate correlation structure is stable and interpretable: NDBBI shows the strongest positive correlation with LST (r = + 0.86), while FVC shows the strongest negative correlation with LST (r = − 0.77). These are a human factor and a natural factor of comparable magnitude but opposite sign, which indicates that human-related modification and natural cover are the two dominant opposing influences on LST. Second, the SPCA composite drivers independently support this interpretation: PC1 (urban construction intensity and vegetation deficit, 34.0% of variance) and PC2 (impervious surface–waterbody interactions, 22.3%) together account for 56.3% of the total variance, and both align with the human-versus-natural framing. Taken together, the bivariate correlations and the SPCA structure provide consistent, stable evidence that urban construction intensity and socioeconomic activity are more strongly associated with LST than vegetation and waterbodies in Nairobi’s informal settlements. The coefficient sums, while pointing in the same direction, are retained only as a supplementary exploratory indication.

5 Discussion

5.1 Synthesis of key findings

Building on the results, informal settlements consistently experience higher LSTs than surrounding non-informal areas, a pattern associated with dense built-up structures, crowded populations, and low green cover (Fig. 7). For instance, Kibera recorded an LST of 34.28 °C in 2015, approximately 2.6 °C higher than the mean LST of its parent administrative subdivision Kibra (31.67 °C). This differential ranged from 1.5 °C to 2.9 °C across years and settlements and persisted throughout the study period. The UHI in Nairobi is polycentric and irregular, with high-LST pockets concentrated in the southeastern industrial belt (Embakasi, Kasarani) and within large informal settlements (Kibera, Mukuru, Mathare), whereas the western highlands (Dagoretti North, Langata, Westlands) remain comparatively cool. For example, Embakasi North was classified as > 95% heat zone in 2015, and by 2025 Dagoretti North’s cold-zone fraction had fallen from approximately 75% to 30%.

Across the 17 administrative subdivisions, the area-weighted average surface temperature decreased from 33.1 °C in 2015 to 29.9 °C in 2020, then rose to 30.7 °C in 2025 — a net cooling of 2.4 °C over the decade. Every subdivision recorded its lowest mean LST in the wet year 2020, and all but four (Kamukunji, Mathare, Roysambu, Starehe) showed a partial rebound by 2025 (Table S4). The four exceptions recorded negligible or slightly negative changes (− 0.03 to − 0.35 °C), indicating local stasis rather than a reversal of the broader rebound pattern. This indicates that the anomaly temporarily suppressed surface temperatures without resetting the underlying spatial pattern. As explained in Sect.  4.1, the apparent expansion of heat island proportions in 2020 reflects a reclassification effect driven by uneven cooling, not a physical expansion of the heat island. Despite these interannual fluctuations, the rank order of hot versus cool areas remained stable: informal settlements and industrial zones stayed hottest, while high-vegetation suburbs stayed coolest. These findings echo known patterns in Nairobi and other megacities: Scott et al. [24] found that Nairobi’s informal settlements averaged 1.8–3.1 °C warmer than the greener Dagoretti station, with certain neighbourhoods and times of day showing differences up to 4.8 °C. Similarly, studies in other African cities (e.g., Johannesburg, South Africa) report nighttime LSTs in Soweto’s informal settlements that are 2.48 °C or more higher than in wealthier districts [26].

The regression analysis (Eq. 14) provides a comparative assessment of the relative associations between individual drivers and LST, subject to the methodological caveats discussed below. Because all predictors were min-max scaled to a 0–1 range, each coefficient represents the change in LST (°C) associated with a full-range change in that predictor, that is, from its minimum to its maximum observed value. For example, a full-range increase in FVC is associated with a 0.178 °C decrease in LST, while a full-range increase in NDBBI is associated with a 0.112 °C increase. To aid interpretation, this translates to approximately 0.011 °C per 0.1-unit increase in NDBBI, a more intuitive unit for a spectral index that naturally ranges from − 1 to + 1. These coefficients represent associations in the normalized regression model, not causal effects, and should be interpreted as comparative indices of relative influence rather than precise temperature changes. This interpretation is consistent with the spatial maps: areas of the city with parks, tree canopy, or matted soils (e.g., Langata) maintain cooler surfaces, while densely built informal settlements with impervious roads and roofs retain heat. The results are consistent with the urban climate principle that “grayscape” expansion increases urban heat while green infrastructure mitigates it [60], though this consistency should be read as supporting, not proving, the underlying mechanisms. Moreover, the coefficient for population density (PD, + 0.190 on the scaled range) indicates that moving from the lowest to highest observed population density is associated with an approximately 0.19 °C increase in LST, a magnitude consistent with other remote-sensing studies linking high-density low-income areas to elevated surface temperatures [74]. However, because building materials, cooking practices, vehicular traffic, ventilation characteristics, and adaptive capacity were not directly measured in this study, these mechanisms are inferred from prior research [24,25,26, 75,76,77] rather than demonstrated by the present analysis.

As a tentative exploratory comparison only, summing the standardized coefficients suggests a larger aggregate association for human-related factors (+ 0.421 °C) than for natural factors (− 0.104 °C) under a hypothetical simultaneous minimum-to-maximum change in all variables. This summation is not a predictive model: no weighting scheme is applied, the scenario assumes simultaneous full-range changes that cannot occur in practice, and the sums are compromised by suppression effects in two of their component coefficients (NDISI and MNDWI). The non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals (+ 0.382 °C to + 0.460 °C for human factors; −0.118 °C to − 0.090 °C for natural factors) reflect the precision of the regression estimates, not the validity of the sums as effect sizes. The comparison is therefore a heuristic summary of relative magnitudes only, and it is not used as the primary evidence for H3. The more robust evidence comes from two sources unaffected by suppression: (i) the bivariate correlation structure, in which NDBBI shows the strongest positive correlation with LST (r = + 0.86) and FVC the strongest negative correlation (r = − 0.77); and (ii) the SPCA composite drivers, in which PC1 (urban construction intensity and vegetation deficit) and PC2 (impervious surface–waterbody interactions) together account for 56.3% of the total variance and both align with the human-versus-natural framing. Taken together, these stable analyses support the interpretation that human-related factors have a stronger aggregate association with LST than natural factors in Nairobi’s informal settlements.

5.2 Mechanistic interpretation and context

The observed spatial-temporal patterns are mechanistically consistent with the measured drivers, though the mechanisms themselves were not directly measured in this study. Land cover and form emerge as primary factors. Informal settlements often feature high fractions of dark, impervious materials, including galvanized iron sheet roofs, mud walls, concrete floors, and narrow access paths with little ventilation (Fig. 8), which absorb solar radiation and re-emit heat at night, contributing to local heat retention [24]. While building materials, cooking practices, vehicular traffic, ventilation characteristics, and adaptive capacity were not directly measured in this study, previous research in Nairobi and other SSA cities has documented these as plausible mechanisms contributing to elevated temperatures in informal settlements [24,25,26, 75,76,77]. The high NDBBI and NDISI values observed in informal settlement areas are consistent with these mechanisms, but the present analysis cannot attribute specific temperature increases to individual factors. In contrast, vegetated and high-altitude areas (Langata gardens, Westlands parks) provide shade and evapotranspiration cooling. The findings mirror these physical expectations: LST in informal settlements exceeds surrounding values, and their NDISI and NDBBI are much higher while FVC is minimal, but this consistency should be read as supporting, not proving, the mechanistic interpretation.

Meteorology also modulates these effects. The unusually wet conditions in early 2020 (186.4 mm rainfall vs. the 58 mm long-term mean) contributed to a temporary reduction in absolute surface temperatures. The apparent expansion of heat island proportions in 2020, despite this cooling, reflects a reclassification effect rather than a physical expansion: the relative threshold dropped with the city-wide mean, while impervious surfaces cooled less than vegetated ones and therefore crossed the lowered threshold (Sect.  4.1). The rank order of hot versus cool areas persisted, with Dagoretti North cooled overall in 2020 but remained cooler than Embakasi, indicating that moisture anomalies mainly shift absolute temperatures while the underlying spatial structure, dominated by built-environment differences, remains unchanged. Thus, city-scale climate factors influence magnitude, whereas urban form sets the pattern.

Socioeconomic factors further link into the causal chain. Previous studies suggest that informal settlement dwellers typically have less access to adaptive infrastructure (air conditioning, insulation, fans) and may engage in heat-generating activities (cooking, small-scale industry, traffic) [24,25,26, 75,76,77]. These factors were not directly measured in the present study; the strong positive associations observed between PD, road density, and LST are consistent with these mechanisms, but they do not establish that the mechanisms are the cause of the observed patterns.

A note on the coefficient sums is warranted here, as they have been presented in Sect.  5.1 as a tentative exploratory comparison only. As explained in Sect.  4.5, two coefficients in Eq. (14), namely NDISI and MNDWI, exhibit suppression effects and are unstable; they cannot be validly summed with the other coefficients. The + 0.421 °C and − 0.104 °C sums are therefore heuristic summaries of relative magnitudes, not effect sizes, and the “net warming” of + 0.316 °C implied by their difference is not a physically meaningful quantity. The comparison nevertheless points in the same direction as the more robust evidence, which includes the bivariate correlation structure (NDBBI r = + 0.86; FVC r = − 0.77) and the SPCA composite drivers, and is retained only as a supplementary indication. The pattern is consistent with conceptual models in which the urban energy balance in informal settlements is skewed toward net positive heat flux, but the analysis identifies associations, not causal mechanisms.

The suppression effects observed for NDISI and MNDWI warrant more detailed discussion because they illustrate a broader methodological issue in urban thermal studies. Suppression occurs when a predictor’s partial coefficient reverses sign relative to its bivariate correlation because the predictor shares variance with other predictors in the model [56, 60, 74]. In this study, NDISI is positively correlated with LST bivariately (r = 0.37) but has a negative multivariate coefficient (− 0.235), while MNDWI is negatively correlated with LST bivariately (r = − 0.29) but has a positive multivariate coefficient (+ 0.160). Two interpretations are plausible: (1) NDISI may capture both impervious surfaces (which warm) and cooler high-albedo impervious features such as reflective roofs or light-coloured pavements (which cool). After controlling for NDBBI, which more specifically captures built-up density, the residual signal in NDISI may be dominated by these cooler surfaces; and (2) MNDWI may capture both open water (which cools) and moist soils or sediments (which can have variable or even warming thermal properties). After controlling for FVC, the residual signal in MNDWI may reflect warmer waterbodies or sediment-laden surfaces in the urban environment. Both interpretations are plausible given Nairobi’s heterogeneous landscape, in which waterbodies are often interspersed with industrial and residential areas. Alternatively, the contradictory signs may indicate model instability, a possibility that warrants caution in interpreting any individual coefficient. This is why the present analysis relies on the bivariate correlation structure and the SPCA composite drivers, rather than individual multivariate coefficients, as the primary evidence for H3. Future research using larger sample sizes, alternative model specifications (e.g., GWR, SAR, random forest), or direct field measurements of surface materials and waterbody characteristics could help resolve these inconsistencies.

5.3 Implications for sustainable development

The disproportionate heat exposure documented here, with informal settlements consistently recording LST up to 2.6 °C higher than surrounding formal areas, suggests potential avenues for policy action, particularly given the vulnerability of Nairobi’s approximately 2.5 million informal settlement residents. The following policy entry points are drawn from existing frameworks and prior literature; they are not derived from the present analysis, which identifies associations between urban form and LST but does not test the effectiveness of specific interventions. The recommendations below are therefore presented as options for consideration, subject to local feasibility assessment and stakeholder consultation. (1) Ongoing informal settlement upgrading programmes such as the Kenya Informal Settlements Improvement Project (KISIP) Phase-II and the Kenya Informal Settlement Upgrading Programme (KENSUP) [78] might benefit from incorporating heat mitigation criteria, such as exploring minimum green cover targets for upgraded blocks, considering community-managed pocket parks, or evaluating the feasibility of high-albedo roofing materials (cool roofs) in new housing. The specific green cover threshold of 15% has been proposed in related policy discussions; however, this value is not derived from the present analysis, which did not test the temperature effect of specific green cover fractions. It is an external policy option that would require local feasibility assessment and empirical validation. (2) The Nairobi City County Climate Action Plan (2020–2050) [79] could strengthen its heat resilience provisions by potentially including informal settlement-specific green cover benchmarks, establishing a dedicated urban heat unit within the Department of Environment, Water and Sanitation, or adopting biennial thermal assessments using the Landsat-based methodology demonstrated here. The methodology of this study can support such monitoring; the specific benchmarks would require separate policy deliberation. (3) The Nairobi Informal Settlement Upgrading and Prevention Strategy (validated March 2025) may benefit from incorporating heat-sensitive urban design guidelines, such as minimum pathway widths to improve natural ventilation, zoning provisions that encourage cool roof coatings, or protection of riparian reserves along the Ngong River as natural cooling corridors. These suggestions draw on established urban climate principles [19, 23, 60, 74], not on intervention trials conducted in this study. (4) Kenya’s National Cooling Action Plan (NCAP 2023–2027) [80] could be revisited to include passive cooling considerations for informal settlements, possibly through pilot subsidy mechanisms or microfinance schemes for reflective roof coatings (e.g., titanium dioxide-based paints), subject to feasibility and cost-effectiveness assessments. This recommendation is consistent with the plan’s objectives but is not derived from the present analysis. If these policy levers are implemented alongside robust monitoring and community participation, Nairobi City County may be able to address some of the thermal inequities highlighted here while advancing broader climate resilience and sustainable development goals. Rigorous evaluation of any intervention, including pre/post thermal measurements, control sites, and community feedback, would be essential to establish what works in practice, since the present study identifies associations rather than intervention effects.

5.4 Limitations and future research directions

Four key methodological limitations merit consideration when interpreting the findings of this study. (1) Spatial resolution: the analysis relies on Landsat thermal data with a native resolution of approximately 100 m, resampled to 30 m. This resolution cannot capture micro-scale thermal variation within informal settlement alleys or heterogeneous construction materials, potentially masking intra-neighbourhood differences. (2) Exposure metric: actual human heat exposure occurs at ambient air level rather than at the land surface. Although prior studies support LST as a reasonable proxy for relative thermal stress, this assumption requires validation using in-situ air temperature and humidity measurements. (3) Temporal coverage: the analysis compares only three years (2015, 2020, 2025) and focuses on February, representing dry-season conditions only. This is a substantive limitation: the findings describe thermal conditions during the hot dry season and may not generalize to the cool dry season (July–September) or the two wet seasons. Seasonal cycles, interannual variability, and longer-term trends therefore remain unexamined, and the three-scene design should be understood as an interannual snapshot comparison rather than a continuous trend analysis. (4) Boundary-digitization uncertainty: historical informal-settlement boundaries were manually delineated, and one settlement (Majengo) exhibited a substantial change in mapped area across the study period. As detailed in Sect.  4.2.1, this produced an anomalous LST trajectory that was resolved only after cross-verification and boundary correction. Majengo was therefore excluded from the settlement-level ANOVA, post-hoc comparisons, and all ranking claims. The boundary correction reduces the apparent 2015–2025 change from − 6.02 °C (digitized) to − 4.15 °C (corrected), but the corrected change still lies approximately 3.2 SD from the 12-settlement mean change (− 1.0 ± 1.0 °C; Table 5) and therefore remains an outlier relative to the settlement-level distribution. Majengo’s exclusion is not based on this SD comparison, however, but on the three boundary diagnostics in Sect.  4.2.1: the collapse in mapped area share, the inconsistency with its parent subdivision (Kamukunji), and the near-identical 2020 and 2025 values despite contrasting meteorological conditions. This limitation highlights the sensitivity of settlement-scale LST analysis to historical boundary definition and the need for boundary harmonization in future work.

A fifth limitation concerns the historical socioeconomic and road-network data. Road network and POI density for 2015 and 2020 were retrieved from OpenStreetMap (OSM) via the OSM History Export tool to ensure temporal consistency with the Landsat imagery. However, historical OSM data are known to be spatially and temporally incomplete in rapidly urbanising African cities, with coverage increasing substantially over time as volunteer mapping activity expands [81, 82]. This introduces a potential systematic error: road and POI densities for 2015 and 2020 may be underestimated relative to 2025, which could affect the socioeconomic driver coefficients reported in Eq. (14) and the SPCA loadings for PC1. Because the 2025 OSM data were used for the driver analysis reported in Sect.  4.4 and 4.5, the primary findings are not affected by this issue; however, any future temporal comparison of driver coefficients should account for the changing completeness of OSM data. Independent road inventories (e.g., national mapping agency data) and satellite-derived built-up footprints would provide more temporally consistent alternatives.

An additional limitation relates to model instability and suppression effects. As noted in Sect.  5.2, the negative NDISI coefficient and positive MNDWI coefficient in the regression model may reflect suppression effects or model instability. The bivariate correlations have been reported alongside the multivariate coefficients to allow readers to assess these contradictions. The aggregated sums of human versus natural coefficients are presented as tentative exploratory comparisons only, not as effect sizes, and the analysis relies on the bivariate correlation structure and the SPCA composite drivers as the primary evidence for H3. Future research with larger sample sizes and alternative model specifications (e.g., spatial regression, random forest) may help resolve these inconsistencies. Furthermore, several mechanisms discussed in the interpretation, including building materials, cooking, traffic, ventilation, and adaptive capacity, were not directly measured in this study. These are framed as plausible mechanisms based on previous research [24,25,26, 75,76,77] rather than demonstrated drivers of the observed patterns. Future work should incorporate direct measurements of these factors to strengthen causal inference. A further limitation concerns spatial dependence. SPCA does not explicitly model spatial autocorrelation; coefficients are therefore interpreted as comparative indices of relative influence rather than spatially precise effect estimates. Geographically weighted regression (GWR) and spatial autoregressive models (SAR) would provide complementary insights, allowing a direct comparison of global versus local driver effects and testing whether the suppression effects observed for NDISI and MNDWI (Sect.  4.5) persist under spatially explicit estimation. This is identified as a priority for future research (Table 10).

To address these limitations and extend the analysis, a multi-pronged research agenda is proposed (Table 10). Priority actions include: (1) deploying localized sensor networks in representative informal settlements to measure air temperature, humidity, and heat-stress indices throughout the year, thereby validating satellite-derived LST and capturing diurnal and seasonal cycles; (2) acquiring higher-resolution imagery from drones or PlanetScope to refine micro-thermal mapping within informal settlement areas; (3) conducting household surveys and ethnographic research to link thermal exposure with lived experience, vulnerability, and coping behaviour; and (4) piloting and evaluating cooling interventions, such as green walls and reflective surfaces, under real-world conditions. A further priority is an equity-oriented analysis of heat vulnerability indices that integrates age, health status, and socioeconomic data to identify the most at-risk communities. Future studies should also harmonize historical settlement boundaries using independent geospatial datasets (e.g., cadastral records, archival maps) and validate satellite-derived LST against repeated in-situ observations, particularly for settlements such as Majengo where boundary uncertainty was identified. Acquiring independent road network and POI inventories for historical years would strengthen future temporal comparisons of socioeconomic drivers. Applying GWR and SAR to the same dataset would allow a direct comparison of global versus local driver effects and test whether the suppression effects observed here persist under spatially explicit estimation. These efforts should be co-designed with local stakeholders and, where possible, led by Nairobi-based researchers to strengthen local relevance and long-term sustainability.

6 Conclusions

This study provides a systematic, decadal-scale assessment (2015–2025) of surface thermal dynamics in Nairobi’s informal settlements, combining SPCA-based driver attribution, SDE analysis of heat island migration, and settlement-specific zonal statistics. While each of these methods has been applied individually in other urban contexts, their integration in a single framework for informal settlements in a sub-Saharan African capital city offers a comprehensive view of the coupled biophysical and socioeconomic factors shaping settlement thermal environments. Three robust findings emerge.

  1. (1)

    Informal settlements consistently exhibit elevated LSTs relative to formal neighbourhoods. Kibera recorded LSTs up to 2.6 °C warmer than its parent administrative subdivision in 2015, with differentials ranging from 1.5 °C to 2.9 °C across settlements and years. Viwandani emerged as the hottest settlement among the 12 retained for formal analysis in all three observation years (34.81 °C in 2015, 32.70 °C in 2020, and 34.49 °C in 2025), while Deep Sea Slums remained the coolest in all three years (28.85, 27.58, and 27.77 °C). Majengo’s digitized 2015 value (34.81 °C) tied with Viwandani’s at the reported precision, but after boundary correction it falls to 32.94 °C (Sect.  4.2.1); because its delineation changed substantially over the study period, Majengo is excluded from all settlement-ranking claims. The UHI follows a polycentric, irregular agglomeration pattern, with high-temperature zones concentrated in the southeastern industrial belt (Embakasi, Kasarani) and major informal settlement clusters, whereas western highlands (Langata, Dagoretti North, Westlands) function as cool islands. Notably, four subdivisions (Embakasi Central, Embakasi North, Makadara, and Ruaraka) remained > 95% heat-dominated across all three years, while Langata retained 44–66% cold island area.

  2. (2)

    The spatial structure of the UHI follows a persistent east–west thermal gradient. The anomalous wet conditions of February 2020 temporarily suppressed mean LST but did not reset this spatial pattern. SDE analysis indicates a net southeastward shift of the UHI centroid by approximately 0.72 km over the decade, which is associated with infrastructure development and land use conversion in southeastern Nairobi, rather than being definitively driven by these factors.

  3. (3)

    Vegetation is consistently and negatively associated with LST. Pearson correlation shows LST most strongly positively correlated with NDBBI (r = 0.86, p < 0.01) and most strongly negatively correlated with FVC (r = − 0.77, p < 0.01). SPCA extracts three composite drivers explaining 77.2% of total variance: urban construction intensity and vegetation deficit (PC1, 34.0%), impervious surface–waterbody interactions (PC2, 22.3%), and topographic–wetness effects (PC3, 20.9%). A comparative analysis of regression coefficients suggests that human-associated factors have a substantially larger potential warming influence than the cooling influence of representative natural factors. In this study, we emphasize that these are associations, not causal warming estimates, and the coefficient sums should be interpreted as comparative indices of relative influence rather than precise temperature changes.

These findings carry policy implications for climate-resilient urban development in Nairobi. The disproportionate heat exposure documented here, affecting approximately 2.5 million informal settlement residents, suggests that integration of heat mitigation criteria into existing frameworks, including the Kenya Informal Settlements Improvement Project (KISIP), Nairobi City County Climate Action Plan (2020–2050), and the National Cooling Action Plan (2023–2027), may be considered. Candidate interventions could include minimum green cover for upgraded blocks, community-managed pocket parks, cool roof coatings, and protection of riparian reserves as natural cooling corridors. For example, a 15% green cover target has been proposed in related policy discussions; however, we note that this specific threshold is not derived from our analysis. It is an external policy option that would require local feasibility assessment and stakeholder consultation. Without deliberate policy action, the southeastward expansion of Nairobi’s UHI may exacerbate thermal inequities affecting vulnerable populations.

Future research should deploy localized sensor networks to validate satellite-derived LST with ambient air temperatures, collect high-resolution thermal imagery to resolve intra-settlement heterogeneity, conduct household surveys to link exposure with coping behaviour, and pilot intervention trials to establish the cost-effectiveness of cooling strategies. Particular attention should be paid to harmonizing historical settlement boundaries using independent geospatial datasets (e.g., cadastral records, archival maps, drone imagery), as demonstrated by the Majengo boundary-correction exercise reported in Sect.  4.2.1, and to validating satellite-derived LST against repeated in-situ observations. The combined methodological framework integrating SPCA, SDE, and settlement-specific zonal statistics could be adapted to other fast-urbanising African cities, though the specific findings should not be generalized without local validation. As climate change intensifies and urbanization accelerates across Africa, informal settlements cannot be an afterthought in urban climate adaptation—they must be its central focus.
