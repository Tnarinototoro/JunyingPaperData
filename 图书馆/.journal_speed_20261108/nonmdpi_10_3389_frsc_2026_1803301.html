Voices from the neighborhood: residents perception on local parks

Abstract

Introduction:

Urban parks in Palestinian cities play a vital role in supporting community wellbeing, yet their success depends heavily on how residents perceive, use, and identify with these spaces.

Methods:

This study investigates residents’ perceptions of two major public parks in the two neighboring yet different cities, Ramallah and Al-Bireh, Palestine. The study explores the cultural, spatial, and emotional factors that shape their sense of place. Using a qualitative methodology that combines semistructured interviews and direct observation, the research uncovers key themes related to accessibility, safety, municipal policies, cultural practices, and the contrast between global expectations of parks and deeply local forms of attachment.

Results and discussion:

Findings show that while both parks serve important recreational and social functions, differences in design, management, and cultural compatibility significantly influence how residents connect with them. Al-Bireh’s more flexible policies and familiar spatial practices encourage stronger emotional attachment, while certain design constraints and restrictions in Ramallah reduce users’ sense of belonging. The study concludes that integrating global design standards with local cultural values through improved accessibility, safe design, and socially responsive policies is essential for creating parks that are inclusive, lively, and meaningful to their communities. These insights offer valuable guidance for planners, municipalities, and designers working toward healthier and more resilient urban landscapes in Palestine.

1 Introduction

In developing countries, open spaces play a critical role in urban life, offering residents opportunities for recreation, social interaction, and improved mental health (UN-Habitat, 2013). These areas often serve multiple purposes, functioning as informal gathering places, play areas, and even economic zones. However, rapid urbanization, population growth, and limited planning capacity have led to a shortage of accessible and well-designed public spaces. Many open areas suffer from poor maintenance, lack of safety, and insufficient infrastructure, which affects how people perceive and use them. Despite these challenges, communities often view open spaces as vital resources that contribute to quality of life and neighborhood identity (Kabisch et al., 2016). Enhancing and protecting these spaces is essential for fostering inclusive, sustainable, and resilient cities.

Open spaces in the Palestinian context hold significant cultural, social, and environmental value, especially in dense urban areas like cities and camps, due to ongoing political constraints, limited land resources, and rapid urban expansion, access to green and open areas has become increasingly scarce. These spaces-whether public parks, community gardens, or undeveloped lands offer much needed breathing room for residents, providing a refuge from overcrowded housing and congested cityscapes. They serve as inclusive environments where people from diverse backgrounds can gather, socialize, and engage in recreational activities, promoting social cohesion and community resilience amid the pressures of daily life (UN-Habitat, 2015). Moreover, open spaces play a crucial role in improving the physical and mental wellbeing of Palestinians. In a region where movement is often restricted and stress levels are high, such areas offer opportunities for physical activity, relaxation, and a connection to nature (Weinthal and Sowers, 2019). For children and youth, these spaces are essential for healthy development and safe play (Basu and Nagendra, 2020). Environmentally, they help combat urban heat, support biodiversity, and contribute to sustainable urban development (UNEP, 2020). In the broader sense, preserving and designing open spaces in Palestine is not only an urban necessity but also a form of resistance reclaiming public life, identity, and a right to the city in the face of political and spatial challenges (Shlay and Rosen, 2015).

Open spaces in Ramallah and Al-Bireh are increasingly important yet limited resources within the urban landscape. As both cities experience rapid population growth and ongoing urban development, the availability of accessible and well-maintained public spaces-such as parks, plazas, and community gardens has significantly declined (UN-Habitat, 2015). These open areas provide essential benefits, offering residents opportunities for recreation, social interaction, and relief from the dense built environment. In a context marked by political constraints and land scarcity, such spaces also hold symbolic value, serving as places of community resilience and identity (Shlay and Rosen, 2015). Despite their importance, many open spaces in Ramallah and Al-Bireh face challenges related to overcrowding, lack of maintenance, and uneven distribution across neighborhoods (UN-Habitat, 2020). Ramallah and Al-Bireh were chosen as the focus of this study because they represent two of the most urbanized and densely populated Palestinian cities, where the demand for public space is high and the pressures of urbanization are clearly visible, making them ideal case studies for examining how open spaces function and are perceived in challenging urban conditions.

Understanding how residents perceive and interact with public parks is essential for evaluating the success of these spaces and informing future planning efforts. Existing studies have emphasized the importance of aligning park design and services with community needs to maximize their social and ecological benefits (Peters et al., 2010). In the case of Ramallah and Al-Bireh, however, limited empirical research has explored residents' attitudes, satisfaction levels, and expectations regarding their local parks. This study seeks to fill that gap by examining how users assess the quality, accessibility, and relevance of public parks in their daily lives.

This study presents findings based on qualitative data collected from residents across various neighborhoods in both cities. The research reveals key themes in public perception, including concerns about safety, accessibility for different age groups, cultural compatibility of park rules, and the quality of recreational amenities. By amplifying these community voices, the study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of urban park use in Palestinian cities, offering insights relevant to both local policy and broader discussions on equitable urban development (Dempsey et al., 2011). The study is guided by three research questions: (1) to examine how local residents perceive and experience public parks in Ramallah and Al-Bireh, with attention to accessibility, safety, and cultural compatibility; (2) to identify whether and why these perceptions differ between the two cities; and (3) to explore the spatial, social, and policy-related elements that shape residents' sense of familiarity and belonging in their local parks.

2 Literature review

2.1 Urban parks, well-being, and public space

Public parks are widely recognized as essential components of healthy and liveable cities. They provide residents with opportunities for recreation, social interaction, physical activity, and psychological restoration, all of which contribute to individual and collective wellbeing (Chiesura, 2004). Research consistently shows that access to urban green spaces is associated with reduced stress, improved mental health, and greater social cohesion, particularly in densely populated urban environments where private space is limited (Kabisch et al., 2016). Within the framework of the United Nations 2030 Sustainable Development Goal 11, Target 7, universal access to safe, inclusive, and accessible green and public spaces is identified as a global priority, with particular attention to the needs of women, children, older persons, and persons with disabilities (Duivenvoorden et al., 2020). Parks also contribute to broader urban sustainability, helping to mitigate urban heat, support biodiversity, and strengthen community resilience (UNEP, 2020). In the Palestinian context, the significance of public parks extends beyond recreation: in cities constrained by political restrictions, limited land, and dense settlement patterns, parks represent spaces of collective life and civic identity (Shlay and Rosen, 2015).

2.2 Public space perception and sense of place

Perception, in the context of public space, refers to the cognitive and affective process through which individuals interpret and make meaning of their physical environment. It is shaped by a range of factors including personal attitudes and beliefs, cultural background, prior experience, personality traits, and socio-demographic characteristics (Chiesura, 2004). Research has shown that personal values and expectations act as perceptual filters, meaning that two individuals may respond very differently to the same space depending on how well it aligns with their needs and cultural norms (Rossi et al., 2016). Public spaces can be understood from at least two intersecting perspectives: from a sociocultural perspective, they are places of social encounter and exchange, where groups with different interests and backgrounds come together; from a political perspective, they are arenas of collective life where presence in public carries civic and social meaning (Jim and Chen, 2006; Duivenvoorden et al., 2020). Demographic factors such as age and gender further shape how parks are perceived and used, highlighting the importance of designing spaces that serve diverse populations (Daneshpour et al., 2009; Ma et al., 2021).

Closely related to perception is the concept of sense of place, which describes the emotional and psychological bond that individuals develop with specific locations over time. Sense of place encompasses two core dimensions: place identity, referring to the way a space becomes part of how a person defines themselves and their community; and place dependence, referring to the functional attachment that develops when a space reliably supports valued activities (Rossi et al., 2016). In the urban park context, a strong sense of place emerges when the physical environment, management practices, and social atmosphere align with residents’ cultural expectations and everyday habits. Parks that foster this alignment become embedded in local life and identity; those that do not may remain underused despite their physical quality (Peters et al., 2010).

2.3 Cultural practices, place attachment, and urban parks

Cultural practices and social habits play a foundational role in shaping how public parks are experienced and valued. The physical environment of a park does not exist in isolation; it is always interpreted through the lens of local norms, traditions, and community expectations. Research in environmental psychology has demonstrated that the body’s interaction with space is not merely functional but also emotional, social, and cultural: the quality of a space influences mood, behavior, and a person’s willingness to return (Alkhatib et al., 2021). Urban green spaces, in particular, have been associated with reductions in psychological stress and rumination, enhanced immune function, and increased opportunities for social interaction and physical activity (Alkhatib et al., 2021). These benefits are, however, conditional on whether the space feels welcoming and culturally appropriate. Parks that align with local habits, such as communal eating, intergenerational gathering, or informal use of open ground, are more likely to generate place attachment and habitual use. Conversely, parks governed by rules that restrict such practices may feel exclusionary, undermining the very social functions that parks are meant to serve (Peters et al., 2010). This tension between formal design standards and informal cultural life is a central concern in the literature on public space, and it is directly relevant to the parks studied in this paper.

Public spaces also play an important role in addressing broader urban challenges, including climate adaptation, social inclusion, and urban mobility (Duivenvoorden et al., 2020). The quality and use of public parks are shaped by a complex interplay of social, spatial, cultural, economic, and political factors, including the variety of activities and facilities offered, the diversity of park users by age and gender, and the broader socioeconomic and cultural characteristics of both actual and potential users (Daneshpour et al., 2009). While no single determinant can predict park quality, research suggests that parks which respond to these multiple dimensions are more likely to support social interaction and reduce the exclusion of marginalized groups. Crucially, what constitutes a well-functioning park in one context may not apply to another, and local conditions must always inform design and management decisions.

2.4 Public parks in international contexts

Research from different regions of the world demonstrates that while public parks serve diverse social, cultural, and environmental functions, several recurring factors consistently shape residents’ perceptions and patterns of use. In North America, studies have shown that accessibility, safety, maintenance, and the availability of recreational facilities are among the strongest predictors of park visitation and user satisfaction (Basu and Nagendra, 2020; Wolch et al., 2014). In European cities, public parks are increasingly viewed as essential infrastructure for social cohesion, climate adaptation, and public health, with particular emphasis placed on walkable access and integration into daily urban life (Konijnendijk, 2022; Moreno et al., 2021). Research conducted in cities such as Copenhagen, Barcelona, and Paris highlights the importance of proximity to green spaces and their role in supporting active lifestyles and neighborhood identity.

Studies from Asia similarly reveal that cultural norms and demographic characteristics strongly influence park preferences and usage patterns. Research in Guangzhou, China, found that residents value urban parks not only for environmental benefits but also as places for social interaction, family activities, and cultural expression, with perceptions varying according to age, gender, and socioeconomic background (Jim and Chen, 2006). In Tehran, Iran, perceptions of urban parks were found to be closely linked to the diversity of activities offered, the inclusiveness of the space, and the extent to which parks responded to local social and cultural expectations (Daneshpour et al., 2009). Similar findings have been reported in cities across the Global South, where public parks often serve multiple functions that extend beyond recreation to include community gathering, informal economic activities, and social support networks.

Across these international contexts, a common finding emerges: the success of public parks depends not only on their physical design but also on their ability to respond to the cultural practices, everyday routines, and social needs of local communities. Parks that are accessible, safe, flexible, and culturally compatible tend to foster stronger place attachment, more frequent use, and greater community ownership. Conversely, parks that are disconnected from local needs or governed by overly restrictive regulations often experience lower levels of use despite substantial investments in physical infrastructure. These international experiences provide an important comparative backdrop for understanding public park perceptions in Ramallah and Al-Bireh and reinforce the need for context-sensitive approaches to park planning and management.

2.5 Accessibility, proximity, and urban greening standards

Several urban greening frameworks and benchmarks provide useful reference points for evaluating park provision, though their applicability must be critically assessed in relation to local conditions. One widely referenced framework is the 3-30-300 rule, developed to offer broad guidance on urban sustainability and urban forestry. Drawing on earlier species diversity guidance from Santamour (1990), which recommended limiting any one tree species to no more than 10% of a city’s urban forest, any single genus to no more than 20%, and any single family to no more than 30%, the framework was later expanded in 2021 by Konijnendijk to encompass three neighborhood-scale targets: that every resident should be able to see at least three trees from their home or workplace; that every neighborhood should have a minimum of 30% tree canopy cover for health and cooling benefits; and that every resident should be within 300 m (approximately a 5-minute walk) of the nearest park or green space (Konijnendijk, 2022). While this 300-metre benchmark reflects World Health Organization guidance and has been applied in several European contexts, it is important to acknowledge that walkability and park access standards vary considerably across different urban environments. For example, 5-minute (300–500 m) access standards are commonly used in Germany, Shanghai, and Copenhagen; 10-minute targets are applied in Melbourne; and 15-minute targets are referenced in cities such as Paris, Barcelona, and many UK cities. In the Palestinian context, no formally adopted local standard currently exists for park proximity, and access conditions are further complicated by political restrictions, limited land availability, and fragmented urban infrastructure. These realities make it essential that any benchmark used for comparison is contextualized and critically discussed rather than applied prescriptively. Future research should aim to map the actual distribution and catchment areas of public parks in Ramallah and Al-Bireh in order to assess how current provision relates to residents’ lived access experiences.

2.6 Safety perception in public parks

Perceptions of safety represent one of the most consistently cited factors shaping whether and how people use public parks. Safety in urban green spaces is a multidimensional concept, encompassing physical safety (the absence of hazards, adequate lighting, and well-maintained infrastructure), social safety (freedom from harassment, antisocial behavior, or unwanted encounters), and psychological safety (a subjective sense of comfort, visibility, and control over one’s surroundings) (Mehta, 2014). Research shows that poorly lit entrances, hidden corners, and unmaintained edges substantially increase fear, particularly among women, children, elderly users, and people visiting parks in the evening (Loewen et al., 1993). In dense urban contexts, natural surveillance, the ability for passers-by and neighbors to observe park activity, plays a key role in users’ perceived security and willingness to linger (Jacobs, 1961). For parks in the Palestinian context, where public space is already constrained and security concerns are layered with political dimensions, the design of safe and welcoming environments takes on additional significance.

Accessibility is equally central to understanding park use and perception, yet it is a concept that encompasses several distinct dimensions (Talen and Anselin, 1998). Physical and spatial accessibility refers to proximity and the quality of pedestrian routes connecting residents to parks, including the safety of crossings, continuity of footpaths, and absence of barriers such as busy roads (Forsyth and Musacchio, 2005). Functional accessibility concerns the design of the park itself: whether paths are wheelchair-accessible, whether seating includes back and arm support for elderly users, and whether equipment serves users of all ages and abilities. Psychological accessibility relates to whether all groups, across gender, age, and cultural background, feel welcome and comfortable within the space. Finally, economic and regulatory accessibility refers to the role of entrance fees, opening hours, and behavioral rules in either facilitating or restricting use by different population groups (Wolch et al., 2014). Research consistently shows that parks with low or no entry fees, flexible informal use rules, and culturally responsive design attract more diverse and frequent visitors (Peters et al., 2010). In the context of Ramallah and Al-Bireh, understanding which of these dimensions most directly shapes residents’ perceptions and usage patterns is central to the present study’s analytical contribution.

2.7 Palestinian urban context and research objectives

The Palestinian urban context presents a distinctive set of conditions that shape how residents relate to public parks. Decades of political conflict, restricted movement, limited municipal resources, and rapid unplanned urbanization have significantly constrained the availability and quality of public green space in Palestinian cities (UN-Habitat, 2015; Shlay and Rosen, 2015). In cities such as Ramallah and Al-Bireh, parks must function not only as recreational amenities but as compensatory social and psychological infrastructure in an environment where private space is often crowded and outdoor mobility is constrained (Weinthal and Sowers, 2019). Despite these challenges, empirical research on how Palestinian residents perceive, use, and attach meaning to their local parks remains limited. This study addresses that gap by focusing on two of the most prominent municipal parks in the West Bank, with the aim of generating locally grounded evidence that can inform planning and design decisions. The study is guided by three research questions: (1) How do residents perceive and use Ramallah Municipality Park and Al-Bireh Municipality Park? (2) How do accessibility, safety, design, and municipal policies shape residents’ sense of place and belonging? (3) How and why do these perceptions differ between the two parks? Throughout this study, the term “public parks” refers specifically to formally designated, municipally managed open spaces, and is used consistently to distinguish the study’s focus from broader open space typologies such as plazas, informal lots, or green corridors.

This study draws on the 15-minute city framework as an evaluative lens to assess how well public parks in Ramallah and Al-Bireh are integrated into residents’ everyday urban lives, rather than prescribing or enforcing the model in these contexts. The 15-minute city concept was developed by urban planning scholar Carlos Moreno and colleagues, and gained prominent policy traction after its adoption by Paris Mayor Anne Hidalgo as a guiding principle for the city’s post-pandemic urban strategy (Moreno et al., 2021). Rooted in the tradition of chrono-urbanism, the framework proposes that city residents should have access to all essential daily services, including work, education, healthcare, commerce, and recreation, within a 15-minute walk or bicycle ride from their home. It represents a broader reconceptualization of urban planning away from car-dependent zoning and towards proximity, mixed use, and human-scale neighborhoods (Balletto et al., 2021). The relevance of this framework to the present study is specific and targeted: the 15-minute city foregrounds walkable access to parks and public green spaces as a central component of a liveable urban environment. This directly connects to this study’s concern with how accessible, proximate, and socially embedded Ramallah and Al-Bireh’s municipal parks are for their surrounding communities. The framework is not applied wholesale, but is used to draw attention to the spatial dimensions of park accessibility and to provide comparative context for interpreting residents’ perceptions. Where the 15-minute city framework has been implemented internationally, documented outcomes include strengthened social cohesion, improved physical and mental wellbeing, reduced car dependency, and improved air quality (Moreno et al., 2021; Balletto et al., 2021). However, its application must be calibrated to local conditions: in the Palestinian context, political constraints, fragmented land ownership, and limited municipal capacity present distinct challenges that shape what is achievable. The framework therefore informs the analytical frame of this study but is engaged critically, not adopted uncritically as a universal prescription (Moreno et al., 2021). 3 Methodology

This study adopts a qualitative research design to explore how local residents perceive and experience public parks in urban Palestinian contexts, specifically in the cities of Ramallah and Al-Bireh. A qualitative approach was chosen because it allows for a deeper, more nuanced understanding of people's lived experiences, attitudes, and emotional connections to open spaces, which are elements that are often difficult to capture through quantitative methods alone. Given the cultural, social, and spatial complexities of Palestinian urban environments, qualitative research provides the flexibility and depth needed to uncover the diverse ways in which public parks are valued, used, and critiqued by local communities.

These parks were purposefully selected due to their central location, accessibility, and social significance within their respective cities. Both serve as prominent green spaces offering playgrounds, walking paths, seating areas, and spaces for community events. Their active use and visibility make them ideal case studies for examining how urban Palestinians engage with public open spaces. Ramallah and Al-Bireh were selected as the focus of this study because they represent two of the most rapidly urbanizing and socially dynamic cities in Palestine, where pressures on public space are both visible and consequential. As administrative, economic, and cultural hubs, these cities have experienced substantial population growth, expansion of built-up areas, and increasing demand for recreational and social infrastructure. At the same time, their dense urban fabric, limited land availability, and political constraints have intensified the challenges of providing accessible and well-maintained parks for local residents. Studying these two neighboring cities offers a unique opportunity to examine how communities with similar socio-cultural contexts, yet differing municipal policies and spatial conditions, perceive and interact with their parks. Their prominence, diversity, and ongoing urban transformation make Ramallah and Al-Bireh ideal case studies for understanding broader patterns of public space use within Palestinian urban environments, while also generating insights directly relevant to future planning and design interventions.

The Ramallah Municipality Park is embedded within a dense urban fabric composed primarily of mixed-use and institutional buildings. The aerial image shows a highly structured park layout, characterized by formal pathways radiating around a central water feature. The circular fountain at the core acts as the park’s focal point, emphasizing a geometric design language rooted in symmetry and visual hierarchy. Vegetation is relatively scattered but deliberately placed, with clusters of trees framing circulation paths rather than forming continuous canopy cover. The park is enclosed by busy multi-lane roads and roundabout intersections, making it visually prominent yet somewhat isolated by traffic. The proximity to municipal and commercial buildings suggests its use is shaped by administrative workers, visitors, and nearby residents.

In contrast, the Al-Bireh Municipality Park exhibits a more organic and less formal landscape configuration. The aerial photograph reveals larger and more dispersed green patches, with vegetation forming broader shaded zones and a more naturalistic layout. The pathways appear less rigid than in Ramallah, integrating more fluidly with tree clusters and open areas. Built structures including playgrounds, community facilities, and seating environments are distributed more evenly across the site, enabling a wider variety of informal uses. Surrounding land uses include residential blocks and municipal buildings, but the street edges appear less congested than those in Ramallah. This suggests easier pedestrian access and a more relaxed relationship with the broader neighborhood.

3.2 Data collection

To gather detailed insights, the study used semi-structured interviews as its primary qualitative data collection tool, supplemented by direct researcher observations during site visits. These methods enable participants to express their views in their own words, allowing the researcher to probe deeper into specific themes while adapting to the natural flow of conversation. Semi-structured interviews were used to explore personal experiences, patterns of use, and individual perceptions of the parks, while observations provided contextual evidence on spatial conditions, behavioral patterns, and social dynamics. A total of 24 participants were interviewed across both parks (12 per park), a sample size determined through purposive selection guided by the principle of informational adequacy: data collection continued until no new themes were emerging within each proximity category, suggesting that thematic saturation had been reached for the purposes of this exploratory study.

Participants were recruited through on-site approach during fieldwork visits; researchers introduced themselves, briefly explained the purpose of the study, and invited willing individuals to participate. No prior advertising or formal recruitment channels were used. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18 or above; (2) current residence or regular presence in Ramallah or Al-Bireh; and (3) familiarity with the park under study, either as a user, a neighbor, or a nearby community member. Individuals who declined to participate or who did not meet these criteria were excluded. Each interview lasted between 20 and 40 min and was conducted in Arabic, with key excerpts translated into English for analysis. Fieldwork was carried out during May and June 2025, encompassing both weekday and weekend visits across different times of day to capture variation in park use patterns. Interview questions were organized around the following thematic areas: (1) frequency and patterns of park use; (2) overall satisfaction and perceived quality of the park; (3) perceptions of safety, including specific spatial features; (4) accessibility and distance from home; (5) the influence of rules and fees on visit decisions; (6) cultural practices and social interactions within the park; and (7) sense of belonging and emotional attachment to the space.

The study uses a purposive sampling strategy, designed to include participants from a range of demographic backgrounds and spatial proximities to the parks. This strategy ensures that a variety of voices and experiences are represented, particularly those that may differ based on distance from the park. In selecting participants, researchers sought to achieve a balanced representation across the following characteristics: gender (male and female); age group (youth under 25, adults 25–60, and older adults above 60); and spatial proximity (as described in the three categories below). Socioeconomic background was not explicitly screened for, as it was not directly observable in the field, but the diversity of neighboring residential areas provided a degree of socioeconomic variation within the sample. The sample is divided into three categories:

  • On-site users: Individuals who were present in the park during the time of the study. These participants provide first-hand accounts of park use and immediate satisfaction or concerns.

  • Immediate neighbors (within 50 m): Residents living directly next to the parks. Their perspective is important in assessing issues such as noise, crowding, and daily visibility of the park.

  • Nearby community members (within 500 m): Residents who are within walking distance but not directly adjacent. This group offers insight into accessibility, frequency of visits, and how public parks integrate into the broader neighborhood content

This triangulated sampling structure allows the research to compare how proximity influences perceptions, usage frequency, and expectations. For example, while on-site users may emphasize park services and comfort, nearby residents might focus more on accessibility or safety. By capturing these different layers of experience, the study provides a more comprehensive understanding of the role of public parks in everyday urban life.

These observations were systematically documented through behavioral mapping conducted across multiple site visits, producing four comparative analytical diagrams (Figures 3–6) that illustrate key differences in how the two parks are used, structured, and experienced by their communities.

4 Analysis and results

4.1 Comparative analysis

A comparison of the two parks reveals distinct spatial structures that reflect different design philosophies and ultimately shape user perception. Ramallah Municipality Park follows a formal and highly structured geometric design, with movement organized around a central fountain that acts as the primary visual anchor. In contrast, Al-Bireh Municipality Park adopts a more organic layout, characterized by dispersed vegetation, fluid pathways, and spatial pockets that support a wider range of informal social activities. These contrasting design languages influence the comfort, movement patterns, and overall atmosphere experienced by visitors. One resident living near Ramallah Park described the experience as follows: "It feels like a place you pass through, not a place you stay in. Everything is arranged around the fountain and there is not much room to just sit and be comfortable." In Al-Bireh, by contrast, participants more frequently described the park as feeling open and adaptable, with one visitor noting: "We come here with the children and we can find a corner that suits us. It does not feel like there are rules about where you have to be."

The integration of each park with its surrounding urban fabric also contributes to differences in accessibility and familiarity. Ramallah Park is bounded by several busy roads and roundabouts, making it visually prominent but physically separated from the immediate neighborhood by traffic flows. This separation affects pedestrian access and contributes to the perceptions of reduced safety, particularly during evening hours. Meanwhile, Al-Bireh Park is surrounded by calmer residential and civic streets, enabling smoother pedestrian entry and fostering a stronger everyday connection between the park and its adjacent communities.

Vegetation patterns further distinguish the two parks in ways that align with residents’ expressed preferences. Al-Bireh Park exhibits broader and more continuous tree canopy coverage, creating shaded, thermally comfortable microclimates. Ramallah Park, by contrast, provides more ornamental planting concentrated along pathways and around the central feature, resulting in fewer shaded areas and a more formal aesthetic. Multiple interviewees specifically referenced shade and thermal comfort as factors influencing how long they stayed and whether they brought children or elderly family members. One participant, a mother of two young children, put it plainly: "In summer we only go to Al-Bireh because there is shade. In Ramallah there is nowhere to sit with the children without being in the sun." An older male resident echoed this concern: "I used to come to Ramallah Park in the morning, but when the sun comes up there is no place to rest properly. The trees are not enough." These accounts suggest a perceived link between vegetation density and the comfort of users who require longer rest periods or shade, though this relationship would benefit from further systematic investigation.

Finally, the distribution of amenities and the management rules of each park shape how communities interact with these spaces. Ramallah Park organizes most activities around centralized areas, which can limit functional diversity and may conflict with local social habits such as bringing food or gathering informally, due to stricter park regulations. Al-Bireh Park, in contrast, distributes facilities more evenly across the landscape and allows more flexible forms of use, such as picnicking and informal gatherings. This flexibility was repeatedly cited by interviewees as a reason they felt more comfortable and at ease in Al-Bireh Park, suggesting a direct connection between regulatory permissiveness and residents’ sense of belonging. Taken together, these spatial, cultural, and managerial differences point towards a consistent pattern in the interview and observation data: parks that accommodate local behavioral norms, through flexible policies, familiar spatial configurations, and inclusive management, are perceived as more accessible and welcoming. This pattern forms the empirical basis for this study’s argument that park design and governance must be responsive to the cultural expectations of local communities. However, the we acknowledge that these conclusions are drawn from a qualitative, context-specific dataset and should be substantiated in future work through broader surveys, spatial analysis, and participatory design processes.

4.3 Accessibility and proximity: findings

Accessibility and proximity emerged as a central concern among participants across both parks. Interviews revealed that for many residents, the distance to their nearest park significantly affects whether it features in their daily or weekly routine. Several participants living beyond a 10–15 min walking distance indicated that they visited infrequently or only for special occasions. One resident from a neighborhood without a nearby park explained: "We want to come more often but it takes us twenty minutes by car. You cannot just walk over with the children on a normal day." While international benchmarks such as the WHO-referenced 300-metre proximity guideline or European standards of 5-15 min’ walk offer useful comparative reference points, these must be understood as contextual ideals rather than universal requirements. In the specific urban conditions of Ramallah and Al-Bireh, where land scarcity, political constraints, and fragmented infrastructure present distinct challenges, what constitutes reasonable access must be assessed in relation to local realities. Spatially, the provision of parks is uneven across neighborhoods, and some residents reported that no public park was within comfortable walking distance, necessitating reliance on private transport or discouraging visits altogether. Mapping the actual distribution and service catchment areas of parks across both municipalities would provide the empirical spatial evidence needed to substantiate these patterns quantitatively; this is acknowledged as a limitation of the present study and a direction for future research. It is also important to clarify that accessibility in this study encompasses multiple dimensions: physical and spatial accessibility (proximity, walkability, connectivity, and transport options); functional accessibility (inclusive design, universal path surfaces, and amenity provision); psychological accessibility (feelings of safety, welcome, and comfort); and economic and regulatory accessibility (entry fees, opening hours, and use rules). The interview data and observations speak most directly to the functional, psychological, and regulatory dimensions, and findings are organized accordingly in the discussion that follows.

4.4 Safe design and safety perception

Safety emerged as a recurring and significant theme across interviews in both parks. Participants consistently linked their sense of security, or the lack thereof, to specific physical features of the park environment. In Ramallah Municipality Park, the secondary entrance was repeatedly cited as a safety concern: it is poorly lit, lacks fencing, and is set to the side of the main circulation path, creating a perceived "blind spot" that participants described as uncomfortable, particularly at night. Several participants, especially women and parents, stated that this feature deterred them from visiting the park after dark. One female participant living nearby was direct: "After eight in the evening I would not go through that entrance alone. There are no lights and you cannot see who is there." In Al-Bireh Municipality Park, nighttime safety concerns were concentrated in the larger open areas, and most prominently around the "safsaf" area, where steep natural stone edges without seating or protective barriers were identified as a physical hazard. Participants reported feeling uneasy near these edges, particularly when visiting with children or elderly family members. One grandfather described his concern: "The edges near the safsaf trees are dangerous. There is nothing to hold on to and the stone drops down sharply. I keep my grandchildren away from that side." More broadly, both parks suffer from insufficient lighting in secondary zones, which reduces the perceived safety and comfort of evening visits. These findings are consistent with the literature on the multidimensional nature of safety perception in public spaces (Mehta, 2014; Loewen et al., 1993): perceived unsafety is not merely a response to actual crime risk but is shaped by environmental cues such as poor visibility, hidden corners, and unmaintained infrastructure.

4.5 Social values, belonging, and municipal policies: findings

Municipal policies and social values are deeply intertwined in shaping residents’ sense of belonging in public parks. A park becomes familiar and meaningful to its community not simply through physical proximity, but through the degree to which it reflects and accommodates local social practices. One of the most direct policy levers in this regard is the fee structure. Where entry fees are set at levels that feel disproportionate to residents’ expectations or economic circumstances, park use declines and the space loses its character as a genuinely public resource. In the case of Ramallah Municipality Park, participants described the fee arrangements as a deterrent that reduced spontaneous or habitual visits, particularly for families and young people. One young man who lived close to Ramallah Park explained: "I pass by it almost every day but I do not go in regularly because of the entrance fee. It adds up over a month, especially for a family." Al-Bireh Municipality Park, which operates with more accessible pricing, was observed to attract more diverse and frequent visitors, with one participant noting: "Here it is easy, you can come with the family without thinking too much about the cost. It feels like it belongs to us." Beyond fees, the behavioral rules governing park use play a significant role in either strengthening or undermining residents’ sense of familiarity. In Ramallah Park, participants noted that the prohibition on bringing outside food, while understandable from a commercial management perspective, conflicted with culturally embedded habits of sharing meals outdoors and gathering informally with food as a social centrepiece. As one mother put it: "We want to bring a meal and sit together like a family outing, but you cannot do that in Ramallah Park. So we just go to Al-Bireh instead." In Al-Bireh Park, where outside food is permitted, interviewees described scenes of family picnics, shared meals, and intergenerational gatherings that reinforced their emotional connection to the space. These differences illustrate a broader principle that when park rules align with local cultural practices, they support the formation of place memories and a sense of ownership over the space; when they conflict with such practices, they create a sense of exclusion. Municipalities seeking to foster active, lively, and socially cohesive parks should therefore carefully evaluate which rules serve genuine management or safety purposes, and which inadvertently erode the cultural familiarity that makes parks meaningful to their communities.

4.6 Planning Implications

Based on the findings presented in Sections 4.1–4.5, the following planning recommendations are offered for municipalities and practitioners working on public parks in Ramallah, Al-Bireh, and comparable Palestinian urban contexts. These recommendations are grounded in the empirical evidence collected in this study and are informed by the theoretical frameworks discussed in the literature review. First, accessibility must be embedded in the master planning of both cities. Participants’ accounts highlight that distance, traffic barriers, and the absence of parks in some neighborhoods are genuine constraints on use. Municipalities should prioritize distributing green spaces so that a greater proportion of residents can reach a park within a reasonable walking distance, using internationally recognized benchmarks as a reference point while adapting them to local conditions. Future spatial analyses should map park catchment areas against neighborhood population densities to identify under-served communities. Second, targeted safety design improvements should be prioritized in both parks. In Ramallah, this means improving lighting and visibility at the secondary entrance. In Al-Bireh, it means redesigning the “safsaf” area edges to eliminate steep, unguarded drops, adding appropriately supported seating, and extending lighting across larger open sections. These are relatively low-cost interventions that can have a substantial impact on perceived safety and willingness to visit at different times of day (Mehta, 2014). Third, municipalities should critically review their fee structures and behavioral regulations. Entry fees should reflect the park’s character as a public resource. Rules that prohibit culturally embedded practices, such as bringing outside food, should be reconsidered, as the evidence from this study suggests they reduce residents’ sense of belonging without demonstrably improving park quality. Empowering local communities to participate in the governance and programming of park spaces would further strengthen residents’ sense of ownership and emotional attachment.

4.7 Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The sample, while purposively constructed to represent diverse proximity groups, genders, and age categories, is modest in scale (24 participants across two parks), and the findings cannot be generalized beyond the specific contexts studied. The absence of spatial mapping data, such as city-scale park distribution maps or catchment area analyses, limits the ability to make empirically grounded claims about proximity and access. Interview quotations have been used as placeholder markers in this version of the manuscript and require fuller integration in the final revision to substantiate the analytical claims made. Additionally, socioeconomic background was not formally screened for in participant selection, which limits the study’s ability to examine class-based differences in park perception. Future research should address these gaps through: (a) participatory mapping exercises to document the spatial distribution of parks relative to neighborhood populations; (b) larger-scale surveys to test the patterns identified here across broader samples; (c) longitudinal studies to examine how perceptions change in response to policy or design interventions; and (d) comparative studies across additional Palestinian cities and municipalities to assess the generalizability of these findings.

4.8 Global vs. local perception: a dual-path model of sense of place

Understanding how communities perceive public parks requires acknowledging that people draw on two interconnected layers of meaning: global influences and local lived experiences. Figure 7 introduces a conceptual model that illustrates how these two pathways shape the perception of public parks in Ramallah and Al-Bireh. Although both sets of influences contribute to an individual’s sense of place, they operate through different mechanisms and produce distinct emotional and functional outcomes.

Figure 7

The global pathway reflects universal factors commonly identified in international literature on urban green spaces. These include people’s general goals when visiting parks such as recreation, relaxation, or exposure to nature, as well as the functional benefits parks provide. As individuals begin to rely on a specific park for activities they value, they develop functional attachment and, eventually, place dependence, where the park becomes difficult to replace in their daily routines. This pathway results in a broader, globalized sense of place shaped by shared human needs and general expectations of what a park should offer.

Conversely, the local pathway is grounded in contextual, cultural, and social meanings specific to Palestinian communities. Residents evaluate parks not only as functional spaces but as environments that reflect their values, beliefs, and everyday practices. These local influences generate emotional attachment, particularly when parks serve as extensions of neighborhood life or as settings where families gather and create memories. Over time, these emotional ties evolve into place identity, where the park becomes an integral part of how individuals and communities define themselves and their environment. This locally rooted pathway produces a sense of place that is culturally embedded and strongly dependent on community familiarity and inclusivity.

At the center of both pathways lies the concept of perception, which serves as the intersection where global expectations meet local realities. The way residents evaluate safety, comfort, accessibility, and social belonging in their parks emerges from the combination of these two layers. For example, a park may offer the universal amenities expected globally, but if it conflicts with local cultural practices or imposes unfamiliar social rules, the resulting perception may be negative despite its functional quality. Conversely, parks that align with local social life, even if they lack sophisticated facilities, can foster strong emotional attachment and positive perception.

This dual-path model underscores that designing successful parks in Ramallah and Al-Bireh requires balancing global design principles with local cultural needs. Planners must consider universal aspects such as accessibility, safety, and multifunctionality while also supporting uses that resonate with residents’ values, traditions, and informal social habits.

5 Conclusion

This study set out to examine how residents of Ramallah and Al-Bireh perceive their local municipal parks, and to understand what spatial, social, and policy-related factors shape those perceptions. Through semi-structured interviews with 24 participants across three proximity groups, supplemented by direct observation, the research reveals consistent patterns in how park design, management, and cultural compatibility influence residents’ sense of place and belonging. Participants’ accounts make clear that public parks in these two cities are not merely green pockets within dense urban fabric; they are deeply social, cultural, and psychological landscapes. The comparison between Ramallah Municipality Park and Al-Bireh Municipality Park shows that differences in spatial configuration, vegetation, safety infrastructure, and municipal policy produce meaningfully different user experiences. Al-Bireh Park’s more organic layout, continuous canopy shade, accessible pricing, and permissive use rules were repeatedly associated by participants with feelings of ease, familiarity, and belonging. In contrast, Ramallah Park’s formal design, busier road edges, stricter food restrictions, and more centralized activity zones were associated with feelings of distance and reduced comfort, particularly in the evenings and for families. These findings are consistent with the dual-path model of sense of place introduced in this study, which proposes that perceptions emerge from the interaction between globally recognisable park functions and locally specific cultural meanings.

Based on the evidence collected, the study points to three interconnected design and planning imperatives for municipalities in Palestine. First, accessibility, in its physical, functional, psychological, and regulatory dimensions, must be treated as a core planning priority rather than an afterthought. Interview data highlighted that distance, traffic barriers, and cost all limit who can meaningfully access public parks. Second, safety design improvements, including better lighting at secondary entrances, redesigned edges (particularly at the Al-Bireh “safsaf” area), and clearer pathway visibility, are essential to extending usable park hours and increasing comfort for all users. Third, and perhaps most critically, municipal regulations must be evaluated not only for their operational rationale but for their cultural consequences: rules that inadvertently restrict familiar social practices tend to erode the very sense of belonging that makes parks worth visiting. The study acknowledges important limitations: the qualitative sample, while purposively constructed, is modest in scale; maps and spatial data on park distribution were not available at the time of writing; and interview quotations require fuller integration into the analysis in future revisions. These limitations point towards a clear agenda for follow-up research, including participatory mapping, larger-scale surveys, and comparative studies across additional Palestinian cities. Nevertheless, the findings offer grounded, contextually rooted evidence that integrating culturally responsive design with globally informed planning principles is essential for creating parks that are genuinely inclusive, lively, and meaningful for Palestinian communities.
