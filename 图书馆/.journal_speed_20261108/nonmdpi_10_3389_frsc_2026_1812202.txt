School of Architecture and Urbanism, University of São Paulo, São Paulo, SP, Brazil See more Article metrics View details Abstract As climate change impacts intensify, urban green infrastructure (UGI) becomes essential for city adaptation. Urban green squares, among UGI types, are easy to implement and integrate into neighborhoods, making them vital for urban resilience. Although their cooling benefits are well established for current conditions, future microclimatic assessments are scarce, creating a critical research gap. This gap is significant because ongoing greening practices coupled with rising tree mortality rates are expected to degrade the microclimate and reduce human thermal comfort in these spaces. This study evaluates the impact of urban green squares on human thermal comfort, measured by Physiological Equivalent Temperature (PET), under future climate change scenarios considering projected tree mortality. We focus on two urban squares in Munich, Germany, using micrometeorological simulations with the ENVI-met model to assess the effects of urban design and tree-planting strategies. Our simulations suggest that by 2050, intense and extreme heat stress will cover up to 96% of the squares’ area due to climate change and tree loss, compared to about 75% today. Analysis of different tree replacement scenarios highlights potential heat mitigation strategies that urban planners could implement progressively. Recommendations emphasize customized approaches for each square, diversification of tree species, and planting of climate-adapted trees to improve survival rates. However, devising effective tree replacement plans that prioritize vital trees remains contentious and calls for further investigation. This study advances understanding of future urban microclimates and provides actionable insights for sustainable urban green space design amid climate change. 1 Introduction Global climate change is not just a problem for the future; it is a challenge we are currently facing (NASA Science, 2022). Recent years have seen a rise in global temperatures (Rohde, 2025), and the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) reports that populations and ecosystems particularly vulnerable to the negative effects of climate change are already encountering significant difficulties in their efforts to adapt. Green areas can play a crucial role in urban adaptation to climate change, reducing urban heat, managing stormwater, and enhancing both physical and social resilience in cities (Zölch et al., 2019; Peinhardt, 2021; Battista et al., 2023; Damyanovic et al., 2024; Mandić et al., 2024). Within this context, urban green infrastructure (UGI) and its ecosystem services—such as cooling through vegetation and improved stormwater management—have become central elements of municipal adaptation strategies (Pauleit et al., 2017; Qi et al., 2020; Azmeer et al., 2024). As a comprehensive landscape-based approach, it can encompass all types of urban green spaces, independent of origin or ownership. Among these, urban squares can serve as important green “oases” embedded in highly built-up areas, typically requiring smaller land take and lower investment than parks and thus being easier to integrate into the existing urban fabric. Urban squares have been shown to substantially contribute to outdoor thermal comfort, acting as cool islands in compact city areas. Recent empirical work highlights how carefully designed squares with appropriate planting schemes can improve local microclimates and lower heat stress for surrounding residents (Nazish et al., 2024). Their results suggest that increasing vegetated surfaces and optimising both the type and spatial arrangement of vegetation can significantly enhance the thermal performance of squares and support mitigation of the urban heat island effect (ÖZEREN and TUNA KAYILI, 2021; Ahmed et al., 2024; Stark da Silva et al., 2024; Lopes et al., 2025). Lai et al. (2019) review a range of measures to improve thermal conditions in urban open spaces, including squares, and report that combinations of vegetation, geometric modifications, and cool materials can reduce air temperatures by around 2 °C on summer days, thereby strengthening the cool island effect. In line with this evidence, Cheaz (2024) documents an experimental square, where introducing vegetation and shading structures led to marked decreases in surface and air temperatures as well as mean radiant temperature, underscoring that, if designed with cooling as a central objective, urban squares can function effectively as urban cool islands. Zhang et al. (2022) reviewed vegetation-related parameters in urban thermal environment studies and classified the main indicators influencing outdoor thermal comfort into four groups: normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), vegetation cover, groups of trees (including tree cover and planting patterns), and individual trees. They found that large-scale studies more often used broad indicators such as NDVI and vegetation cover, whereas micro-scale studies tended to employ more detailed parameters, such as crown size (Zhang et al., 2022). Tree cover emerges repeatedly in studies as a vital factor for enhancing summer thermal comfort in cities, including urban squares (Rahman et al., 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020b, 2021; Moser-Reischl et al., 2019; Zölch et al., 2019; Stark da Silva et al., 2024). This growing focus from scientists and governments on addressing the harmful effects of global warming and rising urban temperatures has led to increased efforts to integrate existing knowledge into practical rules and design guidelines for policymakers, practitioners, and stakeholders (Zardo et al., 2017; Bartesaghi Koc et al., 2018). This study employs the Physiological Equivalent Temperature (PET) index (Höppe and Mayer, 1987), which assesses outdoor thermal conditions by integrating the human heat balance with the principal meteorological drivers of thermal perception, including mean radiant temperature (MRT), air temperature, humidity, and wind speed (Höppe, 1999). PET is widely used in urban climate research because it translates complex atmospheric conditions into a physiologically meaningful measure of heat stress. While other indices, such as the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI), are also valuable for assessing outdoor thermal comfort and similarly rely on mean radiant temperature, PET was selected for this study due to the greater sophistication of its thermo-physiological calculations. Unlike UTCI, which utilizes a simplified regression model, PET comprehensively solves the human heat balance equation. This level of detail makes PET particularly well-suited for capturing the complex, high-resolution microclimatic interactions and localized shading effects of urban tree canopies simulated in ENVI-met. Research conducted in Germany highlights the significant impact of tree cover on PET, demonstrating that trees can reduce local temperatures by up to 13 °C compared to areas that are fully exposed to sunlight (Rahman et al., 2021). It was also observed that in urban environments, shade provided by trees is more effective for cooling pedestrian areas than the process of transpiration alone (Pattnaik et al., 2024). Additionally, having 30–40% greenspace coverage can reduce extreme heat stress by half, with minimal effects on winter cold stress (Rahman et al., 2022). However, it’s important to note that the positive effects of trees on human thermal comfort and the ecosystem services they provide depend on their overall health (Liang and Huang, 2023; Torres et al., 2024). For instance, evaluating tree vitality is primarily based on the extent of their leaf coverage. Trees experiencing chronic stress tend to have smaller leaves and a reduced Leaf Area Index (LAI) (Pattnaik et al., 2024), which consequently diminishes their capacity to intercept radiation, thereby reducing their cooling potential. Tree vitality is recorded in urban tree inventories, which are essential tools for decision-making and knowledge development. These inventories facilitate tasks such as mapping storm-damaged trees and identifying the most affected species and sizes (Jim and Liu, 1997). To assist with assessing tree vitality during these inventories in Germany, the German Conference of Horticultural Officers (GALK) has developed a guide of recommendations for evaluating trees in urban areas (GALK, 2025). Urban trees often suffer water stress from compacted soils and limited planting pits, conditions worsened by climate change. Increased pest pressure and extreme weather further weaken tree vitality, leading to higher mortality in hydrologically disadvantaged sites (Ordóñez and Duinker, 2014; Esperon-Rodriguez et al., 2022; Xing et al., 2025). In their literature review on urban tree mortality, Hilbert et al. (2019) found that annual mortality rates varied significantly. For cohort studies, the rate ranged from 0.6 to 68.5%, while for repeated inventories of uneven-aged trees, the rate ranged from 0 to 30%. One factor that increases the danger of tree mortality is the low diversity of a tree species population, which increases the vulnerability of the urban forest, the entire urban stock of trees, to pests, deteriorating the vitality of the trees (Johnson et al., 1999; Millward and Sabir, 2010). This can be attributed to the fact that when a limited number of species prevail, the potential for widespread losses is amplified, as a single pest or disease affecting one of these species can have far-reaching ramifications (Kendal et al., 2014; Paquette et al., 2021). Conversely, a high species-diversity functions as a stabilizing factor, curtailing the propagation and ramifications of threats that target specific species while concomitantly augmenting the collective resilience of the urban forest against biotic (e.g., pests and diseases) and abiotic disturbances (e.g., climate change and extreme weather conditions) (Rogers et al., 2023; University of Minnesota, 2025). Current urban tree replacement practices focus on economic assessments, strategic planning, and collective management to encourage sustainable forest renewal (Scott and Betters, 2000). One prevalent issue in existing tree replacement decisions is the low species diversity resulting from supply chain constraints and planting practices. This problem necessitates research and targeted actions to develop diverse and resilient urban forests (Hilbert et al., 2023). To tackle this issue, tree replacement approaches have evolved from reactive strategies to coordinated strategies that prioritize species selection, planned replacements, and the use of tree databases (Parker et al., 2004). Effective urban forest management requires a stronger emphasis on promoting tree longevity, enhancing seedling growth, and accelerating the establishment of habitats that support biodiversity (Le Roux et al., 2014). Furthermore, historic cities like Rome have employed master plans that integrate historical and spatial considerations for urban tree stock renewal that can serve as inspiration (Clemente et al., 2025). Large, healthy trees play a vital role in providing ecosystem services. However, challenges such as limited budgets and poor choices of species and planting locations demand proactive management, legal protection, and community involvement to ensure the long-term benefits of urban trees (Hand and Doick, 2019; Hand and Doick, 2019). The economic principle of limited resources typically constrains the allocation of financial resources for maintaining municipal trees. Budgets for tree maintenance are frequently considered less important than those allocated to other municipal services (Vogt et al., 2015). As a result, there is an increased risk of elevated tree mortality rates. Consequently, studies have examined the repercussions of inadequate maintenance policies (Vogt et al., 2015), alongside establishing guidelines that emphasize the necessity for investment in tree maintenance (Millward and Sabir, 2010; Poschenrieder et al., 2022; Suchocka et al., 2023). Many sources highlight the importance of microclimate modeling and adaptive design for maintaining the comfort and functionality of urban squares, especially by using greenery. This is particularly relevant in the context of climate change (Ascione et al., 2024; Yamasaki et al., 2024). However, there is a significant knowledge gap regarding the effectiveness of these strategies under future climate conditions (Castelo et al., 2023; Wannewitz et al., 2024). This concern is amplified by the anticipated decline in human thermal comfort as climate change negatively affects tree health and increases urban heat stress. Additionally, many studies emphasize the need to integrate climate-sensitive tree mortality projections into city climate adaptation plans (Roman et al., 2014; Brandt et al., 2021). Doing so can help cities sustain viable urban forests that mitigate heat and enhance human wellbeing. However, many urban adaptation plans still fall short in addressing tree mortality and its associated vulnerabilities. This underscores the necessity for improved monitoring and greater stakeholder engagement in the planning processes. Besides that, while microclimate models are valuable for understanding potential cooling effects of urban trees, their predictions regarding tree replacement strategies are constrained by incomplete parameterization of biophysical processes, species-specific responses, mortality dynamics, spatial heterogeneity, and urban complexity. This study explores the relationship between urban design and tree mortality on human thermal comfort in two urban squares in Munich. Hohenzollernplatz and Bordeauxplatz, both located in densely populated neighborhoods of Munich, have been selected as study sites. The primary objective of this study is to ascertain how these squares may respond to future climate change scenarios and the anticipated loss of trees. This objective can be subdivided into two parts: firstly, how these squares might react to future climate change scenarios, and secondly, how the anticipated loss of trees influences or exacerbates the expected decline in thermal comfort. To achieve this objective, micrometeorological simulations were developed and conducted using the ENVI-met model (Bruse and Fleer, 1998), allowing to analyze the different scenarios. By comprehending the expected performance of the squares under these future conditions, the study endeavors to identify strategies that could be implemented over time to prevent the anticipated decline in PET in the future. 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Study design The present study was conducted in Munich, Germany. The city is situated in the southern part of the country (48° 8′23″N, 11° 34′28″E, altitude 519 m above sea level). Munich’s climate is classified as Cfb category in the Köppen–Geiger classification, with hot summers, no dry seasons, and increased rainfall in summer. The city of Munich, with a population of 1.6 million (Landeshauptstadt München, n.d.), is Germany’s third most populous city. The average global temperature increased by 1.0 °C between 1881 and 2021. However, Germany has experienced a more pronounced increase of 1.6 °C during the same period. The frequency of days with maximum temperatures reaching 30 °C has increased threefold, while the frequency of days where the air temperature does not exceed 0 °C has decreased from 28 to 19 days per year over the past 75 years (Deutscher Wetterdienst, n.d.). The challenge is to determine how PET can be addressed in a future climate, considering not only the predicted effects of climate change on air temperature and humidity but also its effects on existing vegetation. Two urban squares were selected for analysis to determine the impact of tree replacement – resulting from tree mortality – on PET in the future (Figure 1). The squares were chosen due to their urban morphology. Both Hohenzollernplatz (10,150 m2) and Bordeauxplatz (14,018 m2) are among the largest squares in Munich, and they have a similar number of trees. However, they differ in design: Hohenzollernplatz has a square shape, while Bordeauxplatz is elongated. Additionally, Hohenzollernplatz is predominantly paved, featuring an impervious surface rate of 89%, whereas Bordeauxplatz has a much lower impervious surface rate of only 30%. Another key distinction is their surroundings: Hohenzollernplatz is bordered by buildings on three sides, while Bordeauxplatz is more distanced from structures, with streets acting as its direct boundaries. The data regarding the trees was obtained from the database of the Building Department (Baureferat-Gartenbau) of the Munich City Council. The analysis was conducted through micrometeorological field measurements, followed by modeling the squares and using ENVI-met simulations. A series of measurements were obtained to validate the simulations. To this end, two sets of mobile weather stations (Onset, MA, USA) were utilized, equipped with Globe thermometers and surface temperature sensors mounted on two tripods and iButton Hygrochron temperature/humidity loggers. Measurement campaigns were conducted in August 2020 and December 2021. All sensors, except for the iButtons, were connected to 15-channel HOBO U30 USB weather station data loggers, which recorded five-second averages every 5 min. Due to uncertainty regarding security at weather stations during the night, the data obtained from the mobile weather stations was merged with that provided by the iButton sensors. Validation was based on air temperature (Ta) and relative humidity (RH) variables measured by the iButtons. Due to costs and security issues, MRT could not be measured for validation purposes. However, a recent MRT validation study based on six-directional radiative flux measurements in a very complex environment states a high MRT modelling accuracy of ENVI-met (Schöneberger et al., 2025). The modeled and measured data for Ta and RH fit reasonably well during the summer simulation. However, in the winter simulation, ENVI-met overestimated the air temperature between 11 a.m. and 2 p.m., with differences ranging from 1.35 to 1.64 °C (Figure 2). The relative humidity showed good alignment in both the summer and winter simulations, with no significant deviations noted. Our previous studies have emphasized the crucial role that trees play in PET of the examined squares (Stark da Silva et al., 2023, 2024). However, climate change is widely acknowledged as a significant factor adversely affecting tree health, particularly among native species in temperate climates (Linnakoski et al., 2019; Boonman et al., 2024). As a result, there has been increased discussion regarding which tree species are most suitable for planting to help safeguard urban tree populations in the face of climate challenges (GALK, n.d., 2025). Furthermore, internal guidelines from the Munich Horticulture Department indicate that mature public trees should only be removed if they pose a risk to citizens. Given the significance of trees in the studied squares, the risks they face due to climate change, and the guidelines that recommend they should not be replaced as long as they remain healthy and pose no risk to the community, a decision was made to investigate the past tree mortality rates in Munich. Additionally, the objective is to assess the predicted future performance of these squares to ensure they continue to function as urban cooling oases. Figure 3 Methodology approach. 2.2 Tree mortality scenarios To understand how PET in the study squares will be affected in the future, the number of trees that will survive the climate-change-induced conditions and remain in the future scenario needs to be estimated. The estimation is based on both the replacement guidelines of the Munich Horticulture Department and data on tree loss and replacement from the City of Munich. For this calculation, we focused on trees planted in public open spaces using data collected by the authors from the Munich database. This database contains information on tree locations, species, and ages, covering both street trees and those found in public squares and parks. In recent years, the city of Munich has made significant efforts to enhance the accuracy of its tree loss data, which is maintained in a structured format. However, the dataset only covers a span of five years and exhibits inconsistencies in data collection methods across different years, as well as variations in how lost trees are categorized by their location. As a result, it was necessary to utilize the complete set of tree population data for the inner city area, delineated by the Munich Middle Ring. Despite these limitations, the mortality rates for urban trees calculated in this study align with those observed in other temperate cities (Hilbert et al., 2019). To further address the limitations of the five-year dataset, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by systematically excluding one year at a time to re-estimate the association between climate variables and tree mortality. This analysis revealed moderate to strong correlations between the mortality rate and both the yearly average temperature and the number of hot days, while the correlation with yearly precipitation was weak to moderate. Furthermore, disentangling the specific effects of climate change on increasing tree mortality from standard forest dynamics remains challenging, but the trend of climate-driven mortality is increasingly supported by observations in European forests (Luo and Chen, 2013; Taccoen et al., 2019; Grünig et al., 2026). According to the findings of Hilbert et al., 2019, who conducted a literature review on urban tree mortality, the median values observed were between 2.5 and 2.6%. Where mortality rate was obtained using the formula: 2.3 Climate change scenarios A set of simulation scenarios was developed, incorporating both optimistic and pessimistic scenarios concerning the necessary replacement of trees. The status quo was simulated using data from DWD station 3,379 in Munich City (WST) for July 30, 2020. The future scenario for the year 2050 was obtained through Meteonorm simulation software (Meteonorm Version 8, 2025), taking into account the IPCC RCP4.5 climate change predictions. This represents moderate emission reductions and warming scenarios based on average climate change values. Meteonorm was selected for its ability to provide location-specific current and future Typical Meteorological Years (TMYs) by combining observations from weather stations, satellite data, and reanalysis results, then downscaling this information to a local scale. Its future climate datasets are based explicitly on CMIP6 global climate model ensembles and the IPCC-aligned Shared Socioeconomic Pathways, including the latest versions: SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP3-7.0. (Meteonorm | Climate, 2026). This ensures a direct connection with internationally recognized climate change scenarios. The simulation software has been recognized as a reliable source in various research studies. This process provides essential variables, including shortwave and longwave radiation, which are crucial for conducting an accurate full-forcing simulation using the ENVI-met model (Tsoka et al., 2021; Vilà et al., 2023; Lüttich, 2024). Recent climate analyses have shifted toward IPCC Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP) scenarios (Ai et al., 2024; Norgate et al., 2024). However, the data available by Meteonorm during the simulations conducted in this study is based on the IPCC Representative Concentration Pathway (RCP) 4.5, which does not compromise the credibility of the results. The study examined the mean PET between 10:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m., as well as the percentage of square area falling within the expected range of PET. This timeframe was chosen because it corresponds to the peak values observed during summer field measurements used for simulation validation (see Figure 2), with the highest values recorded at 4:00 p.m. This selection aligns with previous studies conducted under similar climatic and site conditions (Zölch et al., 2019; Erlwein et al., 2021). After 4:00 p.m., there was a significant decrease in PET values due to the pronounced effect of building shade. The study utilizes the status quo in 2020 as a baseline, employing the same scenario with data from 2050. This approach enables understanding how tree replacement rates, depicted as optimistic and pessimistic, influence PET in the examined cases. To this end, simulations were conducted, wherein the number of young trees was augmented to represent the projected replacement rates resulting from future tree mortality. 2.4 Tree’s characteristics in the modeled scenarios As delineated in the previous analysis, tree mortality rates were employed to ascertain the number of mature and young trees in the modeled scenarios. The mature trees used in the modeled scenarios are Tilia cordata, which are provided by the model as a default and are 20 m in height and 13 m in canopy size. The young trees utilized in this study were Acer platanoides, whose characteristics were modified through the Albero tool to 12 m in height and 9 m in canopy size. The dimensions of the young trees were obtained following the guidelines established by the City of Munich for new planting trees. 2.5 Microclimate modeling This study employed the ENVI-met V5.6.1 model to conduct simulations (Bruse and Fleer, 1998). ENVI-met is a microclimate model of high resolution that utilizes fluid mechanics and thermodynamics to simulate the interactions between soil, vegetation, and the atmosphere at the microscale. The model’s capabilities include the calculation of turbulence, air temperature and humidity, radiation fluxes, and pollutant dispersion in three dimensions. The model’s validity for the simulated scenario was verified using field measurements collected during summer and winter, as described by Stark da Silva et al. (2023). Based on the status quo, the squares were modeled at a size of 300 by 300 m, considering their immediate surroundings. Both squares are situated in flat areas and are surrounded by six-story buildings (building height 18 m). The buildings were modeled using the ENVI-met default wall settings with moderate insulation. Based on previous literature (Zölch et al., 2019; Erlwein et al., 2021; Alsaad et al., 2022), a horizontal resolution of 2 by 2 m was implemented to enhance precision, forgoing a nesting grid. Additionally, a 3-m grid was used for vertical resolution, ensuring a more precise representation of the urban landscape. The heights and dimensions of the buildings were obtained from GIS data provided by the city of Munich. The simulations for both squares were initiated at 21:00 on July 30th, 2050, and ran for a total of 30 h, concluding on August 1st, 2050. This timeframe was chosen because the predicted extreme conditions were anticipated for July 31st. The results for PET were extracted at a height of 1.5 m, equivalent to pedestrian level. The meteorological data required for the ENVI-met simulation for the year 2020 was obtained from the weather station with the city station ID 3379 of the DWD (Table 3). Although evidence suggests that wind speed patterns may be affected by climate change in the future, the simulated wind input was not forced to avoid instabilities in the simulation, which may lead to inaccuracies (IPCC, 2023; Martinez and Iglesias, 2024). However, given the utilization of identical inputs in both the present and future simulations, a comparative analysis remains feasible despite the unreliable wind speed. In densely built areas, the greening of squares as cool islands is crucial to achieving this goal; however, it may be compromised by the loss of old trees, as shown in this study. Hohenzollernplatz and Bordeauxplatz are significant parts of Munich’s urban landscape, offering thermal, aesthetic, and cultural benefits that will become increasingly valuable in the face of climate change. Unfortunately, both areas have low species diversity, which makes them more vulnerable to pests and diseases, a known issue with urban forests dominated by a single species (Johnson et al., 1999; Millward and Sabir, 2010). The tree diversity guideline established by Santamour (2004), known as the 10–20–30 rule for species, genus, and family composition, remains a foundational principle. Unfortunately, neither square meets these criteria. A suitable option to increase tree diversity in Munich is following the suggestion of adapting the 10–20-30 rule to local climates and land-use contexts can enhance resilience (Sjöman et al., 2012; Kendal et al., 2014). Preserving all mature trees without planning for their renewal may weaken long-term cooling benefits because as trees age, their canopy vigor and capacity for transpiration decline (Le Roux et al., 2014; Pretzsch et al., 2017). A proactive replacement strategy that anticipates tree mortality and encourages both structural and species diversity can maintain canopy continuity and stabilize ecosystem services (Millward and Sabir, 2010; Norton et al., 2015). Although the costs of replacement are significant—averaging €15,000–€25,000 per tree in dense urban areas (Sueddeutsche Zeitung, 2023)—a phased approach to renewal can spread out these expenses over time and avoid sudden canopy loss (Poschenrieder et al., 2022). Urban forests that feature a mix of ages and species demonstrate greater resilience to climate and biological stresses than uniform stands (Kendal et al., 2014). This diversity also helps reduce the risk of simultaneous mortality events caused by pests or drought (Pretzsch et al., 2017; Sjöman et al., 2018). Therefore, maintaining a diverse canopy and balanced age structure should guide urban forest planning in Munich and similar European cities. From a microclimatic perspective, the current findings confirm that the design of a square significantly affects human thermal comfort. Previous research by Stark da Silva et al. (2023, 2024) showed that spatial layout, rather than just the presence of greenery, plays a key role in the variability of the PET in public squares. The present results support this conclusion: although both Hohenzollernplatz and Bordeauxplatz feature a similar number of trees, Hohenzollernplatz consistently performs better in reducing PET under both current and projected conditions. At Hohenzollernplatz, the loss of canopy cover leads to a sharp increase in PET around 2:00 p.m. under future climate scenarios, indicating a heightened sensitivity to reductions in shading during peak solar hours (Dai et al., 2023; Jayasooriya et al., 2024). By 2050, the projected increase in PET is moderate (approximately 2.5 °C), but even minor losses in canopy coverage could intensify heat stress. Building geometry also plays a role in improving late-afternoon conditions, demonstrating that urban morphology is a decisive factor influencing PET after 4:00 p.m. In contrast, Bordeauxplatz is projected to experience an average PET increase of about 8.4 °C by 2050, with peaks reaching approximately 13 °C around noon. Areas dominated by younger trees experience the highest levels of heat stress, highlighting the cooling advantage of mature trees, which have a greater potential for evapotranspiration (Huang et al., 2020; Xue et al., 2023). Between 10:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m., as much as 90% of the area suffers from extreme heat stress, underscoring the importance of adequate canopy shade and reduction of radiant heat exposure. These findings are consistent with previous research on the nonlinear relationship between canopy cover and thermal comfort, as well as the temporal variability of shading effectiveness (Ziter et al., 2019; Rahman et al., 2020a). The results emphasize that both vegetation and spatial design work together to mitigate urban heat, making it essential to manage both aspects for effective climate adaptation (Norton et al., 2015; Frantzeskaki et al., 2019). Selecting tree species that are structurally resilient and have high transpiration efficiency will enhance thermal regulation as heat events become more frequent and severe (Zhao et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2022). This study does have some limitations. While Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios are becoming more widely used, our analysis relied on the Representative Concentration Pathway (RCP) 4.5 dataset available in Meteonorm. Furthermore, the core data inputs, including land use, tree inventories, and future climate models, came from restricted or proprietary sources, which limits reproducibility with open-access models. The mortality data for Munich restricted our ability to validate GALK vitality classes against observed tree losses, and we could not perform independent validation of nighttime mean radiant temperature due to security constraints. Despite these limitations, our findings align with research conducted in other temperate cities, which supports their robustness. Our analyses of PET dynamics and species composition suggest that maintaining thermal comfort under future climate conditions will require a careful balance. This includes preserving mature trees, implementing strategic replacements, and diversifying species composition. A phased renewal strategy aimed at ensuring continuous canopy cover, guided by locally relevant diversity thresholds, may help sustain both ecological resilience and thermal regulation in Munich’s urban squares. 5 Conclusion This study advances urban-greening research by clarifying how green infrastructure can support climate-sensitive public spaces in dense cities. The findings offer practical guidance for urban forestry and planning, particularly for the design and management of urban squares under spatial and climatic constraints. In densely built urban neighborhoods, limited planting space strongly shapes tree-management strategies. The analysis of Hohenzollernplatz and Bordeauxplatz in Munich demonstrates that square morphology and tree vitality are key determinants of human thermal comfort and projected physiologically equivalent temperature (PET) under future tree-replacement scenarios. Despite limitations such as restricted mortality data and constraints in validating mean radiant temperature, the study yields actionable insights for species selection, vitality-based replacement, and spatial planting optimization. Results indicate that replacement strategies must be tailored to each square’s spatial configuration and vitality structure. At Hohenzollernplatz, replacing the least vital trees (GALK vitality level 3) is advisable, given high replanting costs and a lack of additional planting space. Existing planting areas should be reused to maintain continuous canopy cover. In contrast, Bordeauxplatz, despite its historical layout, offers substantial potential for improvement: increasing species diversity in the central area where space is available, while preserving trees along the edges, is expected to enhance cooling capacity, reduce PET, and improve biodiversity and long-term survival. Although the study focused on two temperate urban squares, the principles are transferable to other cities. Replacing declining trees with younger, resilient specimens emerges as a key strategy for maintaining balanced age and species structures in urban forests worldwide. Replacement rates should exceed current mortality trends to sustain ecosystem functionality, yet analysis of Munich’s recent tree data suggests this practice has not yet been systematically implemented. The findings underscore the need for proactive, adaptive tree-management strategies that go beyond compensating for losses. Management should prioritize species resilient to climate stress, and integrate tree health, local microclimate conditions, and spatial constraints. Continued emphasis on preserving mature trees without systematic renewal risks deteriorating thermal comfort and long-term microclimatic benefits in urban squares toward 2050. In conclusion, sustaining thermal comfort in urban public spaces requires careful assessment of urban morphology, planting design, and species diversity for each square. Expanding tree diversity in suitable locations and implementing phased replacement where immediate replanting is not feasible can strengthen the resilience of urban green infrastructure under climate change. This study faces several limitations. The Munich mortality dataset restricted a robust correlation between GALK vitality classes and historical tree loss; future work should revisit this relationship using the updated 2024 dataset with refined parameters. Additionally, nighttime mean radiant temperature could not be independently validated due to security and logistical constraints; validation relied on air temperature and relative humidity instead. Future research should further explore how diverse tree species influence ecosystem services and microclimate regulation, and how these effects can be optimized for human thermal comfort. Expanding analyses to multiple climate scenarios beyond the 2050 projection used here will strengthen the evidence base for climate-adaptive urban-greening strategies in public spaces.
