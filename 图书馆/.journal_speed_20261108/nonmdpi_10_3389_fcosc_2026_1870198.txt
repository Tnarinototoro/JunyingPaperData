You can register here Notify me Abstract Hunting presents a fundamental cosmological dilemma for Indigenous peoples of the Amazon, because game animals are conceived of as non-human persons with subjectivity, agency, and a penchant for revenge. For the Matsigenka communities that inhabit Manu National Park, the hunting of primates is shaped not only by ecological and nutritional considerations but by a complex of cultural beliefs and food preferences. Drawing on over two decades of participatory hunting monitoring — comprising more than 5,000 hunting events recorded by Indigenous monitors across three Matsigenka communities — combined with quantitative analysis of hunter selectivity and collaborative knowledge production with Matsigenka co-authors, this study examines how sociocultural factors shape primate hunting volume, prey composition, and species selectivity. Primate hunting follows a consistent seasonal pattern, peaking during the rainy season when animals provide a greater nutritional return and declining during the dry season, a pattern reinforced by socially mediated taste preferences. Spider monkeys (Ateles chamek) dominate hunting returns across most communities and periods, while red howler monkeys (Alouatta seniculus) accounted for no more than 10% of hunting returns in any community or period, despite their similar body mass and availability, reflecting normative cultural taboos associated with this species. Analysis of individual hunter activity in relation to birth records confirms a significant reduction in hunting during the pre-and post-partum period, providing behavioral evidence for the influence of traditional concepts about game animal revenge on the hunter's family. These results show a clear and significant role of Matsigenka culture, cosmology, and ritual on the behavior of hunters, underscoring the importance of integrating Indigenous knowledge systems in conservation science and planning.
