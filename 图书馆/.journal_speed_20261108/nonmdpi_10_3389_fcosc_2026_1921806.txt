You can register here Notify me Abstract Human–wildlife conflict involves ecological processes and material harms, but its consequences also depend on the institutions governing land, relief, and participation. This article examines four purposively selected narrative accounts from hill regions of southern Tamil Nadu and the Wayanad–Karnataka border region: those of an Indigenous elder, a field-level conservation official, and two forest-fringe cultivators. Through thematic narrative analysis, we examine how participants connect wildlife encounters with historical dispossession, compensation procedures, livelihood insecurity, ecological change, and local knowledge. The accounts identify different barriers to obtaining relief and participating in conservation, while also revealing shared concerns and responsibilities across community and administrative positions. Comparison with published studies of forest belonging and conservation-related displacement situates these findings within debates about recognition, the distribution of burdens, and decision-making authority. We propose a provisional governance framework linking rights and recognition, accessible relief, shared decision-making, and locally appropriate ecological mitigation. The four accounts provide situated evidence rather than representative findings for South India, and the framework requires further evaluation. The article contributes an interpretive account of how historical relationships and contemporary administrative arrangements shape experiences of human–wildlife conflict.
