Abstract

Urban pluvial flooding can disrupt road networks, delay emergency medical services and produce spatially uneven losses of access to urgent healthcare. This study evaluates how flood-induced road disruption affects ambulance travel performance and identifies buildings, neighbourhoods and populations that experience reduced emergency healthcare accessibility under rainfall events with return periods of 10, 20 and 100 years in Thessaloniki, Greece. High-resolution flood-depth outputs were integrated with direction-specific road operating speeds, building footprints, population estimates, an ambulance terminal and seven public hospitals. Road-section speeds were adjusted according to local flood depth and impassable sections were removed, while minimum-time routes were then calculated for the terminal-to-patient, patient-to-hospital and hospital-to-terminal legs across 21 two-hospital duty configurations. Flooding produced substantial increases in travel time and distance even though only a small proportion of road links became impassable. Under the 100-year scenario, the median full-service-cycle time increased from 24.8 to 33.0 min, patient-delivery completion declined from 100.0% to 86.1% and the proportion of journeys completed within 30 min fell from 97.3% to 56.1%. The patient-to-hospital leg deteriorated most, while comparison with baseline revealed that the greatest flood-related reductions in patient-delivery accessibility within 30 min occurred in northern and northwestern Thessaloniki under the modelled terminal and hospital-duty configurations. The study provides an integrated framework that combines ambulance travel modelling with building- and population-level accessibility analysis under flood conditions. The findings show that limited but strategically located road disruptions can generate disproportionate operational and spatial impacts, highlighting the importance of protecting critical corridors, supporting flood-aware routing and considering hospital availability in emergency preparedness planning.

1 Introduction

Cities concentrate population, economic activity, infrastructure and essential services and their functioning depends heavily on the continuity of urban transport networks. Transport disruption can delay the movement of workers and goods, restrict access to critical facilities and undermine the delivery of essential public services. Recent review evidence identifies delays, infrastructure damage and operational disruption as recurring consequences for transport and logistics systems exposed to adverse weather [1] and this dependence is becoming more vulnerable as climate change alters the frequency and intensity of extreme weather. Urban pluvial flooding is already a significant challenge in cities worldwide, where intense rainfall can overwhelm drainage capacity, inundate transport infrastructure and disrupt essential urban services. Climate change is expected to intensify this challenge, as the frequency and intensity of heavy precipitation are projected to increase across many regions with continued global warming [2]. These developments highlight the growing importance of understanding how pluvial flooding affects the continuity of critical urban services, particularly emergency healthcare, whose timely delivery depends on reliable road-network accessibility. Within the European Union, weather-related extremes caused an estimated €822 billion in economic losses between 1980 and 2024, with hydrological events (primarily floods) accounting for approximately 47% of the total [3], while for 1998–2010, extreme weather was estimated to impose annual costs of approximately €2.5 billion on the EU transport sector, together with a further €1 billion in indirect costs to other sectors arising from transport disruption [4].

Pluvial flooding is particularly consequential for urban transport because intense rainfall interacts with dense development and largely impervious surfaces to generate rapid surface-water flooding and the resulting inundation is both a physical hazard and an operational threat to urban functioning [5]. Floodwater can compromise vehicle safety, reduce operating speeds and, at depths of approximately 0.30 m, render road segments impassable, thereby increasing flood-induced delays [6]. Spatial variation in flood depth and extent can produce nonlinear patterns of road-inundation risk [7]. These effects are especially consequential for Emergency Medical Services (EMS), which depend on rapid and reliable road access, since longer ambulance response times are associated with poorer outcomes for time-sensitive conditions such as out-of-hospital cardiac arrest [8]. Flood-related speed reductions may delay arrival at the patient, while road closures may make some locations unreachable. From an operational perspective, an ambulance mission is a time-critical service trip that continues beyond patient contact because transporting the patient to a receiving hospital also occupies the vehicle and crew. An ambulance terminal is the departure and return base for emergency vehicles, separate from receiving hospitals. Longer travel across the terminal-to-patient, patient-to-hospital and return movements therefore increase the travel component of vehicle and crew commitment and may reduce effective ambulance availability. EMS research similarly shows that dispatching and relocation decisions influence system preparedness and coverage [9].

The consequences are also spatially uneven, as citywide averages may suggest a moderate increase in travel time while concealing neighbourhoods where delays become severe or access is lost. Emergency healthcare accessibility varies with traffic conditions and spatial location within the road network [10] and flood-related transport-network disruption can disproportionately reduce emergency-service coverage for vulnerable populations [11]. More broadly, flood-induced speed reductions and road closures can affect both the timeliness and feasibility of emergency healthcare access, increasing travel times, reducing service coverage and in some locations resulting in loss of access altogether [12,13,14]. Despite this evidence, the implications of urban flooding for ambulance services remain only partly understood. Although existing assessments provide important evidence on changes in emergency-service coverage, they offer a more limited account of how disruption propagates through the successive road movements required to deliver emergency healthcare and how the resulting operational burden is distributed across an urban population.

To address this gap, this study represents the terminal-to-patient, patient-to-hospital and hospital-to-terminal movements of an ambulance service cycle. Operational performance is assessed across all three legs, while spatial accessibility is evaluated using the first two legs, which determine whether the patient can be reached and transported to an available receiving hospital. Applied to Thessaloniki, Greece, a densely developed and flood-prone urban area, the analysis examines how flood conditions on individual road segments affect response, patient-transport and return movements, as well as the combined patient-delivery journey and complete service cycle. The study has two objectives which are to assess how flooding changes ambulance travel performance and to identify where delays and losses of accessibility are concentrated, thereby examining both the operational resilience of emergency medical services and the spatial distribution of emergency healthcare access under flood conditions. It addresses two research questions:

  1. (1)

    How do pluvial flood conditions affect travel time, distance and route feasibility for the response, patient-transport and return legs, and for the complete ambulance service cycle?

  2. (2)

    Which areas experience increased delay, threshold exceedance or complete loss of accessibility, and how do these outcomes vary with flood severity and the configuration of hospitals on duty?

The study makes three principal contributions; conceptually, it integrates the three-leg road-based ambulance service cycle with the two-leg patient-delivery journey to assess how flood-related disruption affects both ambulance travel performance and emergency healthcare accessibility, while capturing the additional travel commitment associated with returning to the ambulance terminal. Methodologically, it integrates high-resolution flood depths, direction-specific operating speeds, depth-dependent road disruption, alternative hospital-duty configurations and building-level population estimates within a common network framework that distinguishes delay from complete loss of route feasibility, while practically, it identifies urban areas and residents for whom ambulance travel becomes delayed or infeasible, providing evidence for emergency-routing preparedness, ambulance staging, protection of critical road connections and coordination of hospital availability during floods. More broadly, it frames continuity of emergency healthcare access as a public-health and critical-service dimension of urban water resilience, linking flood-hazard modelling with transport-network functionality and EMS preparedness to support climate-adaptation planning, consistent with the broader policy direction of the 2025 European Water Resilience Strategy of the EU [15].

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows: Sect.  2 reviews transport disruption caused by flooding, ambulance logistics and emergency healthcare accessibility; Sect.  3 presents the study area, datasets, scenarios and analytical framework; Sect.  4 reports the operational and spatial accessibility results; Sect.  5 discusses the findings, implications and limitations; and Sect.  6 concludes the study.

2 Background

Urban flood impacts on road networks are commonly evaluated by translating water depth into either binary road closure or a continuous reduction in vehicle speed, as binary approaches identify route failure, whereas continuous functions capture progressive delay before a road becomes impassable. The empirical depth-disruption function, for example, converts flood depth into reduced vehicle speed and treats roads as impassable to passenger vehicles at approximately 0.30 m [6]. The transfer of this function to other vehicle types remains uncertain because it was developed for passenger vehicles, while later work shows that flood roadworthiness and network functionality vary materially by vehicle type, including between standard cars and SUVs [16]. At network scale, exposure alone does not determine functional disruption; the effect of an inundated link also depends on its baseline speed, network position and available alternatives, and consequently, a small increase in affected road length can produce a disproportionate loss of connectivity. In Wuhan, China, although the share of flooded roads increased by approximately 3.1% points between the 10-year and 50-year events, the length of impassable roads increased more than fivefold [17], while in Galluzzo, Italy, flood disruption reduced the area reachable by emergency services from 110.5 km² under baseline conditions to 4.1 km² at the second flood peak [18].

Understanding these network failures is particularly important for EMS because road performance directly affects the emergency-care pathway, and ambulance response time is significantly associated with outcomes for time-sensitive conditions, including survival to hospital discharge following out-of-hospital cardiac arrest [8]. Spatial-accessibility studies therefore commonly evaluate the populations or areas reachable from emergency stations within predefined time thresholds. For example, in Leicester, 8-minute ambulance accessibility was mapped under surface-water and fluvial flooding; surface-water depths ≥ 0.25 m were treated as restrictions, whereas fluvial restrictions were based on flood-hazard categories [19]. Later city-scale studies incorporated depth-dependent speeds, multiple traffic conditions and demographic data. In Calgary, EMS experienced the greatest accessibility losses among the emergency services examined, leaving up to 47% of workers outside targeted response conditions during flooding [12], while in Shanghai, under adverse traffic conditions, 15-min ambulance coverage of the examined vulnerable population groups declined from 76 to 88% under normal conditions to 60–73% during a 100-year pluvial flood [13].

Beyond initial response coverage, emergency healthcare accessibility also depends on the movement from the patient to a receiving hospital. Luo et al. [20] examined ambulance-to-patient and patient-to-hospital accessibility under peak and off-peak traffic conditions, while Silva and Padeiro [21] evaluated geographical accessibility through the combined patient-delivery journey in metropolitan Lisbon. These studies demonstrate the importance of considering both movements when assessing emergency healthcare accessibility, although their integration with spatially explicit flood disruption remains limited. EMS operations research adopts a broader interpretation of ambulance performance, with reviews emphasising ambulance location, dispatching, relocation and system preparedness [22, 23]. A patient-transport mission does not end at patient contact, as the vehicle and crew remain occupied during treatment and transport to a receiving hospital and hospital selection may depend on travel time, routing, capacity and waiting conditions [24]. Hospital offload delays can further extend turnaround and postpone the return of vehicles and crews to service [25], while integrated dispatch and relocation models show that current assignments affect the capacity to respond to subsequent calls [9]. Flood-induced delay in either the response or patient-transport leg may therefore extend the travel period for which an ambulance remains committed even when a route remains feasible.

The consequences are spatially uneven and citywide averages may conceal severe local impacts; flooding can reduce emergency-response coverage for vulnerable populations [11], while equity frameworks that incorporate social vulnerability can reveal unequal household burdens that may be hidden by aggregate or network-scale metrics [26]. Indirect and time-varying network effects also mean that accessibility can decline in areas without direct local inundation. For example, accessibility was shown to fluctuate hourly across neighbourhood-scale grid cells, with disruption extending beyond directly flooded road Sect [14]. Seventy-five German hospitals were identified as being at risk of service-population increases beyond regular capacity due solely to flood-related traffic disruption; approximately one-third were located more than 10 km from the nearest inundation [27]. High-resolution population grids and population nuclei can reveal concentrations of accessibility loss and route infeasibility that broader administrative zones may obscure, although estimates of population accessibility loss also depend on the representation and completeness of the road network [28].

Taken together, the literature identifies three complementary but insufficiently connected dimensions of the problem. Flood-transport studies explain how water depth reduces road speed, induces rerouting and causes disconnection but rarely focus on ambulance missions, while flood-related EMS accessibility studies estimate response-time coverage and population exposure but generally focus on the initial movement from a station to an incident location. Studies considering both ambulance response and patient transport provide a broader assessment of emergency healthcare accessibility, although their integration with spatially explicit flood disruption remains limited. Finally, EMS operations research recognises that the ambulance remains committed after reaching the patient and that receiving-hospital selection affects mission duration but rarely incorporates spatially explicit flood disruption. The methodological gap is therefore to evaluate, within one framework, how road-level speed reductions and loss of passability affect the successive movements of an ambulance service cycle while accounting for hospital availability. It also remains necessary to distinguish additional delay, threshold exceedance and complete loss of route feasibility and to identify how these outcomes are concentrated across buildings, neighbourhoods and resident populations.

3 Materials and methods

This section first introduces the Thessaloniki study area and the emergency-care facilities considered, followed by the datasets used in the study, including flood-hazard, road-network and sociodemographic data. It then presents the analytical workflow used to integrate these data, construct scenario-specific road networks, model ambulance service trips across hospital-duty configurations and evaluate operational performance and spatial accessibility.

3.1 Study area

Thessaloniki is the second-largest city in Greece and the largest metropolitan centre in northern Greece. Its urban agglomeration extends along the Thermaic Gulf across several municipalities beyond the administrative boundary of the Municipality of Thessaloniki. The study area covers approximately 111.7 km² and represents the continuously developed urban core broadly enclosed by the coastline and the Thessaloniki ring road. According to the 2021 census, the municipality had 319,045 permanent residents, compared with 1,092,919 in the wider Thessaloniki Regional Unit [29]. The area combines a dense, largely impervious central core with low-lying coastal and western sectors, while elevation rises towards the northern and eastern districts. High-resolution hydrodynamic modelling shows that intense rainfall can generate localised pluvial inundation, with major surface-water flow paths aligned with principal streets [30]. Complementary GIS-based assessment using historical flood records identifies several flood-vulnerable zones within the Municipality of Thessaloniki [31]. Historical records identify the Thessaloniki prefecture as one of Greece’s most frequently flood-affected areas, with 122 recorded events between 1886 and 2022, predominantly urban in character [32]. The concentration of population and essential services in a road-dependent metropolitan area makes Thessaloniki an appropriate case for examining how local flood disruption propagates through the transport network and affects emergency healthcare access.

Ambulance operations were represented from a terminal in Konstantinoupolitika, in the eastern part of the study area. Seven public hospitals were considered as potential receiving facilities, namely Gennimatas, Ippokratio, Agios Pavlos, Agios Dimitrios, AHEPA, Papanikolaou and Papageorgiou. Most are located in or near the central and eastern urban area, whereas Papanikolaou and Papageorgiou occupy more peripheral northern locations. Emergency care is organised through a rotating duty system in which two hospitals are generally on duty, although patient eligibility depends on clinical specialisation and additional facilities may be available for specific services. To examine how hospital availability influences ambulance travel performance and emergency healthcare accessibility, all 21 possible two-hospital combinations were considered as equally weighted analytical configurations (Fig. 1).

3.2 Data

3.2.1 Flood hazard data

Flood-hazard information was produced using the City Catchment Analysis Tool (CityCAT), developed at Newcastle University, UK [33]. CityCAT is a fully coupled modelling system for simulating, analysing and visualising surface water flooding in urban areas, including interactions between surface flow and subsurface drainage networks. The model solves the complete two-dimensional Shallow Water Equations (SWE) within a finite-volume numerical framework [34]. Shock-capturing schemes are employed to reproduce flood-wave propagation, wetting and drying processes and abrupt flow transitions across complex urban terrain [35]. Its object-oriented structure also supports flexibility and further development [33]. Urban features are represented explicitly within the model; buildings are incorporated using the “Building Hole” method [36], while dedicated formulations are available for bridges [37] and different Blue-Green Adaptation measures [38].

For the present analysis, the model domain was represented by a regular grid of 2 m × 2 m cells. The terrain surface was derived from a site-specific Digital Terrain Model provided by the Hellenic Cadastre at the same 2 m resolution and accessed on 14 April 2025. Permeable and impermeable surfaces were assigned Manning roughness coefficients of 0.035 and 0.02, respectively [30, 39], while infiltration over permeable surfaces was represented using the Green–Ampt formulation [40]. Drainage was represented by assigning 20% of rainfall to the drainage system. The flood-hazard library comprises 1 h design-storm simulations with return periods of 10, 20 and 100 years (RP10, RP20 and RP100), developed using historical rainfall observations and the statistical extreme-rainfall methodology described in [30]. For each scenario, the model output records the maximum water depth, expressed in metres, reached in every grid cell during the simulated event. Simulations were conducted in the EPSG:2100 Greek Grid coordinate reference system and the outputs were then transformed to EPSG:4326 to support the spatial analysis and the wider application of the proposed framework. The resulting raster datasets provide spatially continuous information on flood extent, depth and concentration throughout the study area.

3.2.2 Road network data

The road network and segment-level operating speeds were obtained from the Thessaloniki Sustainable Urban Mobility Plan (SUMP), provided by the Hellenic Institute of Transport (HIT). The dataset forms part of the officially approved SUMP published in the Government Gazette of the Hellenic Republic [41] and was produced through traffic-assignment modelling in SUMO (Simulation of Urban MObility) [42], which provides an operating speed for each road segment under loaded traffic conditions. The simulated morning peak from 08:00 to 09:00 was selected to represent high network demand associated with simultaneous commuter and urban freight movements (Fig. 2).

These speeds define the baseline road conditions used to calculate ambulance travel times without flooding, while their spatial variation captures differences in network performance and provides a more realistic routing cost than a uniform speed or posted speed limits. For each flood scenario, segment speeds were then adjusted according to road-specific flood depth, allowing changes in ambulance route choice, travel time and distance to be evaluated.

3.2.3 Sociodemographic data layers

Population data were obtained from the Sustainable Urban Development Observatory of Thessaloniki and comprise spatially explicit counts from the 2011 census at census block level [43], while building footprints were acquired from ONEGEO and were used to represent potential patient-origin locations across the study area [44]. The locations of the ambulance terminal and the seven public hospitals were also included as the departure and receiving facilities used in the service trip analysis.

3.3 Methodological framework

3.3.1 Data integration and spatial preprocessing

All spatial layers were reprojected to a common projected coordinate system and clipped to the study area. Integration followed two parallel workflows consisting of (i) redistribution of census-block population to individual buildings and (ii) assignment of scenario-specific flood depths to road sections (Fig. 3).

Population was available at census-block level for 2011, while the 2021 census provided updated population totals. The 2021 ELSTAT population of 747,870 residents for the six municipalities covering the study area (Thessaloniki, Kordelio–Evosmos, Pavlos Melas, Kalamaria, Neapoli–Sykies and Ampelokipoi–Menemeni) was used as the population reference [29]. These municipalities encompass most of the continuously developed urban territory represented in the study area, although their administrative boundaries do not coincide exactly with the analytical boundary. Their combined 2021 census population was therefore adopted as a reference for rescaling the 2011 census-block population distribution rather than as an independently measured population total for the exact study-area boundary. This total was redistributed across census blocks according to their relative population shares in the 2011 census and subsequently allocated to individual buildings in proportion to their footprint area, without filtering by building use. Where census blocks had no associated building footprints, their population was allocated to surrounding buildings using the same footprint-area weighting approach. Of the 82,199 buildings included in the analysis, 74,753 received positive population estimates, while all buildings were retained as potential patient-origin locations for the building-based accessibility assessment. Population-weighted accessibility indicators were subsequently calculated using the estimated building populations:

$$\:{P}_{i}={P}_{c}\frac{{A}_{i}}{\sum\:_{j\in\:{B}_{c}}{A}_{j}},$$

where \(\:{P}_{i}\) is the population assigned to building \(\:i\), \(\:{P}_{c}\) is the rebalanced population of census block \(\:c\), \(\:{A}_{i}\) is the footprint area of building \(\:i\), and \(\:{B}_{c}\) is the set of buildings within census block \(\:c\); \(\:j\) indexes the buildings in this set. The resulting building-level estimates represented potential patient origins and population exposure.

The road network was divided into sections of approximately 2 m to align with the flood-grid resolution. For each rainfall scenario, flood depth was sampled at the start, midpoint and end of each section; the maximum of the three values was assigned to that section and then linked to the baseline directional SUMO speed of the section. The 2 m resolution therefore applies to the flood-depth representation and road-section sampling, whereas baseline operating speeds remain specific to the original directional road links and building populations represent census-derived estimates. Accessibility outcomes were evaluated at building-linked road-network locations and subsequently aggregated to 500 m × 500 m grid cells to examine neighbourhood-scale spatial patterns.

3.3.2 Road network modelling under flood conditions

For each road section, the assigned flood depth was converted to a maximum safe vehicle speed using the depth-disruption function [6]:

$$\:{v}_{e,s}^{P}=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}0.0009{D}_{e,s}^{2}-0.5529{D}_{e,s}+86.9448,&\:{D}_{e,s}<300,\\\:0,&\:{D}_{e,s}\ge\:300,\end{array}\right.$$

where \(\:{v}_{e,s}^{P}\) is the flood-based maximum safe speed for road section \(\:e\) under scenario \(\:s\), expressed in km/h and \(\:{D}_{e,s}\) is the assigned flood depth in millimetres.

The flood-adjusted operating speed was then defined as the lower of the baseline SUMO speed and the flood-based safe speed:

$$\:{v}_{e,s}=\text{m}\text{i}\text{n}\left({v}_{e}^{\text{S}\text{U}\text{M}\text{O}},\hspace{0.17em}{v}_{e,s}^{P}\right),$$

where \(\:{v}_{e}^{\text{S}\text{U}\text{M}\text{O}}\) is the baseline directional operating speed and \(\:{v}_{e,s}\) is the resulting flood-adjusted speed.

The adjustment was applied separately to each travel direction while retaining one-way restrictions. Section travel time was calculated from length and adjusted speed and impassable sections were removed to produce four directed networks consisting of the baseline, RP10, RP20 and RP100.

3.3.3 Ambulance service trip modelling

Each analysed building was represented by an interior point and linked to the nearest node of the directed road network, while the ambulance terminal and seven hospitals were linked in the same way. The hospitals formed 21 unique two-hospital duty combinations, each assigned equal analytical weight; hospital selection was based on minimum travel time within each pair.

For each network scenario, minimum-time routes were calculated using the shortest-path algorithm of Dijkstra [45]. Terminal-to-building routes represented the response leg, building-to-hospital routes the patient-transport leg and hospital-to-terminal routes the return leg. Route distance was measured along the minimum-time path; where alternatives had equal travel time, the shorter path was retained. The calculation involved nine shortest-path searches per network scenario, comprising one forward search from the ambulance terminal, seven reverse searches from the hospitals and one reverse search from the terminal. The resulting 36 searches across the four network scenarios were used to evaluate 6,904,716 building–hospital-duty-pair–scenario combinations.

For each patient origin and hospital-duty combination, the receiving hospital was the on-duty facility with the shortest patient-transport time:

$$\:{\widehat{h}}_{i,p,s}=\underset{h\in\:p}{\text{a}\text{r}\text{g}\text{m}\text{i}\text{n}}\hspace{0.33em}{t}_{i,h,s}^{H},$$

where \(\:{\widehat{h}}_{i,p,s}\) is the selected hospital for building \(\:i\), duty pair \(\:p\), and scenario \(\:s\); \(\:h\) denotes a hospital in \(\:p\); and \(\:{t}_{i,h,s}^{H}\) is the patient transport time from building \(\:i\) to hospital \(\:h\).

Patient-delivery time and distance were calculated as the sum of the response and patient-transport legs, while for the operational assessment, the minimum-time return route from the selected hospital to the ambulance terminal was also calculated.

$$\:{T}_{i,p,s}^{PD}={t}_{i,s}^{R}+{t}_{i,{\widehat{h}}_{i,p,s},s}^{H},\:\:{D}_{i,p,s}^{PD}={d}_{i,s}^{R}+{d}_{i,{\widehat{h}}_{i,p,s},s}^{H}.$$

Full-service-cycle time and distance were then calculated by adding the return leg:

$$\:{T}_{i,p,s}^{SC}={T}_{i,p,s}^{PD}+{t}_{{\widehat{h}}_{i,p,s},s}^{RT},\:\:{D}_{i,p,s}^{SC}={D}_{i,p,s}^{PD}+{d}_{{\widehat{h}}_{i,p,s},s}^{RT},$$

where \(\:{t}_{i,s}^{R}\)and \(\:{d}_{i,s}^{R}\)are response-leg time and distance; \(\:{t}_{i,{\widehat{h}}_{i,p,s},s}^{H}\)and \(\:{d}_{i,{\widehat{h}}_{i,p,s},s}^{H}\)are patient transport time and distance; and \(\:{t}_{{\widehat{h}}_{i,p,s},s}^{RT}\)and \(\:{d}_{{\widehat{h}}_{i,p,s},s}^{RT}\)are return-leg time and distance. \(\:{T}_{i,p,s}^{PD}\)and \(\:{D}_{i,p,s}^{PD}\)denote the two-leg patient delivery journey, while \(\:{T}_{i,p,s}^{SC}\)and \(\:{D}_{i,p,s}^{SC}\)denote the complete three-leg service cycle.

A patient-delivery journey was feasible when routes existed for both the response and patient-transport legs, while a full-service cycle was feasible when routes existed for all three legs. A building was classified as disconnected for a scenario and hospital-duty combination when no feasible patient-delivery journey existed.

3.3.4 Operational and spatial impact assessment

Operational performance was assessed across all buildings and equally weighted hospital-duty combinations using response time, patient-transport time, return time, full-service-cycle time and distance, patient-delivery completion and full-service-cycle completion, while spatial accessibility was assessed using the two-leg patient-delivery journey and the proportion completed within 30 min. The 30-min threshold was adopted as a comparative indicator of network-based EMS accessibility, consistent with studies that represent the emergency journey through the combined ambulance-to-patient and patient-to-hospital legs and use journeys exceeding 30 min to indicate relatively poor overall access [20, 21]. It is an operational planning benchmark rather than a universal clinical standard and applies only to modelled road travel; call processing, mobilisation, patient access, on-scene treatment, hospital handover and other non-travel intervals are excluded.

Leg-specific and full-service-cycle travel indicators were summarised for completed three-leg cycles, with each feasible building–hospital-duty-pair combination assigned equal weight. Patient-delivery completion and patient delivery within 30 min were evaluated across all building–hospital-duty-pair combinations, with infeasible journeys retained as disconnected outcomes. The hospital-switch rate was calculated among feasible patient-delivery journeys and represented the proportion for which the hospital selected under a flood scenario differed from that selected for the same building and hospital-duty pair under baseline conditions, meaning that the other hospital in the same duty pair became the minimum-time destination. Travel-time distributions therefore describe completed cycles, whereas completion and within-30-min accessibility indicators retain all potential journeys in their denominators.

For each building and scenario, the 21 equally weighted hospital-duty pairs were classified as within 30 min, above 30 min or disconnected, and the share of duty configurations associated with each outcome was calculated as:

$$\:{P}_{i,s}^{k}=\frac{{n}_{i,s}^{k}}{N},$$

where \(\:{P}_{i,s}^{k}\) is the probability of outcome \(\:k\) for building \(\:i\) under scenario \(\:s\), \(\:{n}_{i,s}^{k}\) is the number of hospital duty combinations producing that outcome and \(\:N\) is the total number of combinations.

Population impacts were estimated by multiplying each building-level outcome share by the population allocated to that building and summing across buildings, while comparisons with baseline identified buildings and residents that became newly delayed or newly disconnected under RP10, RP20 and RP100. Building-level results were aggregated to 500 m × 500 m grid cells as neighbourhood-scale analytical units, while for each cell, the share of residents associated with journeys exceeding 30 min and the share associated with disconnection were calculated to identify local concentrations of reduced emergency healthcare accessibility. A journey was classified as newly delayed when the same building–hospital-duty-pair combination was completed within 30 min at baseline but remained feasible and exceeded 30 min under flooding, while journeys without a feasible patient-delivery route were classified separately as disconnected. Total affected outcomes combined delayed and disconnected journeys, with building and resident counts representing equally weighted averages across the 21 hospital-duty combinations. Building-level results were subsequently aggregated to 500 m × 500 m grid cells to identify neighbourhood-scale concentrations of reduced emergency healthcare accessibility.

4 Results

4.1 Network disruption and ambulance service trip performance under flooding

Flooding affected road-network functionality through depth-dependent speed restrictions and the removal of impassable links, while the share of closed directed edges and the unweighted mean speed of edges that remained open are reported by scenario (Table 1).

The proportion of impassable directed edges increased from 1.8% under RP10 to 4.5% under RP100, whereas the unweighted mean speed of open edges decreased only moderately, from 31.0 km/h at baseline to 29.8 km/h under RP100. The network-wide effect was therefore driven less by a uniform reduction in speed than by the closure of a small share of strategically important links. The following results (Fig. 4) show how these changes affected route feasibility and ambulance travel performance in terms of time, distance and mean speed of completed patient-delivery journeys and three-leg service cycles across the four scenarios.

Flooding shifted completed service cycles towards longer times and distances and lower mean route speeds, while median full-service-cycle time rose from 24.8 min at baseline to 29.8 min under RP10, 30.9 min under RP20 and 33.0 min under RP100. The upper tail deteriorated more strongly as the 95th percentile increased from 37.4 min at baseline to 60.5 min under RP100, while the interquartile range widened from 17.4 to 31.8 min to 22.0–48.2 min. Median cycle distance increased from 16.3 to 18.3 km and the 95th percentile from 27.6 to 35.7 km, whereas median cycle speed declined from 40.6 to 33.6 km/h. The wider distributions indicate that some patient origins remained comparatively unaffected while others required substantial detours or experienced severe delays. Service-cycle deterioration was therefore much greater than the small reduction in the mean speed of open network edges would suggest. The patient-delivery distributions show the corresponding deterioration in the two-leg journey (Fig. 4a–c).

To distinguish additional travel burden from loss of feasibility, the operational assessment combined completion indicators with mean times for the three legs and the full service cycle. Time indicators describe completed cycles; disconnected journeys are represented separately by the completion and 30-min accessibility indicators, which retain all potential journeys in their denominators (Table 2).

Flooding reduced both feasibility and timeliness, while the share of patients delivered within 30 min declined more rapidly than patient-delivery completion, showing that many locations remained reachable but at a substantially lower level of service. Under RP100, the patient-to-hospital leg had the largest relative increase in mean travel time, indicating that access to an available receiving facility became a major constraint after the patient had been reached.

Patient-delivery feasibility equalled terminal-to-patient reachability in every scenario, indicating that the patient-to-hospital leg caused no additional disconnections for origins reachable from the terminal. Patient-delivery and full-service-cycle completion rates were identical in every scenario. Although one-way restrictions were retained, every feasible patient-delivery journey also had a feasible hospital-to-terminal return route, and the return leg therefore caused no additional route failures, while its longer duration extended the period for which the ambulance and crew remained committed. Hospital switching became more frequent as flood severity increased, from 2.2% under RP10 to 6.7% under RP100, meaning that flooding changed which of the two hospitals in the same duty pair provided the minimum-time patient-transport route for a relatively small share of journeys. Its limited magnitude indicates that selecting the fastest of the two on-duty hospitals compensated for only part of the wider disruption.

Because the receiving hospital was restricted to the two facilities available in each duty configuration, performance also depended on the spatial composition of the pair. The figure reports the unweighted mean share of patient-delivery journeys completed within 30 min across RP10, RP20 and RP100 for each pair. As scenario-specific performance decreased consistently with flood severity, the mean provides a compact comparison of the relative performance of hospital configurations (Fig. 5).

Performance differed materially among hospital-duty configurations, showing that flood impacts depended not only on overall network disruption but also on the locations of the available receiving facilities, while pairs that preserved access through different parts of the road network generally maintained higher accessibility, whereas pairs dependent on similarly disrupted corridors performed less effectively. Hospital-duty organisation can therefore moderate flood-related service losses, although it cannot fully offset widespread network fragmentation.

4.2 Spatial distribution of delayed and disconnected accessibility

Spatial accessibility was assessed separately for buildings and their allocated populations. Feasible patient-delivery journeys exceeding 30 min were classified as delayed, while journeys without a feasible terminal-to-patient and patient-to-hospital route were classified as disconnected. Newly delayed buildings and residents were identified through comparisons with baseline, while total affected outcomes combined delayed and disconnected journeys (Table 3).

Accessibility losses were substantial even under RP10 and increased with event severity, while the change from RP10 to RP20 was comparatively moderate, whereas RP100 produced a more pronounced expansion of delayed and disconnected outcomes. This suggests that the additional links lost in the most severe event affected routes for which local detours were insufficient.

The share of affected buildings was consistently higher than the corresponding population share, reflecting differences between building-based and population-weighted exposure, although the population impact remained substantial as under RP100, 252,861 residents (33.8%) were associated with patient-delivery journeys exceeding 30 min or complete disconnection. Most delayed outcomes under flooding were newly generated rather than continuations of baseline disadvantage, and all disconnections were new because none occurred at baseline. To show their local concentration, the share of residents with a feasible patient-delivery journey exceeding 30 min was mapped for each 500 m grid cell, while panels (a)–(d) show baseline, RP10, RP20 and RP100, respectively and values in each scenario are equally weighted averages across the 21 hospital-duty combinations (Fig. 6).

At baseline, delayed accessibility was limited to a small number of cells, mainly in the northern part of the study area, while flooding produced a distinct cluster of high delay in the northwestern and northern sectors; this cluster intensified and expanded eastward as severity increased, whereas much of the central, southern and eastern study area retained comparatively low shares of delayed residents. The persistence of the northern cluster indicates that these locations depended on a limited set of road connections that became progressively slower or unavailable.

To distinguish additional flood-related accessibility losses from pre-existing spatial differences, the reduction in the population-weighted share of journeys completed within 30 min under RP100 relative to baseline was also mapped (Fig. 7). The results show that the greatest additional losses were concentrated in northern and northwestern Thessaloniki, including journeys that became delayed or disconnected under flooding.

5 Discussion

5.1 Flood impacts on ambulance travel performance

Flooding substantially affected ambulance service trips despite closing only a small share of the road network, as under RP100, 4.5% of directed edges became impassable and the mean speed of open edges decreased only slightly, yet service-cycle times increased, completion rates declined and route distances lengthened. The impact therefore depended strongly on the network position of affected links because speed reductions and closures on critical corridors forced longer detours or removed all feasible alternatives. Similar studies show that road-network functionality is governed by connectivity and route redundancy, not only by the total length of flooded roads [16, 18].

The widening of travel-time and distance distributions confirms that impacts were uneven across service trips; many origins retained routes with limited change, whereas a smaller group experienced substantial detours and delays, especially under RP100. The strong increase in the upper tail shows that severe flooding concentrated operational burdens on particular journeys rather than producing a uniform citywide slowdown, and these effects arose from the combined influence of depth-dependent speed reductions, road closures and alternative-path availability, supporting the representation of both progressive speed loss and impassability in flood-disruption analysis [6].

The patient-to-hospital leg experienced the greatest relative deterioration because once the patient had been reached, the ambulance had to connect each origin to one of two on-duty hospitals, often through longer cross-city movements and flood-affected corridors. The second hospital provided some flexibility but could not offset widespread network disruption, while the return leg caused no additional disconnections because every completed patient-delivery journey retained a feasible hospital-to-terminal route; however, the longer return movement extended ambulance and crew commitment, with potential implications for preparedness for subsequent incidents.

5.2 Spatial disparities and the influence of hospital availability

Flooding widened spatial disparities in emergency healthcare accessibility, while the share of journeys completed within 30 min declined more strongly than the overall completion rate, showing that accessibility often deteriorated through substantial delay before progressing to complete disconnection. These effects were concentrated in northern and northwestern Thessaloniki and expanded eastward as severity increased, whereas much of the central, southern and eastern study area retained lower levels of delay. This pattern suggests an increasing accessibility disadvantage towards the urban periphery, particularly in the northern and northwestern parts of the study area, where origins depend more strongly on the spatial distribution of the ambulance terminal and available hospitals and on a limited set of connecting road corridors. Although the northern sector already experienced comparatively lower accessibility under baseline conditions, the baseline-relative comparison (Fig. 7) identifies additional flood-related losses concentrated in northern and northwestern Thessaloniki under the modelled terminal and hospital-duty configurations. Consequently, a building can lose accessibility without local inundation when distant links on the route to the terminal or receiving hospital are flooded. Similar indirect effects beyond locally inundated roads have been reported in previous studies [14, 27].

Affected-building shares exceeded population shares in every flood scenario, indicating differences between building-based and population-weighted exposure, while the absolute population impact nevertheless remained substantial, and most delayed outcomes represented new losses of accessibility rather than a continuation of baseline disadvantage. Citywide indicators therefore conceal pronounced geographical differences in modelled accessibility. The findings indicate that flooding can amplify existing accessibility disadvantages at the urban periphery under the modelled ambulance terminal and hospital configurations, including in locations that are not themselves directly inundated. This is consistent with evidence that flood-related transport disruption produces spatially uneven reductions in emergency-service coverage [11, 13].

Hospital availability further modified these disparities, while across flood scenarios, mean 30-min accessibility ranged from 46.2% to 73.8% among the 21 duty combinations. The Agios Pavlos–Papanikolaou pair performed least effectively, whereas combinations of Papageorgiou with Gennimatas or Agios Dimitrios achieved the highest values because geographically complementary hospitals can preserve access through different parts of the network, particularly when one facility provides coverage closer to the affected northwestern sector. However, the relatively small share of journeys for which flooding changed the selected hospital shows that destination choice compensated for only part of the disruption; once critical connections were degraded or lost, switching between the two available hospitals offered limited benefit and hospital configuration therefore influenced timely accessibility but did not overcome wider network fragmentation. Previous research similarly identifies facility location and the functioning of connecting roads as joint determinants of flood-period service coverage [12, 13].

5.3 Contribution to previous research and planning implications

Previous flood-accessibility studies have shown that inundated roads can reduce emergency-service coverage and delay access to vulnerable populations, but they commonly represent accessibility as movement from an emergency station to an incident location [11, 12, 19], while EMS operations research considers dispatching, hospital selection and ambulance relocation, generally without spatially detailed flood disruption [9]. Studies incorporating both ambulance response and patient transport have further established the importance of the combined patient-delivery journey in geographical healthcare accessibility [20, 21]. The present study connects these perspectives by modelling road-specific flood effects across terminal-to-patient, patient-to-hospital and hospital-to-terminal movements, while distinguishing the two-leg patient-delivery journey, which determines spatial healthcare accessibility, from the complete three-leg service cycle, which additionally captures the return-travel component of ambulance and crew commitment.

The findings support interventions in both the road network and emergency-service organisation. Because a limited share of closures produced disproportionate effects, protecting critical corridors and junctions may preserve accessibility more effectively than measures based only on the total extent of flooded roads, while scenario-specific routing information could support flood-aware navigation and temporary ambulance staging closer to neighbourhoods that depend on vulnerable connections, although these measures would require further operational assessment because relocation and fleet availability were not explicitly modelled. In this respect, maintaining the road-based emergency-care pathway should be considered alongside other flood-resilience and climate-adaptation measures in urban planning.

Variation among hospital-duty combinations also shows that the spatial configuration of available hospitals can moderate flood impacts, and EMS and hospital planners could therefore use flood-scenario information to consider whether paired hospitals remain accessible through different parts of the network, alongside clinical capacity and specialisation. Implementation would require coordination among EMS providers, hospitals, traffic authorities and flood-management agencies, while building- and neighbourhood-level outputs can support this coordination by identifying areas where delay or disconnection persists across scenarios and where maintaining a small number of road connections is particularly important. At the road-network level, scenario-specific speed reductions and link closures can help transport and emergency-service authorities identify vulnerable connections for targeted flood-mitigation measures and contingency routing. At the neighbourhood level, the 500 m accessibility maps can support decisions on where temporary ambulance staging or additional preparedness measures may be needed, particularly in areas experiencing substantial baseline-relative accessibility losses. Building-level results can further identify potential patient-origin locations associated with delayed or infeasible journeys, although their use for real-time dispatch would require updated information on flood conditions, traffic and ambulance availability. From a preparedness perspective, EMS and hospital coordinators could use these outputs to identify vulnerable neighbourhoods and connecting roads before anticipated flood events, consider temporary ambulance staging and evaluate alternative receiving-hospital configurations, while operational dispatch decisions would continue to depend on current road conditions, vehicle availability and hospital capacity.

5.4 Limitations and future research

Several assumptions affect interpretation, as flood-related speeds were estimated using a depth-disruption function developed primarily for passenger vehicles, whereas ambulances may differ in ground clearance, stability, operating protocols and acceptable flood-depth thresholds. The pre-computed CityCAT outputs also depend on assumptions concerning rainfall, terrain, infiltration and drainage representation, while uncertainty in simulated flood depths may propagate through the road-speed and closure calculations, particularly near the 0.30 m impassability threshold, where relatively small differences in water depth can alter route feasibility and downstream accessibility. Although quantitative uncertainty propagation was not undertaken, these factors may affect the estimated magnitude and spatial distribution of accessibility losses. Maximum-depth rasters represent the most severe condition reached at each location during an event, but these maxima may not occur simultaneously across the network, so the resulting static representation may impose more extensive simultaneous road restrictions than those present at any particular stage of the event. However, this does not necessarily mean that all reported indicators represent conservative estimates of actual operational impacts, as sequential changes in road availability, flood-induced congestion and the timing of successive ambulance movements may introduce additional disruption, while roads classified as impassable based on their maximum simulated depth may remain accessible during other phases of the event. Time-dependent flood and network modelling would therefore be required to determine how these competing effects influence ambulance accessibility throughout an event.

Road conditions were represented by static morning-peak speeds and did not account for emergency-vehicle priority, behavioural responses, flood-related congestion feedback or changes in traffic demand, while these factors may affect estimated travel times and the proportion of journeys completed within 30 min under different traffic conditions. Morning-peak operating speeds may produce longer travel times than those achievable by emergency vehicles with traffic priority, whereas flood-induced congestion may introduce additional delays that are not captured by the model. Travel-time indicators were also calculated only for completed service cycles, meaning that journeys becoming infeasible under flooding no longer contribute to the reported distributions, potentially masking part of the overall deterioration in service performance. These indicators should therefore be interpreted alongside the completion and within-30-min accessibility measures, which retain all potential journeys in their denominators. Linking buildings and facilities to the nearest network node also introduces uncertainty where local approaches are incompletely represented, while the spatial distribution of accessibility losses depends partly on the fixed eastern ambulance terminal and the considered hospital locations. A geographically distributed ambulance fleet could reduce response times for peripheral origins, although patient-to-hospital accessibility would remain dependent on the available receiving facilities and flood-affected road connections.

Population was allocated to buildings from census data and footprint area, without explicitly accounting for building height, residential use or actual occupancy, while the population adjustment was based on an urban-municipality geography that differs from the study boundary. Consequently, population-weighted exposure and its difference from building-based exposure depend partly on the adopted allocation procedure, and alternative approaches incorporating residential building use and floor area could produce different estimates of the spatial distribution of affected residents. Ambulance demand was represented by potential patient origins rather than observed emergency-call locations, while age distribution and health-related differences in emergency-service demand were not considered. The 21 hospital-duty combinations were equally weighted analytical configurations, and hospital selection was based on minimum travel time without considering actual duty frequency, clinical specialisation, hospital capacity or offload delay, whereas the model also covers only the road-travel component of ambulance operations as it excludes call processing, dispatch, mobilisation, on-scene treatment, hospital handover, simultaneous incidents, fleet availability and dynamic relocation.

The transferability of the framework also depends on the availability and effective spatial resolution of the underlying datasets, as the 2 m flood-depth outputs and road-section sampling were combined with directional operating speeds defined at the original road-link level and census-derived building population estimates, while neighbourhood-scale accessibility was evaluated using 500 m × 500 m grid cells. Comparable applications could use alternative data sources, including open road-network topology and estimated travel-time weights where local traffic-model outputs are unavailable, as illustrated by weighted OpenStreetMap network analysis in Thessaloniki [46]. However, differences in network completeness, road-speed representation and the spatial resolution of population and flood-hazard data may affect the resulting accessibility estimates and their suitability for different planning applications.

Future research should incorporate observed EMS calls and travel records, demographic demand, actual hospital-duty schedules and ambulance-specific flood thresholds. Time-varying flood and traffic networks, alternative fleet deployments, hospital capacity and uncertainty propagation would extend the assessment to evolving operational conditions.

6 Conclusions

This study assessed how pluvial flooding affects ambulance travel performance and emergency healthcare accessibility in Thessaloniki. High-resolution flood depths were integrated with directional road speeds, building-level population estimates, an ambulance terminal and seven public hospitals, and scenario-specific road networks were used to model terminal-to-patient, patient-to-hospital and hospital-to-terminal movements across 21 hospital-duty combinations, while accessibility was evaluated at building, population and neighbourhood levels. The main conclusions are:

  • Flooding disrupted ambulance service trips through speed reductions, road closures and rerouting, with impacts determined strongly by the location and connectivity of affected links.

  • The patient-to-hospital leg deteriorated most, while longer return trips increased ambulance and crew commitment without causing additional disconnections.

  • Under RP100, median full-service-cycle time increased from 24.8 to 33.0 min, patient-delivery completion fell to 86.1%, and delivery within 30 min fell from 97.3% to 56.1%.

  • Delays and disconnections varied across hospital-duty combinations, while comparison with baseline identified additional flood-related losses of timely patient-delivery accessibility concentrated in northern and northwestern Thessaloniki under the modelled terminal configuration.

From an operational perspective, the findings can support EMS preparedness by identifying vulnerable service areas and critical road connections before flood events, informing flood-aware routing and temporary ambulance staging and supporting coordination with receiving hospitals when accessibility differs across duty configurations. These measures may be particularly relevant for peripheral areas with limited network redundancy, although their implementation would require consideration of actual ambulance demand, fleet availability and hospital capacity. As heavy-rainfall and pluvial-flood hazards increase in many urban settings, maintaining emergency healthcare accessibility represents an important dimension of urban resilience, and the proposed framework provides a basis for examining how flood-induced road disruption may affect emergency-service continuity and spatial healthcare access in other flood-prone urban areas.
