The University of Manchester, Manchester, United Kingdom Article metrics View details Abstract Digital transformation is increasingly recognized as an important pathway toward urban sustainability. However, how digital economy development affects integrated pollution–carbon pressure across spatially connected cities remains insufficiently understood. Using a balanced panel dataset of 266 Chinese prefecture-level cities from 2010 to 2023, this study constructs an integrated pollution–carbon pressure index (IPCB) and employs a two-way fixed-effects Spatial Durbin Model to examine the environmental effects of digital economy development. The results show that digital economy development is significantly associated with lower IPCB, indicating that digital transformation contributes to alleviating the combined environmental pressure generated by conventional pollutants and carbon emissions. Spatial-effect decomposition further reveals significant negative direct, indirect, and total effects, suggesting that the environmental benefits of digitalization extend beyond local administrative boundaries through spatial spillovers. Exploratory subgroup analyses reveal differing coefficient patterns across regions, urban-agglomeration status, and city-size groups; because formal cross-group coefficient-equality tests are not conducted, these patterns are interpreted as exploratory rather than as statistically confirmed heterogeneity. Moreover, energy consumption intensity and artificial intelligence attention strengthen the negative association between digital economy development and IPCB, highlighting the importance of local conditions in translating digital transformation into environmental improvements. The main negative association is supported by alternative measurement and permutation-based placebo tests, while the sample-exclusion, lagged-variable, and policy-based checks reveal some variation in local and spillover estimates. These results emphasize the role of digital connectivity, targeted digital upgrading, and technology-related information diffusion in supporting sustainable urban development. 1 Introduction Climate change and environmental degradation remain critical challenges to sustainable development, and recent assessments of climate science emphasize the urgency of reducing greenhouse-gas emissions and achieving long-term environmental sustainability (IPCC, 2023). The 2030 Agenda likewise identifies sustainable cities, responsible production, and climate action as interconnected objectives (United Nations, 2015). Cities are central to these challenges because they concentrate economic activities, energy consumption, infrastructure systems, and emission sources. Conventional pollutants and carbon emissions often originate from common sources, including fossil-fuel combustion, industrial production, and energy-intensive activities. Therefore, an integrated assessment of pollution and carbon-related environmental pressure provides a more comprehensive perspective for evaluating urban sustainability. The digital economy is commonly understood as an ecosystem that encompasses digital infrastructure, digital services, and digitally enabled economic activities (Bukht and Heeks, 2018). Digital development may alleviate environmental pressure through technological progress, production restructuring, and more efficient factor allocation (Hao et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024; Lin, 2024; Wang and Guo, 2023). City-level evidence also links digital economy development to lower conventional pollution emissions (Guo et al., 2023). Improvements in green total factor productivity represent another potential environmental benefit of urban digitalization (Sheng et al., 2024). However, these environmental benefits are not automatic, as digital infrastructure and data-intensive activities may increase electricity demand and generate rebound effects (Lange et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2022). Therefore, the overall environmental implications of digital transformation remain theoretically ambiguous and require further empirical investigation. Recent research increasingly examines the environmental consequences of digitalization through pollution–carbon co-control or synergy perspectives. Industrial digitalization has been associated with enhanced coordination between pollution reduction and carbon mitigation (Yi et al., 2023). Other city-level studies identify synergistic effects of digital economy development while emphasizing that these effects vary across regional development conditions (Liang et al., 2024; Zhai et al., 2024). Recent evidence from China further shows that digital economy development is associated with pollution–carbon synergy, highlighting the growing interest in coordinated pollution and carbon governance (Zheng et al., 2026). Threshold effects reported in related research indicate that the environmental benefits of digital technologies may depend on specific development stages rather than increase linearly (Han et al., 2023). Environmental policies, such as carbon-emission trading, may also simultaneously influence pollution reduction and carbon mitigation outcomes (Lu et al., 2022). Nevertheless, existing pollution–carbon synergy indicators mainly evaluate the coordination between pollution control and carbon mitigation, whereas they do not directly capture the aggregate environmental pressure jointly generated by pollutant emissions and carbon emissions. Therefore, this study defines the dependent variable as an integrated pollution–carbon pressure index rather than a synergy indicator. Another unresolved issue concerns spatial transmission. Environmental effects associated with digital economy development may extend beyond local administrative boundaries due to economic linkages, technology diffusion, and regional interactions. City-level studies show that the environmental effects of digital economy development can generate spatial spillovers (Li and Wang, 2022; Cheng et al., 2023). Regional evidence further identifies spatial interactions between digital technology development and carbon-related outcomes (Liu and Chen, 2023). Similar spatial patterns have been documented in Chinese cities regarding digital development and environmental performance (Jiang et al., 2023). Research on digital economy–pollution interactions also highlights the importance of explicitly modelling geographical dependence (Xu et al., 2022). In the context of expanding digital networks, spatial economic linkages may transmit environmental effects through broader regional connections (Wang et al., 2024). Ignoring these interactions may therefore underestimate the environmental implications of digital transformation and the importance of intercity coordination. The environmental effectiveness of digitalization may also depend on local energy-use conditions. Energy structure and digital development jointly shape carbon-emission outcomes (Li et al., 2021). Energy consumption patterns and industrial value-chain conditions further influence how digital economy development affects carbon-emission efficiency (Lyu et al., 2023). Cross-country evidence indicates that the relationship between digitalization and energy efficiency can be nonlinear (Lin and Huang, 2023). City-level research similarly emphasizes energy-efficiency optimization as an important pathway connecting digital development with carbon reduction (Wang et al., 2025). Cities with high energy consumption intensity may therefore face greater environmental pressure while also possessing greater potential for digital monitoring, intelligent scheduling, and technological upgrading. Artificial intelligence attention represents another contextual condition affecting the environmental outcomes of digital transformation. AI is expected to reshape organizational decision-making and production systems, although its realized effects depend on technology adoption and governance conditions (Makridakis, 2017). Existing studies suggest that AI applications influence urban carbon-emission performance (Chen et al., 2022), while research on automation indicates that technological advancement may generate both environmental benefits and potential challenges (Xu and Song, 2023). The environmental consequences of AI also vary across technological applications and regional contexts (Dong et al., 2024). Industrial AI applications can support carbon reduction through more accurate monitoring and operational optimization (Alghieth, 2025). Unlike direct AI adoption, artificial intelligence attention captures regional information demand, media exposure, and social awareness. Higher AI attention may reduce information barriers, enhance technological acceptance, and strengthen the capacity of enterprises and governments to implement intelligent environmental-management practices. China provides an appropriate empirical setting because its cities have experienced rapid digital transformation alongside substantial pressure to control conventional pollution and carbon emissions. Considerable variation across cities in digital infrastructure, energy intensity, AI attention, industrial structure, city size, and regional development conditions provides useful heterogeneity for identifying local environmental effects, spatial spillovers, and contextual mechanisms. Against this background, this study uses panel data for 266 Chinese prefecture-level cities from 2010 to 2023 and applies a two-way fixed-effects Spatial Durbin Model to examine the environmental effects of digital economy development. Specifically, it decomposes direct, indirect, and total spatial effects, examines the moderating roles of energy consumption intensity and artificial intelligence attention, and conducts exploratory heterogeneity analyses across regions, urban-agglomeration status, and city-size groups. This study makes three contributions. First, it develops an integrated pollution–carbon pressure index that captures the combined environmental pressure generated by conventional pollutants and carbon emissions, while distinguishing this aggregate pressure perspective from existing pollution–carbon synergy evaluations. Second, it incorporates spatial dependence into the analytical framework and identifies both local environmental effects and cross-city spillovers associated with digital transformation. Third, it identifies energy consumption intensity and artificial intelligence attention as contextual factors that shape the extent to which digitalization contributes to environmental improvement. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the theoretical framework and hypotheses. Section 3 describes the data, variables, and methods. Section 4 reports the empirical findings. Section 5 discusses their theoretical and empirical implications. Section 6 presents the conclusions, policy implications, limitations, and directions for future research. 2 Theoretical framework and research hypotheses 2.1 Digital economy, integrated pollution–carbon pressure, and spatial spillovers The digital economy can alleviate integrated pollution–carbon pressure through improved resource allocation, production optimization, and enhanced environmental governance. At the city level, digital economy development has been associated with broader improvements in green development performance (Xiao et al., 2023). Recent studies also link digital transformation with carbon-emission reduction and improved carbon efficiency (Liu et al., 2025). By reducing information asymmetry and transaction costs, digital technologies can improve factor matching efficiency and reduce unnecessary energy and material consumption. Digital technologies enable firms to monitor energy use, optimize production processes, improve equipment operation, and identify inefficient production activities. Energy-consumption management and value-chain adjustment represent important channels through which digitalization can enhance carbon efficiency (Lyu et al., 2023). Digital investment can further support energy-efficiency improvements when integrated into production and management systems (Wang et al., 2025). In addition, digital transformation may stimulate green innovation and strengthen pollution-control capacity (Peng et al., 2023). Beyond production processes, digitalization can improve environmental monitoring and regulatory effectiveness by supporting real-time monitoring, pollution-source identification, early-warning systems, data sharing, and more targeted supervision. Digital platforms may facilitate collaborative environmental governance among governments, enterprises, and the public (Liu and Ma, 2024). Evidence from smart-city pilot programs indicates that urban digital transformation can influence local environmental outcomes (Wu et al., 2023). City-level studies further identify significant associations between digital economy development and environmental pollution reduction (Yuan et al., 2024). These mechanisms suggest that digital economy development may contribute to reducing integrated pollution–carbon pressure. H1: Digital economy development is negatively associated with integrated pollution–carbon pressure in cities. The environmental implications of digitalization may also extend beyond administrative boundaries. Spatial analyses of Chinese cities identify cross-city relationships between digital economy development and carbon intensity (Cheng et al., 2023). Dynamic city-level evidence further indicates that digital development can influence carbon-emission reduction beyond local jurisdictions (Li and Wang, 2022). Spatial dependence has also been documented in digital economy–pollution interactions (Xu et al., 2022). Knowledge diffusion, infrastructure connectivity, industrial linkages, and intergovernmental learning provide potential channels through which digitalization-related environmental effects may spread across regions. Therefore, ignoring spatial interactions may underestimate the environmental implications of digital transformation. H2: Digital economy development exhibits negative spatial spillover effects on integrated pollution–carbon pressure. 2.2 Moderating effect of energy consumption intensity Energy consumption intensity reflects the amount of energy input required to generate economic output. Higher energy consumption intensity generally indicates lower energy-use efficiency, stronger dependence on energy inputs, and greater environmental pressure from pollution and carbon emissions. The environmental consequences of digitalization are closely associated with local energy structures and consumption conditions (Li et al., 2021). Energy consumption characteristics also influence the relationship between digital economy development and carbon-emission efficiency (Lyu et al., 2023). Cities with higher energy consumption intensity may have greater potential for digital technologies to generate environmental improvements because inefficient energy use provides more opportunities for optimization. The relationship between digitalization and energy efficiency may vary across different technological and economic contexts (Lin and Huang, 2023). Digital monitoring, intelligent scheduling, process optimization, and equipment upgrading may produce stronger environmental benefits in cities where energy-use inefficiencies remain significant (Wang et al., 2025). This argument does not imply that high energy consumption intensity itself is environmentally beneficial. Rather, it suggests that the marginal environmental gains from digital transformation may be greater in areas where inefficient energy utilization and energy-intensive production create more room for technological improvement. H3: Energy consumption intensity positively moderates the negative association between digital economy development and IPCB. 2.3 Moderating effect of artificial intelligence attention Artificial intelligence attention reflects regional awareness, information exposure, and public interest regarding AI technologies. Unlike direct measures of AI adoption, AI attention captures the information and cognitive environment surrounding emerging technologies rather than their actual deployment. AI has the potential to reshape organizational decision-making and production systems, but its environmental benefits depend on complementary capabilities, institutional conditions, and application scenarios (Makridakis, 2017). Higher AI attention may facilitate the diffusion of technology-related knowledge, reduce information barriers, and improve social acceptance of intelligent applications. It may also encourage governments and enterprises to pursue data-driven management and intelligent upgrading strategies. Existing studies show that AI applications can improve carbon-emission performance through prediction, optimization, and operational management (Chen et al., 2022). Meanwhile, automation may influence environmental outcomes through changes in production efficiency and energy demand (Xu and Song, 2023). However, AI attention itself does not directly reduce pollution or carbon emissions and should not be regarded as a substitute for technological investment. The environmental effects of AI vary according to application types and regional capacities (Dong et al., 2024). When combined with industrial monitoring, process optimization, and operational control, AI-related technological development may provide additional opportunities for reducing carbon footprints (Alghieth, 2025). Therefore, AI attention is conceptualized as an information, policy, and market environment that may strengthen the capacity of cities to transform digital development into environmental improvements. H4: Artificial intelligence attention strengthens the negative association between digital economy development and integrated pollution–carbon pressure. 3 Research design 3.1 Sample selection and data sources This study constructs a balanced panel dataset covering 266 prefecture-level and above cities in China from 2010 to 2023, resulting in 3,724 city-year observations. Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan are excluded due to differences in statistical systems and limited data comparability. Since the artificial intelligence attention index is available from 2011 onward, the moderation analysis involving this variable is conducted using the 2011–2023 subsample. The data are collected from multiple official and widely used sources, including the China City Statistical Yearbook, China Urban Construction Statistical Yearbook, China Energy Statistical Yearbook, municipal statistical yearbooks, statistical bulletins, and the Peking University Digital Financial Inclusion Index of China. The artificial intelligence attention index is obtained from a licensed research database that provides annual city-level measurements based on the Baidu Search Index and Baidu News Index. To address limited missing observations, industrial wastewater discharge is estimated using the average growth rate of the previous 3 years, while other isolated missing values are supplemented through linear interpolation. Cities with consecutive or substantial missing information are excluded from the sample. Because the artificial intelligence attention series begins in 2011, observations for 2010 are not interpolated. 3.2 Variable definitions and measurement 3.2.1 Dependent variable: integrated pollution–carbon pressure The dependent variable is the integrated pollution–carbon pressure index (IPCB), which measures the aggregate environmental pressure jointly generated by conventional pollutants and carbon emissions. A higher IPCB value indicates greater environmental pressure, whereas a lower value reflects an overall reduction in pollution- and carbon-related environmental pressure. IPCB is not intended to measure pollution–carbon synergy; instead, it captures the aggregate environmental pressure jointly generated by pollutant emissions and carbon emissions. IPCB is constructed in three steps. First, industrial wastewater discharge, sulfur dioxide emissions, and smoke/dust emissions are converted to a direction-consistent common scale so that larger values represent heavier pollution pressure. The entropy-weighting method determines the objective weights, and TOPSIS is then used to obtain the composite pollution index (POL). Second, urban carbon-emission boundaries follow established city-level accounting practice in China (Cong et al., 2014). Emissions are estimated from electricity, natural gas, liquefied petroleum gas, transportation, and centralized heating by multiplying consumption or activity data by the corresponding emission factors. The emission factors and conversion principles primarily follow the 2006 IPCC Guidelines (IPCC, 2006). The current IPCB measure does not separately identify pollution or carbon emissions specifically attributable to the generation, collection, recycling, dismantling, treatment, and disposal of digital or electronic waste. Although some related environmental burdens may be indirectly reflected in aggregate urban pollution and energy-use statistics, their digital-waste-specific contribution cannot be isolated with the available city-level data. This measurement boundary is discussed further in Section 6.3. 3.2.2 Core explanatory variable: digital economy development The core explanatory variable is digital economy development (DIG). The multidimensional measurement framework follows the view that the digital economy encompasses digital infrastructure, digital services, and digitally enabled economic activities (Bukht and Heeks, 2018). The city-level indicator framework draws on established Chinese digital-economy measurement practice (Zhao et al., 2020). To better capture the multidimensional characteristics of urban digital development, the framework also incorporates the Peking University Digital Financial Inclusion Index of China, which has been widely adopted in studies of digital transformation and its environmental and economic impacts (Li and Zhou, 2021). The indicator system consists of three dimensions: digital industrialization, industrial digitization, and digital services and governance. The comprehensive DIG index aggregates the indicators across these three dimensions. Digital industrialization is measured by internet penetration, internet-related employment, telecommunications business revenue per capita, and mobile internet usage. Industrial digitization is measured by patent applications and patents granted, following Zhao et al. (2020), as proxies for local innovation capability and industrial digital transformation potential. Although these patent-based indicators capture observable innovation outputs, they remain relatively coarse proxies for the depth of firm-level digital adoption. Digital services and governance are measured by the ratio of science and technology expenditure to local general public budget expenditure and the Peking University Digital Financial Inclusion Index of China. Urban-agglomeration membership follows the national urban-agglomeration classification used in the source data, covering major agglomerations such as the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei Region, the Yangtze River Delta, and the Pearl River Delta. Regional subsamples follow the official classification of eastern, central, western, and northeastern China. These classifications are used only for exploratory subsample regressions. A significantly positive Moran’s indicates that cities with similar levels of the corresponding variable tend to cluster spatially. 3.3.3 Spatial durbin model and spatial weight matrices Given the significant spatial dependence of IPCB, a two-way fixed-effects Spatial Durbin Model (SDM) is employed to estimate both local effects and spatial spillover effects within a unified framework. The model is estimated by quasi-maximum likelihood (QML) using the xsmle procedure in Stata (Belotti et al., 2017). The reported coefficient standard errors are the default model-based standard errors from the QML estimation rather than city-clustered, two-way-clustered, or spatial-HAC standard errors. Spatial dependence in the conditional mean is explicitly modeled through the spatially lagged dependent variable and spatially lagged explanatory variables. This structural treatment of spatial dependence does not, however, imply that the variance–covariance estimator is robust to arbitrary residual cross-sectional dependence or two-way clustering. Accordingly, statistical significance is interpreted with appropriate caution, and this inferential limitation is acknowledged in Section 6.3. IPCB has a mean of 11.856 and a standard deviation of 1.733, indicating substantial cross-city variation in the integrated pollution–carbon pressure. Previous studies generally examine whether digital transformation improves the coordination between pollution control and carbon mitigation through synergy-oriented indicators (Yi et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2024). Such measures focus on the degree of coordination between environmental objectives rather than the overall pressure jointly generated by pollutant emissions and carbon emissions. Existing research also suggests that the environmental consequences of digital development may vary across different conditions (Zhai et al., 2024). In contrast, this study constructs IPCB to capture the aggregate environmental pressure associated with conventional pollutants and carbon emissions. Therefore, the negative relationship between DIG and IPCB indicates that digital economy development is associated with a reduction in overall pollution–carbon pressure rather than an improvement in pollution–carbon coordination. This distinction complements previous evidence linking digitalization separately to pollution reduction (Guo et al., 2023) and carbon mitigation (Chen et al., 2024), and provides a broader perspective for evaluating the environmental implications of urban digital transformation. 5.2 Spatial transmission and differentiated urban conditions The larger absolute magnitude of the indirect effect compared with the direct effect highlights the spatially connected nature of the digital economy–environment relationship. Digital transformation may influence environmental outcomes beyond individual cities through regional economic linkages, technological diffusion, and spatial interactions. This finding is consistent with previous evidence showing that digital economy development generates spatial effects on carbon-related outcomes (Cheng et al., 2023; Li and Wang, 2022). Research on regional carbon dynamics further emphasizes the importance of considering spatio-temporal interactions rather than focusing solely on local effects (Liu and Chen, 2023). The exploratory subsample analysis shows different coefficient patterns across regions, urban-agglomeration status, and city-size groups. These patterns may reflect differences in digital infrastructure, economic development, and technological absorption capacity across cities. However, because formal cross-group coefficient-equality tests and subgroup-specific effect decompositions are not conducted, these differences should be interpreted as exploratory evidence rather than confirmed heterogeneity. 5.3 Contextual moderation and interpretation The moderating results suggest that the environmental effects of digital economy development depend on local contextual conditions. The negative interaction between DIG and EC indicates that energy consumption intensity strengthens the negative relationship between digital economy development and IPCB. This finding suggests that cities with higher energy intensity may have greater opportunities to improve energy efficiency through digital monitoring, process optimization, and technological upgrading. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence showing that the environmental consequences of digitalization depend on energy-use characteristics (Xu et al., 2022). The nonlinear relationship between digitalization and energy efficiency further highlights the importance of contextual conditions in shaping digital environmental effects (Lin and Huang, 2023). The negative interaction between DIG and AIA indicates that artificial intelligence attention represents another contextual factor influencing the digital economy–environment relationship. Higher AI-related information exposure may facilitate knowledge diffusion and increase demand for intelligent applications; however, it does not necessarily represent actual AI deployment. Existing research suggests that AI applications can influence carbon-emission performance (Chen et al., 2022), while their environmental impacts vary across technological applications and regional conditions (Dong et al., 2024). Therefore, the environmental benefits of AI-related development depend not only on information attention but also on effective technological adoption and supporting institutional conditions. 6 Conclusions and policy implications 6.1 Conclusions Using panel data from 266 Chinese prefecture-level cities during 2010–2023, this study investigates the relationship between digital economy development and integrated pollution–carbon pressure (IPCB) using a two-way fixed-effects Spatial Durbin Model. Unlike synergy-oriented indicators, IPCB captures the aggregate environmental pressure jointly generated by conventional pollutants and carbon emissions. The results show that digital economy development is significantly associated with lower IPCB. Spatial-effect decomposition reveals significant negative direct, indirect, and total effects, indicating that the environmental benefits of digital transformation extend beyond individual cities through spatial linkages. In addition, energy consumption intensity and artificial intelligence attention strengthen the negative relationship between digital economy development and IPCB. Exploratory subsample analyses further show differing coefficient patterns across regional and urban contexts; these patterns are not interpreted as statistically confirmed cross-group differences. Overall, the findings highlight that digital transformation represents a spatially connected pathway for reducing urban pollution–carbon pressure, while its environmental effectiveness depends on local energy conditions and technological and institutional conditions. 6.2 Policy implications First, policymakers should promote digital infrastructure development while reducing regional digital disparities. Since digital economy development generates spatial spillovers, digital upgrading strategies should strengthen intercity connectivity and support less-developed regions in participating in digital–green transformation. Second, regional coordination should be enhanced through cross-city digital platforms, environmental data sharing, and collaborative governance mechanisms to maximize the spatial diffusion of digital environmental benefits. Third, digital transformation should prioritize energy-intensive cities and industries. Improving intelligent energy monitoring, production-process optimization, and energy-efficiency management can help convert digital development into measurable environmental improvements. Fourth, governments should improve the dissemination and application of AI-related knowledge rather than relying only on information attention. Strengthening cooperation among technology providers, enterprises, and environmental regulators can facilitate the translation of AI-related knowledge into practical environmental management solutions. 6.3 Limitations and future research This study has several limitations. First, AIA is measured using Baidu search and news indices, which capture information exposure and social attention but do not directly measure actual AI adoption. Future research could incorporate AI patents, enterprise-level adoption data, and investment information. Second, IPCB measures aggregate pollution–carbon pressure rather than coordination between pollution control and carbon mitigation. Future studies could compare different measurement perspectives by incorporating separate pollution, carbon, and coordination indicators. Third, the current IPCB framework does not separately identify carbon emissions and pollution specifically associated with the generation, collection, recycling, dismantling, treatment, and disposal of digital or electronic waste. Although some of these environmental burdens may be indirectly reflected in aggregate urban pollution and energy-use statistics, the available city-level data do not allow their digital-waste-specific contribution to be isolated. Future research could extend the IPCB framework by incorporating city-level electronic-waste generation, collection, recycling and treatment volumes, treatment-related pollutant emissions, and life-cycle carbon emissions from recycling and disposal when consistent data become available. These indicators could either be incorporated into an expanded IPCB measurement framework or examined as an additional environmental-pressure channel within the spatial econometric model. Fourth, the exploratory subsample analysis does not include formal cross-group coefficient tests, and the Broadband China analysis is supplementary rather than a complete causal identification design. Future research could use more rigorous causal methods and further explore firm- and industry-level mechanisms. Fifth, patent applications and patent grants capture observable innovation outputs and local innovation potential, but they are relatively coarse proxies for industrial digitization because they do not directly measure the depth or intensity of firm-level digital adoption. Future research could incorporate firm-level software investment, digital equipment use, or sector-specific digital adoption measures when comparable city-level data become available. Sixth, the primary SDM inference relies on model-based standard errors from the QML estimation. Although the SDM explicitly models spatial dependence in the conditional mean, these standard errors are not designed to address arbitrary residual cross-sectional dependence or two-way clustering. The reported significance levels should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. Future research could assess the sensitivity of the estimates using alternative covariance estimators, particularly when longer panel dimensions become available.
