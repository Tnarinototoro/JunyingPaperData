Urban Policy Automotive Infrastructure and Services Integrated Geography Intelligence Infrastructure IT Risk Management Smart Infrastructure 1 Introduction Cities are central to contemporary civilisation and increasingly function as complex socio-technical systems in which infrastructure networks, environmental resources, economic activities, and human interactions are closely interconnected [ 1 ]. Rapid urbanisation has intensified these interdependencies and increased exposure to infrastructure failures, environmental pressures, resource constraints, transportation disruptions, and other systemic risks [ 1 , 2 ]. Such challenges are no longer adequately understood as isolated hazards; rather, they can interact across sectors and spatial scales, producing cascading effects on infrastructure performance, environmental sustainability, economic productivity, public health, and human well-being [ 3 , 4 ]. Urban resilience therefore requires more than preventing individual accidents or infrastructure failures. Cities must simultaneously manage safety, health, environmental quality, water services, energy reliability, transportation, and resource circularity under conditions of uncertainty and interdependence. In this framework, resource circularity encompasses waste management, resource recovery, recycling, and the efficient recirculation of materials within urban systems. However, these functions are commonly addressed through separate institutional, regulatory, and operational arrangements. Such fragmentation can limit the ability to identify shared causal pathways, recognise cross-domain vulnerabilities, anticipate cascading effects, and coordinate preventive action [ 2 , 4 ]. Structured management systems have provided established approaches for managing specific organisational objectives, including occupational health and safety, environmental protection, energy performance, quality, and circular economy principles. Although such systems can strengthen organisational performance and risk management, their application generally remains centred on organisational boundaries rather than integrated city-scale infrastructure governance [ 5 , 6 , 7 ]. Consequently, the relationship between organisational management practices and broader urban infrastructure outcomes remains insufficiently developed. A further gap concerns the use of precursor information for cross-domain urban risk learning. Near misses are well established in safety management as sources of information about latent weaknesses and potential accident pathways [ 8 , 9 ]. However, their conceptual extension to interconnected urban infrastructure requires careful delimitation from hazards, deviations, alerts, incidents, and other risk signals. There is therefore an opportunity to examine how appropriately classified near-miss information can complement multidimensional risk assessment without assuming that observations from different domains are directly equivalent. This Perspective proposes the Integrated Risk–Safety–Sustainability (IRSS) Framework for Resilient Urban Infrastructure and Improved City Life. The framework integrates seven urban domains - safety, health, water, environment, energy, transportation, and waste and resource circularity - within a common risk-information and decision architecture. It positions multidimensional risk as the principal integrating construct and near-miss intelligence as a specific source of precursor information for identifying vulnerabilities, interdependencies, and potential cascading effects. The framework further connects risk identification and interpretation with risk-informed decision-making, adaptive governance, and continuous learning, providing a conceptual foundation for future empirical validation, indicator development, and context-specific implementation. 2 Methodological approach This study adopts a conceptual and integrative review approach to develop the IRSS Framework for Resilient Urban Infrastructure and Improved City Life [ 10 , 11 ]. Rather than testing predefined hypotheses, the study seeks to synthesise knowledge from multiple disciplines and develop a conceptual framework capable of addressing complex urban infrastructure challenges through a unified systems perspective. The review was conducted as an integrative conceptual synthesis rather than as a formal systematic review; and therefore, did not follow a formal systematic-review protocol such as PRISMA. The literature was identified through targeted searches of multidisciplinary scholarly databases of Scopus/Web of Science/Google Scholar and authoritative institutional and standards sources. The literature identification and selection were guided by the objectives of the study and the conceptual domains required for development of the IRSS Framework. Searches used combinations of key concepts including “urban resilience,” “urban infrastructure,” “infrastructure interdependencies,” “systemic risk,” “risk governance,” “near miss,” “near-miss intelligence,” “safety,” “environmental sustainability,” “energy,” “transportation,” “water,” “public health,” “circular economy,” “systems thinking,” “socio-technical systems,” and “integrated management.” The search primarily considered literature published during [2015–2026], while seminal and foundational publications outside this period were retained where they provided concepts directly relevant to the framework. Relevant literature was considered when it addressed one or more of the principal themes underlying the framework, including urban resilience, risk management, safety, environmental sustainability, energy, transportation, water, public health, circular economy, systems thinking, socio-technical systems, risk governance, and integrated management. Priority was given to peer-reviewed scholarly literature and authoritative international standards that provided established concepts, principles, models, or approaches relevant to interconnected urban infrastructure systems. Literature focused on isolated technical issues without clear conceptual relevance to risk, safety, sustainability, resilience, infrastructure interdependencies, or governance was not prioritised for the conceptual synthesis. The selected literature was subsequently organised thematically and examined to identify recurring concepts, relationships, and principles spanning urban resilience, safety management, risk governance, environmental management, energy systems, transportation systems, water systems, public health, circular economy, and infrastructure sustainability. Particular attention was given to studies addressing systems thinking, socio-technical systems, urban resilience, integrated management systems, near-miss analysis, sustainability transitions, and risk governance [ 2 , 12 , 13 ]. Relevant international management and circular-economy standards, including ISO 45,001, ISO 14,001, ISO 50,001, ISO 9001, and ISO 59020:2024, were also examined to identify complementary principles applicable to urban infrastructure management. The ISO 59,000 series was considered as a family of circular-economy standards, with ISO 59020:2024 specifically examined for its relevance to circularity measurement and assessment. The framework development process consisted of four iterative stages, following established approaches for conceptual model development and theory synthesis [ 10 , 14 ]. First, key urban infrastructure domains and their associated challenges were identified through the literature synthesis. Second, common risk interactions, interdependencies, and recurring themes across safety, health, water, environmental, energy, transportation, and resource-management systems were examined. The identified concepts were then compared across disciplinary domains to determine areas of convergence, complementarity, and potential integration. Third, the concept of near-miss intelligence was critically examined and conceptually extended from occupational safety management to a broader urban systems perspective. Finally, insights from systems thinking, resilience theory, sustainability science, risk governance, and integrated management approaches were synthesised into a unified conceptual framework. This synthesis focused on identifying complementary concepts, common mechanisms, interdependencies, and potential linkages across disciplinary boundaries rather than simply aggregating findings from individual domains. The seven urban domains - safety, health, water, environment, energy, transportation, and resource circularity - were derived from recurring infrastructure functions, risk themes, and interdependencies identified across the selected literature. Water was treated as a distinct domain because water supply, wastewater, drainage, and associated infrastructure constitute essential urban services with direct linkages to health, environmental quality, resource efficiency, and resilience. The four framework layers were subsequently derived by mapping these seven domains onto a common information and decision pathway comprising (i) the urban socio-technical system, (ii) multidimensional risk identification and near-miss intelligence, (iii) risk assessment and cross-domain interpretation, and (iv) risk-informed decision-making with adaptive governance and continuous learning. The resulting IRSS Framework represents a theory-building contribution that integrates multiple disciplines into a common risk-based architecture [ 10 , 14 ]. Rather than providing empirical validation, the framework offers a conceptual foundation for future empirical research, indicator development, case-study applications, simulation, and urban policy implementation. The framework is intended to facilitate interdisciplinary dialogue and provide a structured basis for investigating integrated approaches to urban resilience, sustainability, infrastructure performance, and quality of life. 3 Fragmentation of Urban infrastructure management Urban infrastructure comprises interdependent safety, health, water, environmental, energy, transportation, and waste and resource-circularity systems that collectively influence resilience, sustainability, and quality of life. Despite these interdependencies, they are commonly governed through specialised institutions, regulations, and performance frameworks. Such fragmentation can constrain information sharing, obscure system dependencies, and limit the capacity to identify cross-sectoral vulnerabilities and cascading effects [ 2 , 4 , 12 ]. Safety and health provide a common human-centred dimension across urban systems. Safety management generally emphasises hazard identification, risk control, and incident prevention [ 15 ]. Management-system integration can also contribute to organisational coordination and operational efficiency [ 16 ]. Public-health outcomes in urban settings depend on multisectoral action, coordinated urban governance, and civic engagement to address shared determinants of health, well-being, and equity [ 17 ]. However, public-health systems, infrastructure planning, and environmental governance may operate separately, limiting opportunities to identify shared risks and implement coordinated interventions. Climate-related impacts and adaptation challenges further demonstrate the importance of integrated planning across urban systems [ 18 ]. Water systems connect service reliability, public health, environmental quality, energy use, drainage, and resource recovery. Pipeline failures, leakage, pressure anomalies, drainage blockages, treatment deviations, and service interruptions may therefore have consequences extending beyond the immediate water system. However, water services are generally managed through specialised operational and regulatory structures, which can limit integration with broader urban risk governance. Recent work has emphasised the need for integrated, adaptive, and technology-enabled water governance that connects resource resilience, environmental protection, infrastructure planning, and cross-sectoral decision-making [ 19 , 20 ]. Environmental, energy, transportation, and resource systems present similar integration challenges. Environmental management addresses pollution, ecosystem protection, climate pressures, and resource consumption, while energy systems underpin transportation, water services, waste management, industry, and essential public services [ 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 ]. Transportation systems simultaneously influence accessibility, safety, environmental exposure, public health, and economic activity, yet mobility planning commonly prioritises efficiency and capacity while related risks are addressed through separate mechanisms [ 25 , 26 ]. Waste management and resource recovery likewise contribute to circularity, resource efficiency, and environmental performance but are frequently separated from safety, energy, and infrastructure planning [ 21 , 27 ]. The resulting fragmentation is particularly significant because disturbances in one domain can affect others. Energy disruptions may impair water and transportation services; water-system failures may affect health and environmental quality; and transportation disturbances may generate safety, health, and environmental consequences. Thus, while specialised management remains necessary, urban resilience requires mechanisms capable of interpreting domain-specific information in relation to wider infrastructure dependencies. This fragmentation provides the rationale for the IRSS Framework and for examining whether near misses, deviations, alerts, and other precursor information can contribute to integrated, cross-domain risk governance. 4 Multi-dimensional risk and near misses in urban systems Urban infrastructure operates as an interconnected socio-technical system in which risks can originate in one domain and propagate through dependencies to other infrastructure, environmental, health, and social systems. Such interactions may involve multiple hazards, vulnerabilities, exposures, and consequences rather than a single failure pathway [ 3 , 4 ]. Within the IRSS Framework, multidimensional risk therefore refers to interactions among the seven urban domains—safety, health, water, environment, energy, transportation, and resource circularity—and their potential effects on essential services, infrastructure performance, public well-being, environmental quality, and urban resilience. Near-miss information provides an additional source of precursor information within this risk environment. In conventional safety management, near misses support learning from events in which an undesirable outcome could reasonably have occurred but was avoided. The IRSS Framework extends this learning principle conceptually to urban infrastructure while recognising that precursor events differ across domains. A near miss is therefore defined as an unplanned event, condition, or interaction that had a credible potential to result in an undesirable consequence, but in which that consequence was avoided through intervention, existing controls or barriers, chance, or other mitigating circumstances. Near-miss intelligence refers to the systematic reporting, classification, analysis, interpretation, and use of such information to identify vulnerabilities, precursor pathways, interdependencies, and opportunities for preventive or adaptive action. Importantly, not every anomaly, deviation, outage, non-compliance, or warning signal constitutes a near miss. Classification requires evidence of a credible pathway to an undesirable consequence. This distinction prevents the proposed concept from becoming synonymous with all forms of urban risk information. 4.1 Conceptual delimitation of near misses and related risk signals For cross-domain application, near misses must be distinguished from related forms of risk information. Table 1 establishes the principal categories used in the IRSS Framework and illustrates their conceptual boundaries through representative examples. A hazard represents a source or condition with potential to cause harm; risk concerns the potential for an adverse outcome and its consequences; a deviation represents departure from an intended operating condition; an alert is a warning generated through monitoring or detection; an incident involves an actual adverse outcome; and a leading indicator provides information about conditions associated with future performance or risk. A near miss differs by requiring both a credible potential for an undesirable outcome and the absence of that outcome. For example, abnormal pipeline pressure is a deviation and an automated high-pressure alarm is an alert; neither is automatically a near miss. If the pressure condition creates a credible potential for pipeline failure or service disruption and the outcome is avoided through intervention or existing barriers, it may be classified as a near miss. The same principle applies to traffic conflicts, energy-system anomalies, drainage blockages, and emission deviations, which should be classified according to their potential consequences and causal pathways. Other categories can nevertheless complement near-miss intelligence within the integrated risk-information architecture. 4.2 Cross-domain application and cascading risk The value of near-miss intelligence in urban systems lies in its potential to reveal conditions preceding undesirable outcomes across interconnected infrastructure domains. Established safety research demonstrates the value of learning from precursor events that expose weaknesses in controls and barriers [ 9 , 13 ]. Within the IRSS Framework, this principle may be extended to transportation conflicts, water-service precursors, energy abnormalities, environmental control deviations, health-related exposure events, and resource-management failures, provided that the proposed near-miss criterion is satisfied. Near misses from different domains should not, however, be treated as directly equivalent or combined into a single undifferentiated count. Their significance depends on system characteristics, exposure, potential consequence, controls, and causal pathways. Instead, domain-specific information is interpreted through a common risk architecture to identify recurring patterns, latent conditions, interdependencies, and potential cascading effects. For example, an energy-system disruption may affect water supply, transportation, healthcare, and communication services, while a drainage failure may generate environmental and public-health consequences. The framework therefore links precursor information with broader multidimensional risk assessment rather than treating each event as an isolated domain-specific occurrence [ 4 ]. 4.3 Limitations and conditions for using Near-miss intelligence Cross-domain application of near-miss intelligence is subject to reporting and measurement limitations. Reporting can be influenced by organisational culture, public awareness, accessibility of reporting mechanisms, monitoring capability, perceived severity, and institutional responsibilities. Consequently, differences in reported near-miss frequency should not automatically be interpreted as differences in underlying risk. Under-reporting, inconsistent terminology, subjective classification, and false alarms may further affect reliability. Observations from different domains should therefore not be directly aggregated without appropriate contextualisation. Meaningful comparison would require consistent classification criteria and consideration of consequence severity, exposure or activity denominators, reporting completeness, and contextual conditions. The IRSS Framework consequently proposes conceptual integration rather than direct numerical aggregation. Domain-specific observations retain their characteristics while being interpreted through a common risk architecture. Near-miss intelligence is consequently presented as a potential early-warning and learning mechanism rather than a validated urban performance indicator. Establishing reporting protocols, classification thresholds, detection methods, normalisation procedures, and empirical relationships with subsequent urban outcomes remains a priority for future research. 4.4 Operationalisation and decision logic of the IRSS framework The IRSS Framework is intended to provide a structured risk-information and decision-support process rather than remain solely a conceptual architecture. Operational inputs may include near misses, hazards, deviations, incidents, alerts, infrastructure-performance data, environmental and health observations, service disruptions, and other relevant monitoring information. These inputs are classified according to domain, location, time, affected system, potential consequence, and relationship with existing controls or infrastructure dependencies. Near-miss information is classified using the criteria established in Sect. 4.1 . The identified signals are then assessed using a context-specific risk-assessment process considering likelihood, potential consequence, exposure, vulnerability, and interdependency. Spatial and temporal characteristics may range from facility or network level to neighbourhood and city level, and from event-based observations to continuous or periodic monitoring. Risks can subsequently be prioritised according to potential severity, exposure, persistence, and cross-domain or cascading effects. The framework does not prescribe universal scoring methods, weighting factors, or alert thresholds because these require calibration to local infrastructure characteristics, data availability, and governance arrangements. Risk prioritisation informs preventive, corrective, or adaptive interventions by relevant infrastructure operators, municipal authorities, public-health agencies, emergency-management bodies, or other responsible stakeholders. The effectiveness of interventions could then be monitored through domain-specific indicators and operational observations, with the resulting information fed back into subsequent assessment and decision-making. The resulting logic can be represented as: Risk signal detection → classification → risk assessment → prioritisation → responsible decision-making → intervention → outcome monitoring → feedback and learning . 5 Need for a system-based integrated approach The preceding review indicates that urban infrastructure domains—including safety, health, water, environmental management, energy, transportation, and resource management—are closely interconnected but are frequently managed through specialised institutional and operational structures. Such fragmentation can limit recognition of interdependencies, cascading risks, and opportunities for coordinated intervention [ 2 , 4 ]. A system-based approach is therefore required to connect these domains within a common risk and decision-making perspective. 5.1 Limitations of current approaches and existing integration efforts Traditional management approaches generally address individual operational domains and may not adequately account for interactions among infrastructure systems, environmental conditions, energy networks, transportation, and resource flows [ 4 ]. Integrated management systems have addressed some of these limitations at the organisational level by combining quality, environmental, occupational health and safety, and related management functions [ 16 , 28 ]. At the urban scale, research has increasingly addressed sustainability–resilience integration [ 29 ], interdisciplinary urban systems [ 30 ], urban resilience [ 31 ], cross-sectoral crisis governance [ 32 ], and integrated urban climate action [ 33 ]. However, these approaches differ in purpose and scope and do not specifically establish a common operational architecture linking multidimensional urban risk with near-miss intelligence. 5.2 Systems thinking and management system integration Systems thinking provides a conceptual basis for examining interactions, feedback, interdependencies, and emergent behaviour rather than treating infrastructure components independently [ 34 , 35 ]. Similarly, the integration of management-system principles can facilitate information sharing, coordinated resource use, and risk-informed decision-making [ 15 , 16 ]. Extending these principles from organisational settings to interconnected urban infrastructure provides a basis for integrating safety, sustainability, resilience, and operational performance within a broader governance architecture. 5.3 Urban resilience requirements Urban resilience requires infrastructure and governance systems capable of anticipating, absorbing, adapting to, and recovering from disturbances while maintaining essential functions. This requires consideration of interconnected safety, health, environmental, energy, transportation, resource, and governance dimensions rather than isolated sectoral performance [ 22 , 36 , 37 ]. These requirements provide the rationale for a framework capable of linking risk information across urban infrastructure domains. 5.4 Research gap and positioning of the IRSS framework Although substantial literature addresses urban resilience, infrastructure interdependencies, systemic risk governance, integrated management, and smart-city decision-making, their integration through a common risk-information architecture remains limited. In particular, near misses are predominantly treated as occupational safety learning mechanisms rather than as potentially useful precursor information across interconnected urban systems [ 8 , 13 ]. Rather than treating these domains as separate management objectives, the framework organises them within a common risk-information and decision architecture. The framework uses multidimensional risk as its principal integrating construct. Risk information may originate from near misses, hazards, deviations, incidents, alerts, monitoring data, and other operational observations. These signals are classified and interpreted in relation to potential consequences, exposure, vulnerability, infrastructure interdependencies, and possible cascading effects [ 3 , 4 , 39 ]. As established in Sect. 4, near-miss intelligence represents a specific category of precursor information rather than a synonym for all risk signals. A distinctive proposition of the framework is the extension of near-miss intelligence from its conventional occupational safety application to a broader urban risk-learning context [ 8 , 13 ]. The purpose is not to treat near misses from different domains as directly equivalent, but to use appropriately classified precursor information to identify latent vulnerabilities, recurring patterns, cross-domain dependencies, and opportunities for preventive or adaptive action. Whether this integrated approach can produce measurable improvements in these areas remains to be established through empirical evaluation. 6.1 Conceptual foundation and integration of Urban domains The conceptual foundation of the IRSS Framework draws on systems thinking, socio-technical systems theory, urban resilience, sustainability transitions, and integrated management principles. These perspectives recognise that urban outcomes emerge from interactions among technological, organisational, environmental, institutional, and behavioural components rather than from isolated infrastructure assets [ 2 , 12 , 39 ]. Accordingly, the framework treats the seven urban domains as interconnected components of a broader urban system. Existing management and circular-economy standards, including ISO 45,001, ISO 14,001, ISO 50,001, ISO 59020:2024, and ISO 9001, provide complementary principles for occupational health and safety, environmental management, energy performance, circularity measurement and assessment, and organisational management. Their integration within the IRSS architecture provides a conceptual bridge between established management practices and city-scale infrastructure governance [ 5 , 6 , 7 ]. 6.2 Multidimensional risk integration Risk provides the central integrating mechanism of the framework. In interconnected urban systems, risks may originate in one domain and influence other domains through infrastructure dependencies, shared resources, environmental pathways, or organisational interactions [ 3 , 4 ]. The framework therefore considers safety, health, water, environmental, energy, transportation, and circularity risks collectively. Their interactions may generate cascading effects—for example, an energy disruption may affect water supply and transportation, while drainage failure may create environmental and public-health consequences. The purpose is not to create a single aggregated risk score, but to enable domain-specific risks to be interpreted within their wider system context [ 25 , 39 ]. 6.3 Near-miss intelligence within the framework As defined and delimited in Sect. 4 , near-miss intelligence represents systematically captured and interpreted information from events or conditions that had a credible potential for an undesirable outcome but did not result in that outcome. Its value within IRSS lies in identifying vulnerabilities, precursor pathways, control weaknesses, and emerging interdependencies [ 8 , 13 ]. The framework does not assume that every outage, anomaly, deviation, environmental exceedance, or operational failure is a near miss. Such observations may instead constitute hazards, deviations, alerts, incidents, or other risk signals and may complement near-miss intelligence. Nor does the framework propose direct numerical aggregation of heterogeneous near misses. Instead, domain-specific information is retained within its context and examined in relation to consequence, exposure, vulnerability, causal pathway, and interdependency. This distinction strengthens the conceptual validity of extending near-miss learning beyond occupational safety. 6.4 System architecture and decision pathway The operational architecture of the IRSS Framework follows a four-layer structure. Layer 1—Urban Socio-Technical System : This layer represents the seven urban domains and their physical, technological, environmental, organisational, institutional, and human components. Layer 2—Multidimensional Risk Identification and Near-Miss Intelligence : This layer captures near misses and other relevant risk information, including hazards, deviations, incidents, alerts, monitoring observations, and service disruptions. Information is classified according to its nature, potential consequence, and relationship with the relevant urban domain. These observations are not automatically classified as near misses. Classification as a near miss requires evidence of a credible potential for an undesirable consequence that was avoided; otherwise, the observation is retained under the appropriate category of risk information. Layer 3—Risk Assessment, Interpretation and Cross-Domain Analysis: Information from Layer 2 is assessed using context-appropriate measures of likelihood, consequence, exposure, vulnerability, severity, uncertainty, and interdependency. Cross-domain analysis examines whether a signal originating in one domain may affect other systems or create cascading consequences. Layer 4—Risk-Informed Decision-Making, Adaptive Governance and Continuous Learning: The assessment results support prioritisation of interventions, resource allocation, operational controls, emergency preparedness, infrastructure planning, and governance decisions. Monitoring of intervention outcomes and subsequent operational experience provides feedback to the earlier layers. Thus, the framework can be represented as a continuous pathway: Capture → Classify → Assess → Interpret → Prioritise → Intervene → Monitor → Learn . This pathway provides the operational logic connecting heterogeneous urban risk information with coordinated decision-making. 6.5 Operationalisation of the IRSS framework To move beyond a purely diagrammatic representation, the framework proposes that operationalisation should begin with the identification of relevant data inputs, indicators, spatial and temporal units, assessment criteria, and decision responsibilities. Potential inputs include near misses, incidents, infrastructure deviations, water-service interruptions, environmental measurements, energy anomalies, transportation conflicts, health-related exposure information, and resource-loss observations. The indicators used should remain domain-specific rather than being forced into a single universal metric. Depending on the application, they may include event rates, failure frequency, exposure levels, service interruption frequency, environmental concentrations, energy intensity, mobility indicators, water losses, or material recovery rates. Spatial units may range from individual facilities and infrastructure assets to neighbourhoods, service areas, or the city as a whole, while temporal assessment may involve real-time monitoring, daily or seasonal analysis, or longer-term trend assessment. Risk prioritisation should consider likelihood, consequence, exposure, vulnerability, severity, interdependency, and uncertainty. Normalisation or weighting may be applied where comparative analysis is required, but the specific rules should be determined according to the application context rather than imposed universally. Similarly, alert thresholds should be established using domain-specific regulatory requirements, historical baselines, engineering limits, service standards, or locally defined risk criteria. Decision responsibility would depend on the nature and scale of the identified risk and may involve infrastructure operators, municipal authorities, utility providers, safety and environmental personnel, public-health agencies, transportation authorities, emergency-management organisations, and other relevant stakeholders. The framework therefore provides the architecture for decision integration without prescribing a single institutional arrangement. 6.6 Illustrative Cross-domain application The operational logic of the IRSS Framework can be illustrated through a hypothetical heatwave scenario. An extended period of high temperature may increase electricity demand while simultaneously increasing heat-health risks, affecting public transportation reliability and placing additional pressure on water and waste-management services. Within the IRSS architecture, temperature and energy-demand data, heat-related health observations, transport disruptions, water-service information, and relevant operational near misses are first captured and classified. The information is then assessed in relation to severity, exposure, vulnerability, service dependencies, and potential cascading effects. If the assessment indicates that increasing electricity demand may compromise transport or water-service reliability while heat exposure is increasing among vulnerable populations, the information can be escalated for coordinated action. Potential interventions may include energy-load management, adjustment of transport operations, targeted public-health measures, enhanced water-service monitoring, and contingency arrangements for essential waste services. Subsequent monitoring of service reliability, health outcomes, and system performance provides feedback for further assessment and learning. This hypothetical example demonstrates how heterogeneous risk information can move through the proposed IRSS decision pathway—capture → classify → assess → interpret → prioritise → intervene → monitor → learn—without requiring all observations to be classified as near misses. Near-miss intelligence therefore functions as one component of a broader risk-information system, while specific indicators, thresholds, weighting methods, and decision criteria remain subject to context-specific calibration and empirical validation. 6.7 Urban outcomes, governance and quality of City life The framework is intended to provide a conceptual basis for considering infrastructure reliability and safety, public-health protection, environmental quality, energy resilience, safer mobility, water-service reliability, resource circularity, and overall urban resilience. These outcomes are interconnected rather than independent; improvements or deterioration in one domain may influence others. The framework consequently provides a conceptual basis for considering a shift from sector-specific management toward coordinated and risk-informed urban governance. It can provide a basis for integrating information from infrastructure operators, environmental regulators, public-health authorities, transportation agencies, utilities, emergency-management organisations, and municipal decision-makers. Such integration is consistent with the emphasis on adaptive capacity, cross-sectoral coordination, and learning in contemporary urban resilience research [ 2 , 39 ]. The framework also has potential relevance to smart-city and digital infrastructure initiatives because its architecture can accommodate multiple data streams and decision-support tools. However, digital technologies are considered enabling mechanisms rather than defining components of IRSS. The core contribution remains the integration of risk information, cross-domain interpretation, decision-making, and continuous learning. The framework is also conceptually aligned with SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure), SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), and SDG 13 (Climate Action). 6.8 Detailed operational architecture of the IRSS framework While Fig. 1 presents the overall conceptual structure of the IRSS Framework, Fig. 2 provides its detailed operational architecture. Decisions and interventions are subsequently linked to potential urban contributions, while monitoring, operational experience, and lessons learned provide feedback for subsequent assessment, decision-making, and continuous learning. The four layers are connected through information flows and feedback mechanisms, representing an iterative learning cycle rather than a strictly linear sequence. 6.9 Novel contributions of the proposed framework The novelty of the IRSS Framework lies not in introducing systems thinking, urban resilience, sustainability, integrated management, or multidimensional risk as entirely new concepts. As discussed in Sect. 5.4 and summarised in Table 2 , these foundations are well represented in the existing literature. The contribution lies instead in their specific integration through a common risk-information and decision architecture. Three conceptual contributions are particularly important. First, the framework positions multidimensional risk as an integrating mechanism across seven urban domains rather than treating safety, health, water, environmental, energy, transportation, and resource circularity risks as independent concerns. Second, it extends near-miss intelligence from a predominantly occupational safety learning mechanism toward a carefully delimited cross-domain source of urban risk information, while explicitly distinguishing near misses from hazards, deviations, alerts, incidents, and leading indicators. Third, it connects precursor information with cross-domain risk assessment, coordinated decision-making, adaptive governance, and continuous learning, thereby providing an operational pathway from observation to intervention and feedback. The framework consequently offers a conceptual bridge between organisational management systems and city-scale infrastructure governance. Its value is therefore best understood as an integrative architecture for connecting heterogeneous risk information with urban decision-making, rather than as a validated predictive model or universal risk-index system. Empirical studies are required to test its classification criteria, indicators, thresholds, weighting approaches, decision processes, and measurable contribution to urban resilience, sustainability, and quality of life. The anticipated benefits should therefore be interpreted as propositions for future testing rather than as demonstrated outcomes. 7 Discussion This section discusses the conceptual implications of the proposed IRSS Framework in relation to integrated urban management, cross-domain near-miss intelligence, resilience, sustainability, and governance. Because the framework has not yet been empirically validated, the discussion focuses on its conceptual contribution, implementation conditions, limitations, and priorities for future research. 7.1 Integrated management as a response to Urban system complexity Urban infrastructure comprises interconnected physical, environmental, technological, organisational, and social systems. However, safety, environmental protection, energy, transportation, health, water, and resource management are often addressed through separate institutional and operational mechanisms. Such fragmentation can constrain the recognition of interdependencies and the management of systemic risks [ 2 , 39 ]. The IRSS Framework provides a conceptual structure for examining this fragmentation by organising these domains around a common risk-based perspective. Rather than treating safety, environmental performance, energy efficiency, transportation reliability, health, water services, and circularity as independent objectives, it considers them as interacting dimensions of urban system performance. This positioning is consistent with systems thinking and risk-governance perspectives, which emphasise interactions, dependencies, feedback, and cross-sectoral consequences in complex socio-technical systems [ 4 ]. The framework therefore offers a basis for moving from predominantly sector-specific management towards more coordinated and adaptive decision-making. Its contribution, however, is conceptual: whether such integration produces measurable improvements in urban resilience or sustainability remains to be established empirically. 7.2 Near-miss intelligence as a Cross-domain Risk-information mechanism A central proposition of the IRSS Framework is the extension of near-miss intelligence beyond its conventional occupational safety application. Near misses have traditionally supported learning from events that expose weaknesses in controls and barriers and may precede adverse outcomes [ 8 , 9 ]. The framework proposes that appropriately classified precursor events in transportation, energy, water, environmental, health, and resource systems may similarly provide information about emerging vulnerabilities. This proposition does not imply that every anomaly, deviation, alert, or service disturbance constitutes a near miss. As established earlier, classification should depend on the existence of a credible pathway to an undesirable consequence and the avoidance of that consequence through intervention, controls, or other circumstances. Cross-domain interpretation should therefore retain the characteristics and context of individual events rather than treating heterogeneous observations as directly comparable numerical measures. If empirically supported, such information could complement conventional lagging indicators and may provide additional evidence to help identify emerging vulnerabilities and investigate potential cascading pathways. However, the reliability, predictive value, and transferability of cross-domain near-miss intelligence remain unresolved and require systematic empirical assessment. 7.3 Implications for Urban resilience and sustainability The IRSS Framework conceptually connects risk identification with resilience, sustainability, and service continuity. Urban resilience requires systems to anticipate, absorb, adapt to, and recover from disturbances while maintaining essential functions [ 2 ]. The proposed integration of cross-domain risk information could potentially support this objective by facilitating the recognition of dependencies, vulnerabilities, and emerging disruptions. The framework also incorporates environmental protection, energy performance, resource efficiency, and circularity, thereby linking risk management with broader sustainability objectives. Its conceptual alignment with SDG 3, SDG 9, SDG 11, SDG 12, and SDG 13 provides a policy-level connection between integrated risk governance and internationally recognised sustainability priorities. These potential contributions should not, however, be interpreted as demonstrated outcomes; their magnitude and distribution require empirical evaluation. 7.4 Governance feasibility and implementation conditions The increasing complexity and interdependence of urban systems require governance arrangements capable of coordinating across traditional administrative and sectoral boundaries. Transportation, environmental management, energy, public health and safety, water, and waste services are commonly assigned to specialised agencies. Although such arrangements provide domain-specific expertise, they can constrain coordination when risks, dependencies, and service disruptions extend across institutional boundaries [ 4 , 39 ]. The IRSS Framework therefore envisages coordinated governance rather than replacement of existing institutional responsibilities. A municipal or designated metropolitan authority could provide overall coordination and framework ownership, while infrastructure and utility operators, transport agencies, environmental and public-health authorities, emergency services, and relevant community stakeholders retain responsibility for domain-specific functions. Implementation could proceed through four linked activities: (i) mapping critical infrastructure domains and interdependencies; (ii) establishing protocols for capturing and classifying near misses and other precursor information; (iii) integrating relevant risk and performance information across responsible organisations; and (iv) using the resulting information for risk prioritisation, intervention, monitoring, and adaptive review. Effective implementation would require clearly defined ownership, accountability, data-sharing responsibilities, and decision authority. Relevant information may include near-miss and incident records, infrastructure-condition data, environmental and energy observations, transportation events, service disruptions, water-system information, and resource-flow data. Interoperable data standards, common metadata and classification protocols, data-quality assurance, controlled access, and secure information-sharing mechanisms would be important for consistent cross-agency interpretation. Implementation would also require sustained financing for monitoring infrastructure, data management, analytical capacity, personnel training, system maintenance, and institutional coordination rather than reliance solely on short-term technology investments. Governance feasibility further depends on responsible management of privacy, cybersecurity, and predictive decision-making. Where information contains personal, location-sensitive, or confidential data, collection and sharing should follow data-minimisation, access-control, security, and applicable legal requirements. Predictive analytical systems should incorporate data-quality assessment, uncertainty evaluation, human oversight, and periodic validation to reduce the potential for false alarms, biased outputs, and inappropriate automated decisions. These safeguards are particularly important where risk information may influence public services, resource allocation, mobility, or emergency interventions. The framework should also recognise social equity and competing urban objectives. Risk-reduction and resilience investments may generate unequal benefits or impose disproportionate costs on particular communities. Similarly, efficiency-oriented interventions may conflict with safety requirements, extensive monitoring may create tensions between situational awareness and privacy, and resilience investments may compete with affordability and immediate service-delivery priorities. The IRSS approach therefore requires such trade-offs to be explicitly considered during risk prioritisation and decision-making rather than assuming that safety, efficiency, sustainability, privacy, and affordability are always mutually reinforcing. Practical implementation may be constrained by institutional fragmentation, inconsistent terminology and reporting practices, incompatible information systems, limited data availability, resource constraints, and difficulties in establishing cross-sectoral accountability. A progressive implementation pathway, beginning with selected infrastructure domains or pilot applications and expanding following evaluation and stakeholder review, may therefore be more feasible than immediate city-wide deployment. Digital technologies, including IoT systems, digital twins, and predictive analytics, may support this process, but their adoption should remain subordinate to governance requirements, data quality, transparency, cybersecurity, and human decision-making. Accordingly, the IRSS Framework provides a conceptual governance pathway rather than a prescriptive institutional model. Its implementation would depend on local administrative structures, regulatory arrangements, infrastructure characteristics, institutional capacity, financial resources, data availability, and stakeholder participation. Future empirical research should therefore determine appropriate governance configurations, accountability mechanisms, data-sharing arrangements, indicators, decision thresholds, and implementation models across different urban contexts. 7.5 Future research directions Several research priorities follow from the conceptual nature of the framework. First, city-level case studies should examine its applicability across different infrastructure and governance contexts. Second, measurable indicators and operational definitions for multidimensional risk and near-miss intelligence should be developed and empirically tested. Third, research should examine the potential of digital technologies to improve the detection, integration, and interpretation of cross-domain risk signals without introducing unacceptable uncertainties or governance risks. Fourth, comparative studies should examine how the IRSS Framework relates to established urban resilience, infrastructure interdependency, risk-governance, and management-system approaches. Future research should also investigate quantitative assessment methods, including risk-prioritisation models and decision-support tools, while avoiding premature aggregation of heterogeneous near-miss information into a single universal index. Such research could establish whether the framework provides measurable advantages over sector-specific approaches. 7.6 Limitations, boundary conditions and validation agenda The present study is conceptual and does not provide empirical validation of the IRSS Framework. It has not yet been tested through simulation, pilot implementation, or city-level case studies. Its applicability is therefore expected to vary according to infrastructure characteristics, data availability, institutional arrangements, governance capacity, and stakeholder participation. The framework may be more readily implemented in data-rich cities with real-time monitoring, spatial datasets, and integrated information systems, whereas data-scarce settings may require simplified indicators, expert assessment, participatory reporting, and other low-data approaches. Similarly, application to formal and informal infrastructure systems may require adaptation to differences in service provision, institutional responsibility, monitoring capacity, and community participation. The framework does not prescribe universal indicators, weighting schemes, thresholds, or implementation procedures; these parameters require context-specific calibration. The cross-domain application of near-miss intelligence also presents important uncertainties concerning reporting completeness, classification consistency, data quality, comparability, and its relationship with subsequent adverse outcomes. Near-miss information should therefore not be assumed to be a universally reliable predictor or directly aggregated across heterogeneous domains. Validation can proceed through a staged pathway. First, operational definitions and candidate indicators should be developed through literature synthesis and expert consultation. Second, expert evaluation should assess conceptual coherence, indicator relevance, feasibility, and cross-domain applicability. Third, pilot implementation should examine data requirements, reporting mechanisms, risk-assessment procedures, decision thresholds, and institutional responsibilities. Fourth, comparative case studies across cities with different data availability, infrastructure conditions, and governance capacities should assess transferability and context-specific adaptation. Finally, outcome-based evaluation should determine whether application of the framework improves risk identification, response coordination, infrastructure resilience, environmental performance, public-health protection, resource efficiency, or other predefined outcomes. This validation agenda can address several testable questions: whether cross-domain risk integration identifies vulnerabilities not detected through sector-specific assessment; whether appropriately classified near-miss information provides additional or earlier warning of adverse outcomes; whether integrated risk information improves intervention prioritisation and coordination; and whether framework effectiveness varies with data maturity, infrastructure formality, and governance capacity. Accordingly, the IRSS Framework should presently be regarded as a conceptual and testable decision architecture rather than a validated universal model. Its contribution lies in providing a structured basis for integrating multidimensional risk, near-miss intelligence, infrastructure interdependencies, and adaptive decision-making, while leaving empirical calibration and context-specific implementation to future research. 8 Conclusions Urban infrastructure systems are increasingly characterised by interdependencies among safety, health, environmental, energy, transportation, water, and resource-management functions. However, these domains are often governed through specialised institutional and operational arrangements, which can make cross-domain vulnerabilities and cascading effects difficult to recognise. The present Perspective therefore proposed the Integrated Risk–Safety–Sustainability (IRSS) Framework for Resilient Urban Infrastructure and Improved City Life as a conceptual architecture for connecting these domains through a common risk-informed decision process. The framework positions multidimensional risk as an integrating construct through which risks across safety, health, water, environmental, energy, transportation, and resource-circularity domains can be considered in relation to infrastructure interdependencies and potential cascading effects. It further proposes near-miss intelligence as a potential cross-domain risk-information and early-warning mechanism. Rather than treating every anomaly or deviation as a near miss, the framework retains a defined distinction between hazards, risks, deviations, alerts, incidents, leading indicators, and near misses, while allowing appropriately classified precursor information to inform cross-domain risk assessment. The distinctive contribution of the IRSS Framework therefore lies not in introducing systems thinking, urban resilience, sustainability, or integrated management as new concepts. Its conceptual proposition is to organise these established perspectives around a multidimensional risk architecture and to explore whether near-miss intelligence, traditionally associated with occupational safety learning, can provide useful precursor information across interconnected urban systems. The framework links this information with risk assessment, prioritisation, intervention, outcome monitoring, and feedback and learning, while retaining domain-specific indicators and decision criteria. The framework is intended as a conceptual and testable decision architecture rather than a validated universal model. Its applicability will depend on local infrastructure characteristics, data availability, institutional arrangements, governance capacity, stakeholder participation, and the maturity of monitoring and information systems. In particular, the reliability, comparability, and predictive value of cross-domain near-miss intelligence remain to be established empirically. The framework therefore does not prescribe universal indicators, weighting schemes, thresholds, algorithms, or governance structures. Future research should progressively evaluate the framework through indicator development and expert assessment, pilot implementation, comparative case studies across different urban contexts, and outcome-based validation. Particular attention should be given to differences between data-rich and data-scarce cities, formal and informal infrastructure systems, and varying governance capacities. Research should also examine whether integrated risk information identifies vulnerabilities that sector-specific approaches may overlook, whether appropriately classified near-miss intelligence provides additional or earlier warning of adverse outcomes, and whether cross-agency integration improves the coordination and effectiveness of urban interventions. Such validation will determine whether the IRSS Framework can be translated from a conceptual proposition into a context-sensitive approach for urban risk governance, infrastructure resilience, sustainability, and improved quality of city life.
