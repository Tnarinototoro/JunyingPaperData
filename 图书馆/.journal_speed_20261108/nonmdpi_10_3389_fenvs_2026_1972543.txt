Department of Industrial Engineering and Management, Yuan Ze University, Taoyuan, Taiwan See more Article metrics View details Abstract Introduction: Groundwater recharge is traditionally understood to be primarily controlled by precipitation and other climatic processes. However, intensive anthropogenic water management and urbanization may weaken this relationship in megacities. This study investigates hydrological decoupling at the municipal scale in Beijing, China, and quantifies the relative contributions of anthropogenic and climatic drivers to groundwater dynamics. Methods: We analyzed 19 years of multisource hydroclimatic data using nonparametric trend analyses, antecedent-window correlation analyses, and hierarchical multiple regression. Spatial analyses were conducted to examine the potential effects of impervious surfaces and managed vegetation on natural groundwater recharge. Future drought risks and groundwater trends were also assessed under the SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 climate scenarios for 2024–2100. Results: Annual precipitation showed no statistically significant correlation with groundwater depth (Spearman’s ρ = 0.04, p = 0.624), and no significant delayed associations were identified across antecedent climatic windows of 1–12 months. Hierarchical regression attributed 60.0% of the variance in groundwater depth to anthropogenic drivers, represented by total water supply, compared with 35.6% attributed to natural climatic forcing. Spatial analyses further indicated that dense impervious surfaces and managed vegetation may constrain natural recharge and weaken the influence of meteorological variability on groundwater dynamics. Both future climate scenarios indicated substantial drought risk, while historical evidence suggested that groundwater trajectories would remain strongly influenced by active water management. Discussion: The findings indicate that anthropogenic water management can become a dominant control on urban groundwater dynamics, potentially outweighing the influence of climatic variability. This hydrological decoupling challenges conventional assumptions about precipitation-driven groundwater responses and highlights the importance of integrating water governance, urban land-surface characteristics, and climate adaptation into groundwater management strategies for heavily urbanized basins. 1 Introduction Groundwater is the most tapped natural resource, providing about one-third of the irrigation water for the world and about one-half of all drinking water. The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and GRACE Follow-On (GRACE-FO) satellite gravimetry missions have demonstrated that many of the world’s largest scale aquifer systems are undergoing chronic declines at rates exceeding the hydrological modelling projections (Rodell and Reager, 2023; Chen et al., 2022; Humphrey et al., 2023; Forootan et al., 2024). Such observations highlight an important disconnect: the frameworks that underpin groundwater science and policy have, to a large extent, been developed assuming that the dynamics of groundwater are driven by natural hydroclimatic forcing (especially recharge from the unsaturated zone by precipitation) (Liu et al., 2024; Haas et al., 2024). However, the demographic and infrastructure realities of the 21st Century are increasingly countering this assumption in the world’s most water stressed environments. Global urbanisation rates are increasing and water consumption (and hydrological interference) come together in limited spatial areas, especially in megacities (Olabode and Comte, 2022; Olabode and Comte, 2024/09; Bose et al., 2024). These urban areas place dramatically opposing demands on groundwater resources: high land use demands intensive groundwater abstraction for urban water supply needs, while impervious surfaces of the land also impede natural diffuse recharge of groundwater (Lubis et al., 2025; Jaiswal et al., 2024; Razi et al., 2023). Recent studies further emphasize how rapid land-use changes and unplanned urbanization directly disrupt natural hydrological cycles. For instance, urban expansion has been shown to significantly reduce groundwater recharge rates by replacing permeable surfaces with impervious built-up areas (Waiyasusri et al., 2024). Furthermore, the transformation of natural landscapes into urban and commercial zones often encroaches upon and obstructs natural drainage networks, exacerbating surface runoff and freshwater shortages while severing the natural precipitation-recharge pathway (Usaa et al., 2026; Waiyasusri and Wetchayont, 2025). These pressures have led to hydrogeological vulnerabilities in quickly expanding coastal and inland megacities like Lagos, Kolkata, Karachi, and Ho Chi Minh City that are no longer predictable in response to interannual precipitation variations (Olabode and Comte, 2022; Olabode and Comte, 2024/09; Bose et al., 2024; Olabode and Comte, 2025; Riaz et al., 2025; Ha et al., 2025). Water management, however, such as abstraction licensing, transfers from surface water and managed aquifer recharge (MAR) programs, are now increasingly controlling aquifer behaviour. Such a ‘displacement of the climate’ by anthropogenic interventions has led to a phenomenon, which is referred to here as hydrological decoupling, that changes the primary role of climate in driving groundwater dynamics. In the classical paradigm of hydrology, there is a statistically robust and physically explicable linkage between precipitation and groundwater recharge. For natural and lightly developed catchments, this relationship is well bounded by infiltration theory: the transfer of atmospheric water to the saturated zone is controlled by antecedent soil moisture, rainfall intensity, and properties of the vadose zone, and will result in characteristic lagged correlations over seasonal to multi-annual timescales (Liu et al., 2024; Razi et al., 2023). These relationships have been found in basins with minimal management, using non-parametric statistical techniques such as the Mann-Kendall test and Spearman rank correlations (Jaiswal et al., 2024; Razi et al., 2023). In high-density urbanised and highly extracted catchments, however, the physical connections that underlie these relationships are strongly compromised. High-density impervious surfaces send water to engineered drainage systems rather than through the soil column and away from the natural soil column, thus eliminating the diffuse recharge (Jaiswal et al., 2024; Waiyasusri et al., 2024). At the same time, the moisture that does reach the water table will have high evapotranspiratory demand from managed vegetation in urban parks and peri-urban zones of agriculture, thus further reducing the amount reaching the water table (Olabode and Comte, 2025; Riaz et al., 2025). One of the most well-documented examples of groundwater overexploitation in the world is the NCP and its megacity, Beijing (Liu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Du et al., 2024). Beijing’s alluvial-fan aquifer system was historically dependent on summer monsoon (June–August) seasonality to recharge the aquifer system. However, in the last 50 years, economic growth and population growth have accelerated the rates of groundwater pumping much higher than it can be replenished, resulting in high and persistent depletion of the aquifers (Li et al., 2023; Du et al., 2024). Consequently, China has taken a massive response in terms of water governance interventions, notably the South-to-North Water Diversion Project (SNWDP), which aims at providing water for urban use and alleviating dependence on local groundwater resources (Liu et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024). Recent groundwater storage recovery estimates based on GRACE-InSAR fusion data have indicated partial recovery of groundwater storage in parts of the Beijing Plain after the commissioning of the SNWD, but the extent of the recovery is still not well understood and the sensitivity of the groundwater system to natural precipitation has not been extensively tested at the municipal scale (Wang et al., 2023). The question of whether the classical precipitation–groundwater relationship has been broken in Beijing is crucial for fundamental science and practice. Urban hydrology in megacities may need a different theoretical paradigm from the one based on the climatic assumption, in order to replace the current groundwater models (Liu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023)in scientific terms. Governance-wise, it is a question of whether future groundwater security is dependent on climatic relief, which is the premise of most existing drought-risk assessments (Zhou et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Ling et al., 2023; Soni et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024) or whether it is a matter of policy, assessed by extraction quotas, inter-basin transfers, and recharge augmentation. Both the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP) 2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 emission scenarios show a general increase in droughts in northern China under the same general conditions over the 21st century (Zhou et al., 2023; Ling et al., 2023; Soni et al., 2023). In the classical paradigm, this means increasing groundwater stress. In contrast, however, if the precipitation–groundwater connection is already broken, projections of the future of the aquifer might not be linked to precipitation anomalies and be dependent on human decisions about water budgets. The primary objective of this study is to explicitly test and quantify the hydrological decoupling hypothesis within the Beijing Municipality using a comprehensive 19-year (2005–2023) multi-source hydroclimatic dataset. Specifically, we aim to: Determine if a statistically significant linkage between precipitation and groundwater persists using non-parametric trend analyses and antecedent window correlations. Identify the physical processes disrupting natural recharge through spatiotemporal analysis of land-use imperviousness (Global Human Settlement Layer) and vegetation condition (MODIS NDVI). Assess whether projected future climate changes (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios) will alter future groundwater dynamics, or if anthropogenic management will remain the dominant driver. The expected outcomes of this research are to provide the first comprehensive, quantitative attribution of hydrological decoupling at the municipal scale in North China. We anticipate demonstrating that natural climatic forcing has been effectively superseded by anthropogenic water management. Ultimately, this study aims to establish hydrological decoupling as a critical conceptual framework for heavily managed urban hydrology, offering significant implications for future groundwater monitoring frameworks, drought hazard assessment, and water governance in the Anthropocene. 2 Methodology Classical hydrology assumes that the amount of water available to a watershed, such as the amount of natural recharge, is determined by physical laws and is regulated by climate variables (e.g., precipitation). This physical connection however, is often broken in heavily managed basins where water resources are under intensive pressure. The hydrological decoupling hypothesis implies that when precipitation is no longer a viable driver of groundwater changes, then anthropogenic influences (such as over-extraction, managed aquifer recharge, and surface sealing) are more important in the natural hydrologic cycle. This study used statistical non-parametric and spatiotemporal analyses to identify this decoupling and explain the human drivers behind it, as outlined in the comprehensive methodological workflow (Figure 1). The total geographical coverage area of the municipality is approximately 16,410 km2. The topographical features consist of a flat alluvial plain in the southeast and mountainous terrain in the west and north, with the mountains covering about 62% of the total area (Olabode and Comte, 2025; Riaz et al., 2025). Beijing experiences a typical warm-temperate continental monsoon climate, with approximately 60%–70% of the annual precipitation falling during the summer monsoon season (June to August). As a megacity undergoing rapid urbanization and economic growth, Beijing has faced acute water shortages over the past 2 decades. The region has historically relied on intensive groundwater extraction to support its expanding population and economy, resulting in significant depletion of its groundwater resources (Olabode and Comte, 2025; Riaz et al., 2025). This strongly modified hydrological environment, characterized by intense anthropogenic influence, provides an ideal test-bed to explore the hydrological decoupling hypothesis. Given that these comprehensive regional assessments were published as PDF reports, we employed Python-based text processing techniques utilizing Regular Expressions (Regex) to systematically extract the annual average groundwater depth for the Beijing plain. It is important to note that these bulletins provide a single, municipality-wide average depth per year, rather than spatially distributed point data from individual observation wells. Consequently, this dataset is highly suitable for robust temporal trend analysis but precludes continuous spatial interpolation (Kriging or IDW) across the broader North China Plain. To ensure the reliability and accuracy of this automated extraction process, a formal uncertainty analysis was conducted. A random subset comprising 10% of the total bulletins (specifically, the years 2015 and 2020) was selected for manual visual validation. The relative error between the automated Regex outputs and the manually verified values was calculated. The validation yielded a relative error of 0.0% ± 0.0% for groundwater depth, thereby confirming the high precision and robustness of the data parsing methodology for use in subsequent statistical analyses. 2.2.2 Historical climatic and remote sensing data The independent climatic and environmental variables were calculated by spatial mean at the boundary of the Beijing basin to build the historical monthly time series (2005–2023) for the following datasets: - Climatic variables, namely, monthly precipitation, maximum temperature (Tmax), soil moisture and surface runoff, were extracted from TerraClimate at 4.6 km spatial resolution; surface runoff was used as a reliable proxy for ungauged streamflow in the region. - MODIS products were used to obtain the vegetation and thermal characteristics. These monthly averages were created by temporally aggregating the data from the 16-day Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) of the MOD13Q1 product (250 m resolution) and the 8-day Land Surface Temperature (LST) of the MOD11A2 product (1 km resolution) with the same temporal scale as the climatic variables. 2.2.3 Future climate projections Monthly precipitation forecasts were obtained from the Earth System Grid Federation (ESGF) portal to evaluate future drought risk and possible climate changes. Based on their successful experience in East Asia, the ACCESS-CM2 model was chosen. Data were extracted under two Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) spanning 2015–2100: SSP2-4.5 (moderate mitigation) and SSP5-8.5 (fossil-fueled development). The main libraries used were Pandas (v2.3.3) and NumPy (v2.2.6) for data manipulation, SciPy (v1.15.3) and Stats models (v0.14.6) for non-parametric statistical tests and FDR corrections, Matplotlib and Seaborn for data visualization and xarray (v2025.6.1) to handle multidimensional CMIP6 netCDF datasets. 2.3 Data preprocessing and temporal standardization The integration of datasets with multi-sources, but different native temporal resolutions (MONTHLY for TerraClimate, 16-day for MODIS, and DAYS for CMIP6 projections) is a basic limitation to separate climatic for anthropogenic influences on groundwater dynamics. A systematic temporal standardization protocol was used to build up a unified analytical framework appropriate for statistically testing pairwise. 2.3.1 Harmonization of high-frequency remote sensing products The 16-day NDVI (MOD13Q1) and 8-day LST (MOD11A2) composites were temporally aggregated into monthly mean values over the Beijing basin boundary. This aggregation involves assigning the vegetation and thermal dynamics to the same temporal scale as the monthly TerraClimate climatic variables (monthly grid), so that all the remote sensing-based indicators are placed on the same temporal grid. 2.3.2 Construction of the unified monthly matrix A matrix was formed for the historical period (2005–2023) by calculating the spatial mean of each variable over the Beijing municipality. A listwise deletion procedure was used: months with an incomplete record for any of the variables were not included in the analysis. This conservative method avoids the imputation biases that may occur and, especially when hydroclimatic time series are non-stationary. 2.3.3 Multi-scale temporal alignment for distinct analytical objectives The preprocessed data had been purposefully combined at different temporal scales based on the type of statistical method used: - For lagged correlation analysis (Section 2.4.2) - To account for short-term delayed hydrological responses, while preserving the annual resolution of the groundwater observations, we used a moving-window aggregation approach. Climatic variables (precipitation, temperature, soil moisture, runoff, and NDVI) were summed or averaged over a sliding time window from 0 to 12 months before the end of each hydrological year. These windowed climatic metrics were then matched to the respective annual groundwater depth and tested for influence of climatic signals at specific antecedent timescales on aquifer status. This is consistent with the more traditional hydrological approach of using moving averages and antecedent precipitation index as a measure of cumulative climatic forcing on groundwater systems. - For long-term trend analysis (Section 2.4.1) Monthly time series were compiled to annual time series to assess monotonic trends for the entire 20 years. Each of the water fluxes (precipitation and surface runoff) were added to in an annual basis, and state variables (maximum temperature, soil moisture, and NDVI) were calculated as annual mean values; groundwater depth was available as annual averages in the bulletins. This annual aggregation helps to remove the high-frequency seasonal noise and highlight the interannual to decadal signal of relevance for assessing structural changes in the hydrological system. In this dual-track preprocessing approach we guaranteed that each statistical analysis went on data that were aggregated at the proper temporal scale, maximizing both physical and statistical meaning of the results. - Quality Control Parameters and Outlier Detection In order to preserve the physical integrity of the time series, a robust Quality Control (QC) procedure had been adopted before analysis. Linear interpolation was only allowed for time intervals of ≤2 months; larger time intervals were filled by nearest-neighbor propagation to avoid creating artificial hydrological trends. All variables passed the minimum criterion of 15 years of data (over 19 years). In addition, an automated outlier detection algorithm was used to identify any values greater than ±3 standard deviations from the 36-month moving average. The above extreme values were flagged and kept in the dataset because they are more likely to be actual extreme hydrological events (e.g., extreme storms, droughts) than sensor errors. These thresholds were determined based on the characteristics of the data and initial sensitivity analysis to provide good temporal coverage with minimal effect of outliers. 2.4 Statistical and spatio-temporal methods A set of non-parametric statistical tests and correlation analysis was used to quantify the hydrological decoupling hypothesis and to separate climatic from anthropogenic forcing effects. The annual groundwater depth exhibited a distinct non-monotonic trajectory (Figure 3a). Initially, the depth increased from 20.2 m in 2005 to a peak of 25.8 m in 2015, indicating a period of sustained aquifer depletion. While this marked a strong recent recovery, the overall Sen’s Slope for the entire period was slightly negative (), indicating that the effects of the earlier depletion phase predominated. While climatic variables fluctuate spatially and temporally, the landscape’s physical and biological responses are increasingly mediated by anthropogenic management rather than natural meteorological forcing. 4 Discussion 4.1 Opening statement The most important conclusion of this study is that groundwater depth changes in the Beijing Municipality are highly decoupled from precipitation changes over the period (2005–2023), the correlation coefficient between the two is nearly zero (; , Table 2), pointing to a strong decoupling of groundwater changes from natural climatic variations over the last 2 decades. This is not merely an artifact of absent correlation; rather, hierarchical regression quantitatively proves that anthropogenic water management explains 60.0% of groundwater variance, decisively overshadowing natural climatic forcing (35.6%). The result is contrary to the traditional hydro-climatic model that accredit precipitation as the dominant control over groundwater recharge. The decoupling of the water cycle in a city like Beijing, which has experienced some of the most rapid urbanization and intensive groundwater extraction in East Asia, highlights the potential to restructure the fundamental water cycle of a large metropolitan region by human actions. Not a short-term fluctuation, but a structural shift which is stable and persists over a period of decades in the hydrological behaviour of a basin of global significance. 4.2 Mechanistic interpretation The statistical dominance of anthropogenic drivers (proven in Section 3.7) is physically explained by the spatial transformation of the Beijing landscape. The GHSL data reveals that impervious surfaces act as a physical barrier, preventing the diffuse infiltration of meteoric water. Simultaneously, the NDVI pattern indicates managed vegetative cover acting as a consumptive sink. Together, these spatial realities explain why natural recharge has been superseded by managed water transfers. Two complementary physical mechanisms explain the observed statistical disconnect. Firstly, the changing partitioning of the precipitation into infiltration and surface runoff in the urban core of Beijing has drastically changed (as quantified by the GHSL, 2020; Figures 7b) due to the dramatic increase in impervious surface as a result of urban expansion. The primary pathway for infiltration to reach the shallow aquifer is prevented by impervious cover and episodic precipitation events are not effective in recharging the shallow aquifer. The mapping of reduced infiltration zones in the Beijing Plain is in accordance with the mapping of maximum land subsidence reversals by Zhou et al. (Zhou et al., 2024) suggesting that in these areas recharge is now almost entirely controlled by management. Secondly, the MODIS NDVI data show that there is a large-scale managed vegetation canopy (urban parks, ornamental greenbelts, peri-urban agriculture) maintained by irrigation, rather than precipitation (Figures 7a). This is managed ET, which acts as a long-term consumptive sink, trapping artificial water supplies with little production of recharge from precipitation. As detailed in Sections 3.4, 3.5, spatial corroboration of the statistical disconnect comes from the co-occurrence of maximum built-up density () and minimum groundwater–precipitation correlation (). 4.3 Comparison with previous studies The finding is in contrast to the results of Long et al. (Long et al., 2025) who reported unprecedented large-scale aquifer recovery in the NCP, with groundwater-level rises of 0.7 m yr-1 in the unconfined aquifers, mainly due to South-to-North Water Diversion Project (SNWDP) delivery and controlled extraction. Both studies confirmed that climate change has become the primary factor influencing groundwater dynamics, but the spatial scale of the analysis lead to different conclusions. In both areas, the volumetric signature of the SNWDP inputs is detectable, but at the NCP-wide scale (130,000 km2), the signature is clearly detectable, whereas at the municipal scale of Beijing, the interplay between urban surface sealing, managed evapotranspiration, and spatially heterogeneous extraction results in a regime in which no single climatic signal dominates, and thus decoupling instead of recovery. This difference highlights a basic scale dependence in groundwater–climate dynamics that has not been considered in much detail in earlier megacity studies. The situation is similar in other mega-urban areas, where anthropogenic domination is visible over the climatic forcing, but with very few institutional interventions. (Ríos-Sánchez et al., 2024) demonstrate that informal groundwater extraction and regulatory shortcomings are more important factors controlling groundwater dynamics in the peri-urban area of Mexico City than precipitation variability. The groundwater depletion at decadal scale in the Indo-Gangetic Plain, including the Delhi region, is mainly driven by irrigated agriculture and urban demands, while the influence of precipitation variation is a secondary modulating factor, as reported by (Kunwar et al., 2024). (Rani and Bano, 2024) also show that DRM has continued to experience hydrological stress even when rainfall in a monsoon season is high. Decoupling-like situations occur in all three cities, but are not mitigated. The key difference is that in Beijing, aquifer depletion is no longer the objective but managed decoupling (SNWDP deliveries since 2014 and systematic managed aquifer recharge and extraction caps) is actively driven toward aquifer stabilisation in contrast to the other cities. 4.4 Broader implications The lessons learnt from the governance of Beijing are applicable directly to the context of fast-growing megacities where groundwater use is not regulated. As our results show, after hydrological decoupling, the aquifer system is no longer sensitive to droughts or wet anomalies, but rather to policy decisions regarding the future of the system. The duality means there are important implications. On the risk side, the groundwater crisis will not self-correct in managed cities under only a wet precipitation cycle; without active intervention, groundwater depletion trajectories will continue under all precipitation cycles in the future, even under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 as shown in Section 3.8 , with eight drought years projected in each case. Well-designed management frameworks can beat climatic variability: Liu et al. ( Liu et al., 2024 ) show that, in scenario modelling, the contributions from SNWDP can support positive groundwater trends in the Beijing Plain, even though a variety of climate uncertainties are considered. Three policy recommendations are hereby proposed to implement megacities facing hydrological decoupling based on the above: Establishment of continuous and high-resolution groundwater monitoring networks that can be used to detect the early signatures of decoupling before irreversible aquifer deformation happens; Legally binding extraction quotas tied to real-time aquifer-level thresholds rather than fixed volumetric permits; and Investment in MAR infrastructure that captures surplus diversion flows during wet years, thus reducing the impact of prolonged droughts, similar to the approach adopted in Beijing for ecological recharge corridors (Liu and Bai, 2024). 4.5 Limitations There are a number of methodological limitations to be noted. The analysis is based on annual groundwater depth averages, masking out intra-annual recharge pulses and seasonal dynamics of the relationship between precipitation and groundwater, which would be better captured by the monthly resolution data available. Second, using spatial averages on a municipality-wide scale removes the heterogeneity within the municipality, and the Beijing Plain has a range of densities with urban cores, peri-urban transition zones and areas of residual agricultural land that could have different recharge behaviours. Disaggregation of the analysis at sub-catchment level would allow a more mechanistic interpretation. Thirdly, the groundwater data are not differentiated between shallow, unconfined and deeper, confined aquifers that have different degrees of response to the surface processes. Despite these restrictions, the result of the near zero correlation between annual precipitation and groundwater depth (ρ = 0.04, p = 0.624) is a strong conclusion, which appears across various non-parametric tests and which is supported by the independent spatial evidence of the NDVI and built-up surface layers, and does not depend on the temporal statistical analysis. The weaknesses described here are not a reason to doubt the presence of hydrological decoupling in Beijing: they set a challenging agenda for future research. Finally, while we project future climatic drought risk (Section 3.8), we do not simulate future groundwater levels. Consequently, future groundwater trajectories in heavily managed systems like Beijing will be dictated entirely by anthropogenic policy scenarios (SNWDP quotas, extraction limits, and managed recharge rates) rather than the climatic scenarios presented here. 4.6 Concluding statement The natural precipitation–groundwater linkage may be the hallmark of the twenty-first century urban water cycle if hydrological decoupling prevails, meaning that anthropogenic factors dominate the linkage. The diagnostic signatures derived from the Beijing experience offer insights into this situation and show how the crisis of decoupled depletion can be managed towards stabilisation. We suggest that the concept of hydrological decoupling should be recognized as a vital paradigm for heavily managed urban hydrology, necessitating the development of different monitoring approaches We suggest that the concept of hydrological decoupling should be recognized as a vital paradigm for heavily managed urban hydrology, necessitating the development of different monitoring approaches, governance tools and a rethinking of the paradigm assumption for governing groundwater in densely populated, intensively developed catchment. The ability to identify, describe and manage this decoupled condition will be critical to water security in the megacities of the future in the face of rapid urbanisation and growing groundwater demand worldwide. 5 Conclusion This study offers solid statistical and spatial observations of the onset of hydrological decoupling in the Beijing Municipality. This multi-source, long-term dataset (2005–2023) combined with non-parametric trend analyses and antecedent window correlations was used to illustrate that the dynamics of this heavily managed megacity have been effectively taken out of the control of natural climatic variations. The negligible correlation between annual precipitation and groundwater depth (; ) and absence of any significant delayed response in all the seasonal climatological windows strongly refute the classical hydro-climatic paradigm for the region. Many more variables control the groundwater changes than natural recharge. Spatial analyses indicate that the natural precipitation inputs to the aquifer are intercepted by the consumptive water demand of the vegetation managed by humans and by the physical sealing of the aquifer by impervious surfaces with high concentration. Because natural climatic forcing has been decoupled from the aquifer, the future trajectory of groundwater in Beijing will be dictated entirely by anthropogenic policy choices (extraction quotas and inter-basin transfers), not by climatic consequences. While this study projects future climatic drought risk, it does not simulate future groundwater levels, emphasizing that long-term water security now rests on active governance, not meteorological relief. The results suggest a potential paradigm shift in urban water resource management for heavily managed megacities. Historical precipitation-groundwater relationships are not a viable option in rapidly urbanizing megacities around the world for use in resource planning. Continued monitoring networks with high resolution and legally binding water extraction quotas are essential to the future of water security in such environments. This decoupled state must be recognized, characterized and actively managed to avoid irreversible deformation of the aquifer and sustainable water management in the Anthropocene.
