It has turned out to be a significant cause of global warming, climate change and air pollution. This paper examines the impact of urbanization and fossil fuel energy consumption on black carbon emissions across 125 countries from 2005 to 2022 with the help of both panel regression and multiple linear regression model estimated using OLS. The results of the F-test, LM test and Hausman test support the Fixed Effects Model at the p value < 0.01 level. This includes strong country-specific heterogeneity among MLR that indicates countries such as Cote d’Ivoire, Romania, Belarus, Azerbaijan and Namibia, demonstrating a strong positive significance between urbanization and fossil fuel energy consumption on black carbon emissions. In contrast, developed countries such as Iceland, Belgium and Canada, UK, Ireland and many others have negative or weak coefficients, suggesting the positive impact of clean technologies and stricter policies. The novel contribution of this study lies in combining global coverage with heterogeneous country level impact analysis and suggestions for policymakers with evidence-based insights on integrated urban planning and energy efficiency that will help to reduce black carbon emissions and achieve sustainable development goals such as promoting good health and wellbeing (SDG3), fostering sustainable cities (SDG11) and climate action (SDG 13). Pollution Energy and Society Economy-wide Country Studies Carbon cycle Environmental Impact Fossil Fuel Co-Benefits of Emission Reduction Strategies in Climate Policy 1 Introduction The global community continues to prioritize the Sustainable Development Goals(SDGs) and the climate change mitigation under the Paris Agreement adopted in 2015 [ 1 ]. Yet despite international commitments to limit global warming to well below 2 °C, emissions from both long lived greenhouse gases and short lived climate pollutant remain a significant challenge [ 2 ]. Among short lived climate pollutants, black carbon emissions(BCE) have attracted increasing scientific and policy attention due to their strong warming potential and severe health implications [ 3 ]. Black carbon emissions refers to strongly light absorbing carbon particles released into the atmosphere during the incomplete combustion of fossil fuels, biofuels and biomass [ 4 ]. And as a major component of fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅), black carbon contributes substantially to atmospheric warming and adverse health outcomes, including cardiovascular and respiratory diseases [ 5 , 6 ]. Unlike carbon dioxide (CO₂), which remains in the atmosphere for centuries, black carbon persists for only days to weeks but has a disproportionately high radiative forcing effect [ 7 ]. Recent global emission inventories and satellite-based datasets confirm the significant contribution of anthropogenic black carbon to near term climate forcing and regional environmental degradation [ 3 , 8 ]. In empirical macroeconomies studies, BCE typically represents national level black carbon emissions from sectors such as residential combustion, transportation, industry, and open burning, and it used to analyze linkages between energy systems, urban development, and climate impacts [ 9 ]. Fossil fuel energy consumption (FFEC) refers to the amount of primary energy derived from coal, oil, and natural gas within an economy, commonly measured as a percentage of total energy consumption [ 10 ]. FFEC serves as a widely accepted indicator of a country’s energy structure and carbon intensity [ 11 ]. Although fossil fuel combustion is typically associated with carbon dioxide emissions, it is also a primary source of black carbon when combustion efficiency is low and emission control technologies are inadequate [ 12 ]. Recent empirical evidence suggests that the environmental impact of fossil fuel consumption is asymmetric and varies across income levels depending on technologies advancement and regulatory enforcement [ 13 ]. Therefore, examining FFEC in relation to BCE is essential for understanding short lived climate pollutant dynamics within energy dependent economies. Urbanization (URB) refers to the increasing concentration of population in urban areas and is commonly measured as the percentage of the total population residing in cities [ 1 ]. Rapid urban expansion alters of energy demand, transportation intensity, infrastructure development, and industrial activity [ 14 ]. Urbanization may increase black carbon emissions through higher transportation density, electricity demand, and construction activities. However, it may also contribute to emission reductions through technological upgrading, centralized heating systems, improved public transport, and stricter environment regulation [ 15 ]. Empirical studies show that the environmental effects of urbanization are not uniform and depend heavily on institutional quality, structural transformation, and energy mix composition [ 11 ]. Consequently, the relationship between urbanization and BCE remains theoretically ambiguous and warrants systematic global investigation. While extensive literature has examined the determinants of carbon dioxide emissions, comparatively fewer studies have focused on black carbon emissions from a macroeconomic perspective. Existing research largely concentrates on country specific emission inventories or regional atmospheric analyses [ 9 , 16 ]. Moreover, most global panel studies emphasize CO₂ within the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) framework, with limited attention to short lived climate pollutant such as BC [ 17 ]. Given that black carbon formation is highly sensitive to combustion efficiency and regulatory standards, its relationship with economic and demographic transformation may differ significantly from that of CO₂. This creates a substantial gap in globally comparable econometric evidence examining how fossil fuel energy consumption and urbanization jointly influence black carbon emissions across heterogeneous national contexts. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to examine the impact of FFEC and URB on BCE across 125 countries over the period 2005–2022. By employing panel data econometric techniques alongside country level multiple liner regression (MLR) analysis, the study provides comprehensive cross-country evidence on structural drivers of BCE. In doing so, study contributes to the literature in four distinct ways, First, it extends macro environmental econometric analysis beyond carbon dioxide by focusing specifically on BCE, thereby incorporating short lived climate pollutants into global panel modeling. Second, it simultaneously examines FFEC and URB within unified empirical framework, offering a more integrated understanding of structural emissions drivers. Third, by combining panel regression with country specific MLR analysis, the study identifies cross county heterogeneity and distinct emission regimes that aggregate models may conceal. Fourth, the finding generates evidence based and differentiated policy implications aligned with SDG 13 (Climate Action), SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), and SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), thereby supporting targeted mitigation strategies within broader energy transition pathways. Through these global assessments, the study advances understanding of how structural transformation in energy systems and urban development shapes black carbon emissions and informs climate policy design in the context of sustainable development. 2 Literature review Black carbon emission has become a major focus area in environmental research due to its strong climate warming potential and links to human activities. Wildfire, agricultural residue biomass burning and residential use of solid fuels remain significant contributors to black carbon emissions [ 3 , 18 , 19 , 20 ]. Industrial combustion of coal and gas and the use of other fuels by industries also increase BC emission, especially in coastal areas where the emissions accumulate in the sediments [ 21 ]. Areas with more stringent clean air regulations have recorded reduction in BC emissions, but these reductions remain uneven globally [ 22 ]. Previous studies show that BC emissions are strongly source-dependent and are affected by policy changes, resulting in large global disparities. These findings highlight that BC emissions vary across regions depending on emission sources and regulatory frameworks. The release of BC varies across countries, depending on the energy usage, efficiency of the combustion, and land-use. In South Asia, India shows high spatial and temporal heterogeneity in BC emissions, with the residential biomass burning and transportation [ 23 ]. Similarly, Pakistan’s contributions to BC are significant due to rapid industrial development, energy use and urbanization [ 24 ]. In China, one of the largest BC emitters, saw rapid growth in emission until the early 2000s, but has since decline due to intensive efforts, and further reductions are expected by 2050 [ 25 , 26 , 27 ]. Overall, these studies demonstrate that national energy structures and development patterns strongly influence BC emissions. [ 28 ] shows that in Latin America, the BC inventory in Mexico is shows uncertainty under all methodologies, but transportation and biomass combustion remain the dominant sources. Furthermore, urban studies in Curitiba, Brazil show a strong reliance on vehicle emissions, reflecting methodological challenges common in developing countries [ 29 ]. While historical reconstructions in the united states show that BC emissions decreased due to clean technology and policies [ 30 ]. BC emissions are largely influenced by domestic fuel combustion, transportation, savannah fire and human activities in eastern sub-Saharan Africa [ 29 , 31 ]. These investigations prove that the socioeconomic and environmental factors in the areas predetermine the emissions of BC. These regional differences show that socioeconomic conditions and technology levels shape BC emissions. Urbanization acts as the main driver in Black carbon emissions. Urban areas directly influence BC by changing its spread and settlements. Pollution in cities becomes more severe and lasts longer [ 32 , 33 , 34 ]. Rapid industrialization, extensive transportation systems and concentrated energy use in urban areas are the main sources of emissions in Chinese and Indian cities [ 35 , 36 ]. According to [ 37 , 38 ] urbanization increases BC levels especially in developing nations with inadequate emission control technology including, semi -urban South India and rapidly industrializing Chinese metropolitan areas. African cities provide additional insights into the urbanization BC nexus [ 39 ]. In Nairobi, Kenya, traffic emissions are shown to contribute to BC pollution, showing how rapid urban growth poor emission controls, worsen public health risks [ 40 ]. Study in South Asia indicates that BC emissions induced by urbanization affect regional air quality through long-distance aerosol movement [ 41 , 42 ]. These findings suggest that urban expansion intensifies BC emissions and extends impacts beyond city boundaries. Throughout studies by [ 43 , 44 , 45 ] show that BC emission in Europe is significant in urbanized valley areas, due to burning of biomass particularly domestic wood. Overall, urbanization is generally considered a structural force in environmental pressures. As cities grow in size, economic activity becomes more concentrated, leading to increased transport intensity, vehicle density, and industrial clusters [ 19 , 46 , 47 ]. Growth in infrastructure, housing, and electricity consumption increases residential energy demand [ 48 ]. This growth increases fuel consumption, particularly in the transportation and manufacturing industries [ 16 ] reveal that global BC inventory covering 1960–2017 shows an inverse U-shaped trend, which was rising due to urbanization and then declining as rural emissions decreased. Therefore, urbanization can both increase and reduce BC emissions depending on structural and technological changes. However [ 35 ], reveal that economic diversification such as shift from manufacturing to service oriented economies help to decouple urban growth from BC within cities in China. Furthermore [ 33 , 49 ], argue that even in municipal governance, strict emission measurements and green transportation reduce total BC level of Chinese cities. Developing economy evidence indicates that industrial competitiveness and use of renewable energy can facilitate the adoption of carbon neutrality and technological capacity can help mitigate all the negative environmental impact of high urbanization [ 50 ]. However, evidence on how governance and technology influence BC dynamics in developing countries remains limited. BC emissions are common in urbans areas around the world and fossil fuel energy consumption largely contributes to the release of carbon particles into the atmosphere [ 51 , 52 , 53 ]. The role of fossil fuel sources in BC varies over time and location [ 52 ]. that seasonal increases typically occur during the winter due to increased fossil fuel consumption. Emission are often the highest near roads and ports that shows transportation and shipping are the main sources [ 35 , 36 ]. High BC levels in urban areas in eastern Indian areas are directly linked to traffic, with up to 57% of BC coming from fossil fuels [ 54 ]. Likewise, input-output analysis and structural decomposition show that BC emissions are linked to final energy consumption patterns and industrial fuel mix in China [ 35 ]. The relationship between fossil fuels and BC can also be demonstrated by policy-related changes in energy consumption. During the COVID-19 lockdowns, there were sharp decreases in BC level due to reduced fossil fuel consumption in transport and industry. Hence, Policy recommendations also emphasize improving fuel quality in there sectors [ 21 ]. Additionally, technological advancement, renewables, biofuels, hydrogen and eco-friendly transportation, AI driven energy efficiency and the use of rare earth minerals in renewable energy systems can reduce emissions. This is presented as both a climate mitigation and effective environmental policy to improve air quality [ 11 , 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 ]. Various studies worldwide highlight fossil fuels are major source of BC. Research in India shows that fossil fuels are major source of BC emissions. A case study in Ahmedabad confirmed that fossil fuel combustion is more prevalent than wood combustion [ 53 ]. Further reinforcement is found in a study conducted in Agartala, where it was found that fossil fuels contribute 56% during winter and up to 85% during monsoon [ 59 ]. A study in China shows a strong link between increased coal petroleum use and combustion-related pollutants since 2000 [ 18 , 60 ] pointed that in North Africa, Fossil fuels account for 90.1% and 93.8% of total BC, primarily due to vehicle emissions, industry and other socioeconomic activities. Furthermore long-term study of 27 EU countries in Europe found that BC emissions persisted mainly due to fuel use, economic growth and population [ 9 ]. At the same time, one of the challenges identified in UK is the large discrepancy between different BC emissions from industrial and domestic combustion sources [ 21 , 61 ] points out that more studies are needed on this aspect. Although many studies focus on individual countries, there are very few studies on the global scale. Even previous studies indicate that BCE shaped by urbanization via structural economic patterns [ 35 ], FFEC has uneven effect on the eco system and persistent human activities drive accumulation BCE in cities [ 18 , 62 ]. The current literature has narrow geographic focus and consists of single-region analysis. Research that covers both BCE, FFEC and urbanization in diverse economic circumstances remains prominently lacking on a global scale. Therefore, with a comprehensive analysis backed by an integrated framework that links FFEC, URB and BCE in a way not previously examined, the subject study significantly contributes to the academic discourse, support environmental policy and sustainable development. 2.1 Theoretical framework and conceptual framework We have used integrated theoretical framework that combines multiple environmental and economic theories like STIRPAT (Stochastic Impacts by Regression on Population, Affluence, and Technology) Model, the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis and Ecological Modernization Theory (EMT). STIRPAT model serves as the primary quantitative tool for this framework enabling systematic analysis how population, affluence and technology influence environmental impacts such as BCE. Previous studies have successfully applied the STIRPAT model to examine the effects of demographic, economic and technological factors on environmental degradation [ 63 , 64 ]. Where URB used to capture the demographic concentration and structural transformation dimensions associated with population pressure and economic expansion. FFEC captures technological and energy-structure dimensions, directly linking combustion-based energy use to BC emissions. Accordingly, this study helps to identify cross-country heterogeneity and allows for systematic investigation of human-driven BC emissions across different stages of development [ 63 , 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 ]. The heterogeneous findings of this study are broadly consistent with the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis. Countries with fossil fuel intensives show positive and significant impacts of FFEC and URB on BCE reflecting the upward-sloping phase of the EKC. Conversely, advanced economies with cleaner energy transitions, stronger institutional frameworks and technological progress with negative or insignificant relationships, indicate downward-sloping phase of the EKC [ 69 , 70 , 71 ]. Ecological Modernization Theory (EMT) hypothesis supports this study by explaining how transportation efficiency, hydrogen power and other sustainable technologies can reduce BCE while promoting urbanization [ 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 75 ]. Furthermore, Ecological Unequal Exchange (EUE) theory supports to elucidate how institutional and legal limitations may cause variations from EKC predictions and cross-country variations in BCE trends [ 76 , 77 , 78 ]. The panel covers 125 countries for 2005–2022. The period is selected because internationally comparable black carbon data become consistently available from the mid-2000s onwards (Table 1 ). It also captures structural changes in global urbanization and energy systems while minimizing missing observations. Countries are included based one consistent data availability for all variables, representation across income groups and variation in energy structures and urbanization levels. This board sample improves external validity and enables systematic cross country heterogeneity analysis (Table 1 ). This indicates that fossil fuel intensity alone does not fully explain differences in BCE, a pattern consistent with prior work showing that BC emissions depend more on combustion efficiency, fuel composition, emissions factors, and pollution control technologies rather than aggregate fossil energy use [ 3 , 4 , 12 , 16 ]. Updated emission inventories support this view showing that countries with comparable energy consumption levels may exhibit substantially different BC emission intensities due to variations in sectoral combustion technologies and regulatory enforcement [ 28 , 29 , 30 , 79 ]. This distinguishes BC from CO 2 emission chemically proportional to fossil carbon oxidation, BC forms under incomplete combustion conditions and is therefore highly sensitive to combustion design and fuel quality [ 80 , 81 ]. This explains why fossil consumption does not translate mechanically into proportional BC emissions across countries. 5.2 Panel-level findings: structural cross-country effects At the panel level, the fixed effect panel result show that URB has a statistically significant association with BCE, whereas fossil energy consumption does not exhibit a uniform effect at the aggregate level. The insignificance of FFEC at the panel level indicates heterogeneous structural elasticities across countries. However aligns with asymmetric energy–environment literature demonstrating that fossil fuel impacts depend on institutional quality, technological upgrading, and regulatory capacity [ 56 , 62 , 63 ]. From STIRPAT perspective [ 64 ], environmental impacts reflect not only scale effects but also technological and structural mediators .Recent extensions confirm that energy composition and institutional thresholds condition emissions outcomes [ 30 , 65 , 72 ]. Urbanization reshapes residential heating, transport systems, and individual clustering [ 35 , 82 ]. However ecological modernization theory suggest that at higher income levels, urbanization can facilitate efficiency improvements through centralized infrastructure and regulatory enforcement [ 73 , 74 ]. Institutional threshold effects further moderate the urbanization emissions nexus [ 14 , 83 ]. Thus, the aggregate insignificant of FFEC conceals structured heterogeneity rather than absence of effect. 5.3 Cross-country heterogeneity The country level estimates confirm that the insignificance of FFEC in the aggregate panel model conceals substantial structural heterogeneity. Rather than global elasticity, the results identify three systematic regimes that align with theoretical and empirical strands in the environmental economics literature. 5.3.1 Reinforcing effects of FFEC and URB on BCE Azerbaijan, Belarus, Côte d’Ivoire, Namibia, Romania, Congo, Mongolia, and Yemen exhibit positive and statistically significant effects for both FFEC and URB. The magnitude of FFEC elasticities is considerable in Côte d’Ivoire (0.0070) and Congo (0.0059), while Belarus (0.0025), Namibia (0.0026), and Mongolia (0.0018) shows smaller yet significant coefficient. URB elasticities range from 0.008 to 0.060, with stronger urban effect observed in Congo (0.0604) and Romania (0.0398). These results suggest complementary effect, fossil fuel reliance and rapid urban concentration operate jointly to intensify combustion related emissions. This pattern reflects a reinforcing combustion urbanization mechanism, where rapid urban growth increases transport demand, residential heating, diesel generator use, and industrial combustion without equivalent improvements in emission control technologies. Similar mechanisms are documented in fossil intensive and lower income context, where incomplete combustion and reliance on solid fuels remain prevalent [ 4 , 23 , 31 ] and it consistent with inventory based studies further confirm that inefficient combustion processes are dominant BC source in transitional and resource dependent economies [ 16 , 24 ]. It also corroborates the STIRPAT base analyses which demonstrating that energy intensity and fossil reliance significantly elevate environmental pressure in developing regions [ 63 , 64 ]. In this regime, urbanization operates as a scale effect, amplifying combustion activity faster than upgrading can offer it. 5.3.2 Adverse effects of FFEC and URB on BCE A contrasting group of advanced European economies including Belgium, Estonia, Greece, Ireland, Luxembourg, Portugal, Spain, and the United Kingdom show negative and statistically significant URB coefficients and negative FFEC effects. This pattern indicates partial or full decoupling between FFEC, URB and BCE In advanced economies, urbanization is often associated with centralized heating systems, electrified public transport, renewable integration and strict particulate emission standards. These mechanisms reduce per capita combustion intensity even as urban density increases. This finding well align with ecological modernization theory [ 73 ] which posits that technological innovation and institutional reform and transform environmental pressure into efficiency gains. Empirical evidence supports this moderating role of institutions and energy mix in shaping the urbanization emissions nexus [ 14 , 83 ] . Moreover, coordinated clean air policies have been shown to sustainability reduce BCE even during continued economic expansion [ 22 , 49 ]. Unlike CO 2 emissions which are chemically tied to FFEC, BCE are highly responsive to improvements in combustion efficiency and filtration technologies [ 12 , 80 ]. Therefore, negative GGEC coefficients in some countries likely reflect structural substitution effects, where cleaner fossil technologies replace more polluting traditional fuels. 5.3.3 Mixed effects of FFEC and URB on BCE A third group of countries like China, Indonesia, Japan, Cambodia, Nepal, and Thailand show mixed or statistically insignificant effects. This pattern suggests transitional dynamics. Inn these economies, FFEC may remain high, but technological upgrading, regulatory tightening and structural transformation moderate the translation of energy use into BCE. China’s Clean Air Action Plan, achieved significant BC reductions despite sustained fossil energy demand [ 22 ]. Updated global BC inventories similarly show that emission intensity can decline even when aggregate consumption rises [ 3 , 16 ]. Updated global BC inventory similarly shoes that emission intensity can decline even when aggregate consumption rises [ 56 , 62 ]. Most macro level environmental studies focus primarily on CO 2 emissions and the EKC [ 70 , 71 ]. However, BC differs fundamentally as a short lived climate pollutant with immediate radiative and health effects [ 4 , 84 ]. Because BC formation depends heavily on combustion efficiency, its elasticity with respect to FFEC is inherently more heterogeneous than that of CO 2 . While [ 35 ] and [ 82 ] identify structural drivers of BC within China, the present study extends the analysis globally and demonstrates that heterogeneous regimes are not country specific anomalies but systematic structural patterns. Furthermore [ 9 ], document persistence in BCE across European countries, our findings complement this by identifying the structural determinants underlying such persistence differences. Taking Together, the evidence suggests that the BCE, FFECC and URB relationship is not liner or universal. Instead, it’s coordinated by Energy composition and combustion technology, Institutional quality and regulatory enforcement by, Energy structure efficiency, and Structural transformation stage. The regime-based interpretation provides a more nuanced explanation than conventional homogeneous panel estimated and clarifies why FFEC does not exert a statistically uniform impact on BCE at the global level. 6 Conclusion The present research investigated the relationship between the use of fossil fuel energy and urbanization on black carbon emissions for 125 countries in the period from 2005 to 2022 via panel data regression. The research findings indicate that FFEC and urbanization affect BCE; however, their impacts on black carbon emissions depend on each country based on their level of development, policy implementation, and technology advancement. High levels of BCE are observed in developing and transitional economies owing to their reliance on fossil fuels, high levels of urbanization, and inefficiencies in their energy sectors, whereas developed economies have weak or negative relationships because of cleaner technologies, proper policies, and efficient control of emissions. There are certain limitations in this study. Firstly, the study was done using secondary data sources, hence there might be errors in the measurements, and some observations are missing. Secondly, national-level data may lead to masking regional differences in terms of urbanization and energy consumption. Thirdly, the study focuses on FFEC and urbanization as the main BCE determinants, whereas many other factors like transport emission, residential fuel use, renewable energy, and policy variables were not considered. Future research can use dynamic panel approaches such as GMM models along with adding other variables to study time effects and causal mechanisms. 7 Policy recommendations Based on descriptive statistics and panel regression of BCE, FFEC and URB analysis, distinct cross-country patterns in the relationship between FFEC, URB and BCE are identified. These findings demonstrate that BCE reduction strategies should be aligned with national energy structure, urban development pathway and technological conditions rather than applying a universal policy approach. These insights underscore the need for evidence based, context specific policy interventions reflecting the heterogeneous emission regimes identified in this study. 7.1 Specific policy recommendations based on descriptive statistics Resting on the descriptive statistics and the visualization that has been provided regarding BCE, FFEC and URB, the policy recommendations are offered to deal with the issues of environmental sustainability, energy transition, and urban development: High BCE countries like Gabon and Equatorial Guinea need to focus on cleaner industrial and domestic energy technologies. The policies must be implemented to shift solid fuel to LPG or electricity in homes and use of particulate filters I vehicles and industrial facilities. Behavioral change can also be facilitated by public awareness campaigns on the health and environmental effects of BC. Countries with high fossil fuel dependance should prioritize improving combustion efficiency and reducing emission intensity rather than focusing only on reducing aggregate energy consumption. This includes accelerating renewable energy adoption, improving fuel quality, updating industrial combustion systems and strengthening emission control technologies. In the case of highly urbanized cities like Hong Kong, Belgium and those of Uruguay, environmentally friendly planning requires integrated urban planning. They need to promote the compact city design, develop a better transport network and develop more green areas as a way of improving air quality and mitigating the urban heat island effect. Smart city technologies, efficient public transportation systems, strict vehicle emission standards and improved urban infrastructure can reduce BCE intensity by increasing energy efficiency and reducing incomplete combustion sources Cross cutting strategies must incorporate reinforcement of data monitoring system in monitoring progress in emissions reductions and energy efficiency, development of regional cooperation in terms of knowledge sharing and technology transfer and making sure the policies are inclusive in equitable especially of the vulnerable populations in fast urbanizing regions. 7.2 Specific policy recommendations based on the cross-country heterogeneity and panel regression findings The finding of the panel regression indicates that there are three empirical patterns. First, fossil fuel energy consumption, as well as urbanization has both a strong and significant positive impact on black carbon emissions in a sub net of countries (Azerbaijan, Belarus, Cote d’Ivoire, Namibia, Romania, Congo and Mongolia) suggesting that urbanization is a high emission regime. Second, URB is negatively related to BCE and FFEC coefficients are small or even negative in most higher economies (e.g. Belgium, France, Canada, Germany, United States), indicating further urbanization can coexist with the reduction of the emission intensity. Third, a number of the transition cases (e.g. Estonia, Moldova< Cameroon) exhibit negative FFEC coefficients, which is also active in the transition of the highly polluted traditional fuels towards comparatively cleaner energy sources (Appendix 3 ). This heterogeneity should consequently be represented in policy suggestions as opposed to a presumed universal course. For reinforcing regime countries, policies should focus on reducing BCE intensity from rapidly increasing fossil demand .Priority areas include improving fossil fuel combustion efficiency, reducing dependance on high emitting fuels, upgrading urban transport systems and introducing particulate emission control technologies in industrial sectors, which are recognized as major BC source in urbanizing economies [ 21 , 35 , 51 , 52 , 82 ].accelerating the move towards coal-free fuels, heavy oil and diesel to cleaner fuels and more modern energy services, especially in urban areas with diesel generators, charcoal and traditional biomass still common [ 29 , 31 , 81 , 85 , 86 ] and implementing emission-mitigating technologies in urban transport and industry to curtail the BCE effect of the further emission of FFEC and URB. To the high economies which have negative URB coefficients, the findings suggest that certain mixes of cleaner energy sources, high standards and developed urban systems may decouple urban development and BCE. The following cases can be used in concrete models of clean urbanization which can guide the developing countries .Sustaining and increasing further low-carbon electricity (renewables, nuclear, high-efficiency gas) to ensure that the increasing urban electricity demand does not become higher BCE [ 16 , 49 , 54 ]. Maintaining high emission rates in the transport and industrial sectors (vehicle standards, filtration, efficient heating), which are probably the foundation of the negative URB-BCE correlation .Transmit technical and planning expertise (e.g. compact city design, transport-based development) to the fast urbanizing economies, so that they can also replicate the negative marginal effect of URB on BEC that is observed in Belgium, France or Canada. In the case of transition and substitution where FFEC coefficient are negative, the results indicate that switching to less efficient commercial fuels through traditional biomass and low-grade coal can short term decrease BCE, though the growth in total fossil fuel consumption may occur. In transitional regime countries, policies should support gradual fuel substitution, cleaner household energy systems, improved combustion efficiency and stronger regulatory frameworks to ensure that short term emission reductions contribute to long term sustainability energy transitions rather than creating new fossil fuel dependencies. Overall, the empirical evidence demonstrates that BCE mitigation requires policies tailored to different stages of the urbanization energy emission pathway. Reinforce regime countries require emission intensity reduction, mitigating regime countries should reserve efficiency oriented urban development patterns and transitional regime countries require balanced fuel substitution and technological upgrading. Moreover, policies should promote green technology, prevent greenwashing and incentivize green investment and eco innovation to support sustainable development across all economies [ 87 , 88 , 89 , 90 ]. Taken together, the empirical evidence across all groups shows that policies must be aligned with a country’s position on Urbanization energy emissions spectrum identified in our fixed effect results in high emission urbanization regimes. The priority should be to reduce the BCE intensity of rapidly rising FF demand in advanced economies, the focus is on preserving and diffusing the conditions under which URB has become negative predictor of BCE and in transition economics carefully fuel substitution can deliver initial BCE reduction part of a broader shift towards cleaner energy system [ 91 , 92 ].
