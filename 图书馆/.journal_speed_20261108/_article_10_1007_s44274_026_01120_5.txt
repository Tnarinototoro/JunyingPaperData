Using a panel of 47 Sub-Saharan African countries over the period 2005–2024, the study constructs a residual-based CO₂–GDP decoupling index and applies two-way fixed-effects estimation, dynamic two-step System Generalized Method of Moments (System GMM), and Method of Moments Quantile Regression (MM-QR) to address unobserved heterogeneity, endogeneity, dynamic persistence, and distributional heterogeneity. The results show that national innovation capacity and institutional quality are positively and statistically associated with CO₂–GDP decoupling in the fixed-effects and MM-QR estimations, with the positive effects observed across lower, median, and upper conditional quantiles of the decoupling distribution. In contrast, neither effect is statistically significant in the short-run dynamic System GMM specifications, suggesting that the contribution of innovation and institutional quality to decoupling operates primarily through gradual structural adjustments rather than immediate changes in emissions intensity. Globalization is consistently associated with weaker decoupling, and its adverse effect becomes larger at higher conditional quantiles. Continuous time-interaction estimates further show that the marginal effect of national innovation capacity on decoupling strengthens progressively over the sample period, indicating a growing contribution of innovation to the separation of economic growth from CO₂ emissions. The findings suggest that innovation capacity alone may not be sufficient to achieve sustained growth–emissions decoupling. Effective decarbonization requires technological upgrading to be supported by stronger institutions, financial allocation toward low-carbon activities, and trade and external economic policies that limit the transmission of carbon-intensive production and investment. The study concludes that Innovation alone is insufficient to guarantee stronger decoupling; it must be institutionally enabled, financially directed, and environmentally targeted. Economic Development, Innovation and Growth Economics of Innovation Knowledge and Innovation Economic Growth Development Economics African Economics 1 Introduction For much of modern economic history, economic growth has been inseparable from rising carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions [ 17 ]. Industrialisation, energy-intensive production, and expanding consumption have ensured that increases in output are typically accompanied by proportional increases in environmental pressure [ 26 ]. This historical linkage underpins the central dilemma of contemporary climate policy: how economies can continue to grow while substantially reducing carbon emissions. As global climate risks intensify and international mitigation commitments become more stringent, resolving this tension has moved from a theoretical concern to an urgent empirical and policy challenge. Recent decades, however, we have witnessed a growing interest in the concept of decoupling, defined as the weakening or breakdown of the relationship between economic growth and environmental degradation [ 18 ]. Unlike emissions-level analyses, decoupling focuses on whether economies can expand output while stabilizing or reducing emissions intensity. Evidence from selected advanced economies suggests that relative or even absolute decoupling may be achievable under certain conditions, particularly where technological progress, structural transformation, and strong institutions coexist [ 11 , 15 ]. Yet, the drivers of decoupling remain unevenly understood, and empirical findings are often context-specific and inconclusive. A large strand of the empirical literature has relied on the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis, which posits a nonlinear relationship between income and environmental degradation [ 20 , 21 , 25 , 26 , 28 ]. While the EKC framework has generated valuable insights, it has also attracted sustained criticism. First, EKC estimates often yield unstable turning points and weak predictive power across countries [ 21 ]. Second, income-based nonlinearities provide limited guidance on how decoupling occurs, offering little insight into the mechanisms that enable economies to grow with lower carbon intensity [ 13 , 14 ]. Most importantly, EKC-based studies tend to treat technological progress as implicit or exogenous, rather than as a measurable and policy-relevant driver of environmental performance. Against this background, Innovation has emerged as a central candidate mechanism for achieving growth–emissions decoupling [ 10 ]. Technological advances can reduce the carbon intensity of production through energy efficiency improvements, cleaner production processes, and the diffusion of low-carbon technologies [ 2 , 5 , 6 ]. However, Innovation does not operate in a vacuum. Its effectiveness depends critically on the institutional environment within which it is embedded. Weak governance, regulatory uncertainty, and limited absorptive capacity can undermine the environmental returns to Innovation, even when Innovation indicators appear strong on paper [ 1 , 8 , 12 ]. Conversely, high-quality institutions can amplify the environmental benefits of technological progress by facilitating coordination, enforcement, and long-term investment. Despite the growing recognition of these interactions, existing empirical studies suffer from three important limitations. First, much of the literature continues to focus on emissions levels rather than decoupling outcomes, conflating scale effects with efficiency improvements. Second, Innovation is often proxied narrowly or treated linearly, ignoring the possibility that its impact on decoupling may be nonlinear or conditional on institutional quality. Third, many studies rely on static or mean-based estimators, which fail to account for persistence, endogeneity, and heterogeneity across countries at different stages of decoupling. This paper addresses these gaps by examining whether national Innovation capacity accelerates the decoupling of economic growth from CO₂ emissions, and how institutional quality conditions this relationship. Rather than asking whether growth eventually reduces emissions, the study asks whether Innovation enables economies to grow without proportionally increasing carbon emissions. Methodologically, the analysis adopts a decoupling-index framework and combines fixed-effects estimation, dynamic System GMM, and quantile regression techniques to capture persistence, endogeneity, nonlinearities, and distributional heterogeneity. Innovation is modeled both linearly and nonlinearly, allowing for diminishing or accelerating effects as Innovation capacity deepens, while institutional quality is explicitly incorporated as both a direct and moderating factor. Using a balanced panel of countries covering the period 2005–2024, the study provides new evidence on the structural and institutional conditions under which Innovation contributes to environmentally sustainable growth. The findings show that Innovation capacity is positively associated with decoupling in the fixed-effects and quantile specifications, although this relationship is not robustly detected in the dynamic System GMM estimates. Institutional quality also supports decoupling in the fixed-effects and quantile models, while the dynamic results suggest that these effects may operate more through gradual structural channels than short-run adjustment. Third, it offers policy-relevant insights for climate strategy, highlighting why innovation-led improvements in decoupling may succeed in some contexts but fail in others. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant theoretical and empirical literature. Section 3 outlines the data, methodology, and econometric strategy. Section 4 presents the empirical results, while Sect. 5 discusses the findings and their implications. Section 6 concludes with policy recommendations and directions for future research. 2 Literature review 2.1 Theoretical review The relationship between economic growth and environmental degradation has long been examined through the lens of the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC), which posits an inverted U-shaped relationship between income and pollution [ 24 ]. Early EKC-based reasoning suggests that emissions rise during early stages of development due to industrialization and scale effects, but decline at higher income levels as economies transition toward services, adopt cleaner technologies, and strengthen environmental regulation [ 16 , 21 , 25 ]. While this framework has been influential, recent theoretical work increasingly questions its adequacy for explaining contemporary environmental challenges, particularly climate change. One major limitation of the EKC framework is its implicit treatment of technological progress. Innovation is often assumed to occur automatically as income rises, rather than being modeled as a measurable and policy-driven process [ 21 ]. This assumption obscures the mechanisms through which economies may decouple growth from emissions. Recent theoretical contributions emphasize Innovation-led efficiency effects, where technological progress reduces the carbon intensity of production independently of income levels [ 3 ]. In this view, decoupling is not an automatic outcome of development but a function of deliberate investments in Innovation, energy efficiency, and low-carbon technologies. Institutional theory further complements this perspective by highlighting the role of governance quality in shaping environmental outcomes. Strong institutions enhance regulatory enforcement, reduce uncertainty, and align incentives for firms to invest in cleaner technologies [ 1 , 8 , 18 , 23 ]. Conversely, weak institutional environments may blunt the effectiveness of Innovation, allowing scale effects to dominate efficiency gains. Theoretical models that integrate Innovation systems and institutional quality therefore predict conditional and nonlinear decoupling, where the environmental returns to Innovation depend on governance capacity and structural context. More recent frameworks move beyond emissions-level analysis to a decoupling paradigm, which explicitly distinguishes between economic expansion and environmental pressure [ 11 ]. This approach aligns with production-function theory, where output growth and emissions intensity are jointly determined by technology, institutional effectiveness, and factor allocation. Under this framework, Innovation and institutions interact to shift production toward lower-carbon trajectories, making decoupling a dynamic and path-dependent process rather than a deterministic income effect. 2.2 Empirical review A study by Aquilas et al. [ 6 ] examine the relationship between industrialization and environmental sustainability using panel data methods. Their analysis shows that industrial expansion initially increases environmental pressure, but the effect weakens in contexts where technological upgrading occurs. A key strength of the study lies in its attention to structural transformation, but it does not explicitly model Innovation capacity or address emissions–growth decoupling directly. Similarly, Ahmed et al. [ 2 ] investigate the environmental impact of industrialization using nonlinear econometric techniques. While they confirm the presence of EKC-type behavior, they also find that technological progress alters the emissions trajectory. Unlike Aquilas et al. [ 6 ], Ahmed et al. incorporate technology proxies, but both studies focus on emissions levels rather than decoupling outcomes. These studies highlight the importance of technology in moderating environmental impacts but stop short of examining whether Innovation enables economies to grow while reducing emissions intensity. This motivates the present study’s shift toward a decoupling-based framework. Also, Iqbal et al. [ 13 ] analyze the impact of green technological Innovation and ICT development on CO₂ emissions using dynamic panel techniques. Their findings indicate that green Innovation significantly reduces emissions, particularly in countries with higher technological readiness. The study’s strength lies in its explicit treatment of Innovation dynamics and endogeneity. Lee and Han [ 17 ] similarly examine environmental Innovation and CO₂ emissions, finding that Innovation contributes to emissions reduction but with diminishing returns at higher levels of technological maturity. While both studies emphasize Innovation, Lee and Han focus on advanced economies, whereas Iqbal et al. include a broader country set. Although both studies demonstrate that Innovation reduces emissions, neither examines whether Innovation weakens the link between emissions and economic growth. The present study extends this line of inquiry by explicitly modeling Innovation’s role in accelerating growth–emissions decoupling. Alexiou [ 4 ] investigates the relationship between patent systems and CO₂ emissions, showing that stronger Innovation institutions enhance the effectiveness of technological change in reducing emissions. The study underscores the institutional foundations of environmental Innovation but relies on patent counts as a narrow proxy for Innovation capacity. Eugster [ 10 ] focuses on environmental policy and clean Innovation, demonstrating that policy stringency improves Innovation outcomes and environmental performance. While both studies emphasize institutions, Eugster highlights regulatory channels, whereas Alexiou focuses on Innovation systems. These studies suggest that institutions condition the environmental impact of Innovation, yet they do not assess how institutional quality interacts with Innovation to influence decoupling. This study addresses that gap by explicitly modeling Innovation–institution complementarities. Best et al. [ 9 ] assess the effectiveness of carbon pricing across countries and find that pricing mechanisms reduce emissions but may dampen growth if institutional capacity is weak. Their work highlights the trade-off between growth and environmental policy in weak institutional contexts. Polat and Çil [ 21 ] test the EKC hypothesis using panel threshold models and find that institutional quality shifts emissions trajectories. While both studies emphasize institutions, Polat and Çil provide evidence of nonlinear effects that are consistent with threshold-driven decoupling. Existing evidence confirms nonlinear and institutional effects but remains centered on emissions levels. This study advances the literature by integrating Innovation, institutions, and nonlinear dynamics within a decoupling framework. The literature reveals three unresolved issues. First, most studies examine emissions levels rather than decoupling outcomes, making it difficult to distinguish scale effects from improvements in emissions efficiency. Second, Innovation is often treated as a linear and uniformly beneficial driver of environmental performance, even though its effects may depend on absorptive capacity, institutional quality, and the direction of technological change. Third, existing studies rarely combine decoupling measures, Innovation capacity, institutional conditions, dynamic endogeneity, and distributional heterogeneity in one empirical framework. This study addresses these gaps by modelling national Innovation capacity and institutional quality within a residual-based decoupling framework using fixed-effects, System GMM, and Method of Moments Quantile Regression. 2.3 Hypotheses development Grounded in Innovation theory, institutional economics, and the decoupling literature, the study advances the following hypotheses: H1: National Innovation capacity significantly accelerates the decoupling of economic growth from CO₂ emissions. H2: The relationship between national Innovation capacity and growth–emissions decoupling may exhibit nonlinear effects as Innovation capacity deepens. H3: Institutional quality has a direct positive effect on growth–emissions decoupling. H4: Institutional quality conditions the relationship between national Innovation capacity and growth–emissions decoupling. H5: The effects of Innovation capacity, institutional quality, and globalization differ across the conditional distribution of growth–emissions decoupling. 2.4 Conceptual framework The conceptual framework of this study views growth–emissions decoupling as the outcome of a dynamic interaction between Innovation capacity, institutional quality, and economic activity. Economic growth generates scale effects that increase emissions, while Innovation introduces efficiency effects that reduce carbon intensity. Institutional quality operates as an enabling mechanism that conditions the effectiveness of Innovation by shaping incentives, enforcement, and absorptive capacity. Innovation capacity influences decoupling both directly, by reducing emissions per unit of output, and indirectly, through its interaction with institutional quality. Institutional quality may support decoupling by improving regulatory capacity, policy credibility, and coordination. It may also condition the effectiveness of Innovation, although this moderating role remains an empirical question tested in the analysis. These relationships may vary across countries and decoupling regimes, reflecting differences in technological maturity, institutional capacity, and structural conditions. This framework underpins the empirical strategy adopted in the study, which combines fixed-effects, dynamic System GMM, and quantile regression methods to capture persistence, endogeneity, nonlinearities, and distributional heterogeneity in the decoupling process Fig. 1 illustrates the conceptual framework. Fig. 1 Full size image Conceptual framework 3 Methodology 3.1 Data and sample This study uses a panel dataset covering 47 Sub-Saharan African countries over the period 2005–2024. The period is appropriate because it captures important changes in Innovation capacity, institutional development, economic growth, and climate policy orientation across the region. It also covers the post-2015 Sustainable Development Goals and Paris Agreement period, while providing sufficient observations before 2015 for comparison. The full panel consists of 47 countries over 20 years, yielding 940 country-year observations. After Cook’s distance screening, the main fixed-effects sample contains 884 observations. The System GMM estimation uses 893 observations because lag construction and instrument availability alter the usable dynamic sample. However, all 47 countries remain represented in the final GMM specifications. Data on CO₂ emissions, GDP per capita, GDP growth, domestic credit to the private sector, and public education expenditure were obtained from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators. Institutional quality indicators were drawn from the Worldwide Governance Indicators. National Innovation capacity was measured using the Global Innovation Index. Additional globalization-related indicators were obtained from publicly available international databases, including The Global Economy database where relevant. 3.2 Variable construction 3.2.1 Dependent variable: CO₂–GDP decoupling index The dependent variable is a CO₂–GDP decoupling index. Following the residual-based decoupling literature, the study constructs the index by first estimating the relationship between CO₂ emissions and economic output. This approach makes it possible to determine whether a country emits more or less carbon than would be expected given its level of economic activity. The first-stage emissions-growth relationship is specified in Eq. ( 1 ): $$\begin{array}{cccc}& \text{l}\text{n}(C{O}_{2it})=\alpha +\beta \text{l}\text{n}(GD{P}_{it})+{\mu}_{i}+{\lambda}_{t}+{\varepsilon}_{it}& & \end{array}$$ (1) In Eq. ( 1 ), \(\text{l}\text{n}(C{O}_{2it})\) denotes the natural logarithm of CO₂ emissions per capita for country \(i\) in year \(t\) , while \(\text{l}\text{n}(GD{P}_{it})\) denotes the natural logarithm of GDP per capita. The term \({\mu}_{i}\) captures country fixed effects, \({\lambda}_{t}\) captures year fixed effects, and \({\varepsilon}_{it}\) is the residual term. The residual from Eq. ( 1 ) captures the part of CO₂ emissions that is not explained by economic scale, country-specific characteristics, or common year effects. The decoupling index is then constructed from the residual obtained in Eq. ( 1 ), as shown in Eq. ( 2 ): $$D_{it} = - \left( {\frac{{\hat{\varepsilon}_{it} - \overline{\varepsilon }}}{{\sigma_{\varepsilon } }}} \right)$$ (2) In Eq. ( 2 ), \({D}_{it}\) represents the CO₂–GDP decoupling index, \({\widehat{\varepsilon }}_{it}\) is the estimated residual from Eq. ( 1 ), \(\overline{\varepsilon }\) is the mean of the residuals, and \({\sigma}_{\varepsilon }\) is the standard deviation of the residuals. The negative sign in Eq. ( 2 ) is introduced so that higher values of the index indicate stronger decoupling. A positive value of \({D}_{it}\) means that a country emits less CO₂ than would be predicted by its level of economic activity. A negative value indicates coupling, meaning that emissions exceed the level predicted by GDP. This residual-based construction is useful because it separates environmental performance from economic size. Instead of simply asking whether emissions are high or low, the index asks whether emissions are high or low relative to the level of economic activity. This makes the index more suitable for examining whether Innovation capacity helps countries grow with lower carbon intensity. However, because GDP per capita is used in the construction of the decoupling index in Eq. ( 1 ), GDP-related variables are interpreted cautiously in the regression analysis. Their coefficients are treated as controls for short-run growth dynamics rather than as independent causal effects on the decoupling index. 3.2.2 Main explanatory variable: national innovation capacity The main explanatory variable is national Innovation capacity, measured using the Global Innovation Index (GII). The GII is published by the World Intellectual Property Organization and provides a composite measure of national Innovation performance. It is scored on a scale from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating stronger Innovation capacity. This study uses the published overall GII score and does not reconstruct the index. The measure is retained because it captures national innovation capacity as a system-level capability rather than a single input such as research-and-development expenditure or patent counts. The GII includes an institutions pillar, whereas institutional quality is measured separately from the Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI). This creates conceptual proximity but not a mechanical duplication: the two measures are constructed by different organisations from distinct indicator sets and show a moderate correlation in the estimation sample (r = 0.41; Appendix Table 11). The analysis therefore treats their coefficients as conditional associations and does not claim that they identify wholly separable causal mechanisms. In the sample, GII scores range from 10.2 to 40.9, with an average of approximately 24.2. Where country-year GII observations were missing, within-country linear interpolation was used only to preserve continuity in the balanced panel after excluding countries with insufficient focal-variable coverage. Interpolated values do not create new innovation information and may smooth short-run variation. For this reason, the results are interpreted as associations and the GII interpolation is treated as a measurement limitation. The published overall GII score remains the underlying innovation measure. 3.2.3 Institutional quality Institutional quality is measured using a composite index constructed from the Worldwide Governance Indicators. The index is based on government effectiveness, control of corruption, regulatory quality, political stability, and voice and accountability. Rule of law is not included in the baseline composite because its close conceptual and empirical overlap with regulatory quality would place disproportionate weight on a closely related regulatory dimension. This is a measurement choice, not a claim that rule of law is unimportant. Principal Component Analysis is used to combine the five indicators into a single institutional-quality index. The first principal component captures the largest common variation, has a mean close to zero and a standard deviation of approximately 1.88, and explains 84% of the variance (Appendix Table 8). Appendix Table 11 reports correlations and variance-inflation factors; the correlation between GII and the institutional index is 0.41 and the highest VIF is 2.34. These diagnostics do not suggest problematic multicollinearity, but the interpretation remains cautious because innovation systems and institutions are substantively related. 3.2.4 Control variables The study includes a set of control variables that may influence growth–emissions decoupling. These include GDP growth, domestic credit to the private sector, globalization, and public education expenditure. GDP growth is included to control for short-run macroeconomic dynamics that may influence the relationship between emissions and output. However, because GDP per capita is used in constructing the decoupling index in Eq. ( 1 ), the coefficient on GDP growth is not interpreted as a purely independent causal effect. Rather, it captures short-term growth dynamics that may affect decoupling performance after the residual-based index has accounted for the level of economic activity. Domestic credit to the private sector is included as a measure of financial depth. Financial deepening can support investment in productive capacity and technological adoption, but it may also finance carbon-intensive sectors if credit allocation is not aligned with green investment priorities. Globalization is included to capture the effect of external economic integration. Greater integration may facilitate technology transfer and market access, but it may also intensify emissions through resource extraction, trade-related production, and carbon-intensive supply chains. Public education expenditure is included as a proxy for human capital investment. Education spending may support Innovation capacity and absorptive capability, although its effect on decoupling may operate indirectly rather than immediately. 3.3 Model specification The study examines whether national Innovation capacity and institutional quality contribute to the decoupling of economic growth from CO₂ emissions. The general functional relationship is presented in Eq. ( 3 ): $$\begin{array}{cccc}& {D}_{it}=f(Inno{v}_{it},Ins{t}_{it},{X}_{it})& & \end{array}$$ (3) In Eq. ( 3 ), \({D}_{it}\) denotes the CO₂–GDP decoupling index, \(Inno{v}_{it}\) represents national Innovation capacity, \(Ins{t}_{it}\) represents institutional quality, and \({X}_{it}\) is a vector of control variables. Equation ( 3 ) provides the general analytical structure for the empirical models estimated in the study. To test the direct effect of Innovation and institutions on decoupling, the baseline empirical model is specified in Eq. ( 4 ): $$\begin{array}{cccc}& {D}_{it}=\alpha +{\beta}_{1}Inno{v}_{it}+{\beta}_{2}Ins{t}_{it}+\gamma {X}_{it}+{\mu}_{i}+{\lambda}_{t}+{\varepsilon}_{it}& & \end{array}$$ (4) In Eq. ( 4 ), \({\beta}_{1}\) captures the effect of national Innovation capacity on decoupling, while \({\beta}_{2}\) captures the effect of institutional quality. The vector \({X}_{it}\) contains the control variables, \({\mu}_{i}\) captures country-specific effects, \({\lambda}_{t}\) captures year-specific effects, and \({\varepsilon}_{it}\) is the error term. To examine whether the effect of Innovation on decoupling is nonlinear, the study introduces a quadratic Innovation term, as shown in Eq. ( 5 ): $$\begin{array}{cccc}& {D}_{it}=\alpha +{\beta}_{1}Inno{v}_{it}+{\beta}_{2}Inno{v}_{it}^{2}+{\beta}_{3}Ins{t}_{it}+\gamma {X}_{it}+{\mu}_{i}+{\lambda}_{t}+{\varepsilon}_{it}& & \end{array}$$ (5) Equation ( 5 ) allows the study to test whether Innovation has diminishing or accelerating marginal effects on decoupling as Innovation capacity increases. A positive and statistically significant \({\beta}_{1}\) , combined with a negative and statistically significant \({\beta}_{2}\) , would suggest an inverted-U relationship. This would mean that Innovation improves decoupling up to a point, after which its marginal effect weakens. To test whether institutional quality moderates the effect of Innovation, the study estimates the interaction model presented in Eq. ( 6 ): $$\begin{array}{cccc}& {D}_{it}=\alpha +{\beta}_{1}Inno{v}_{it}+{\beta}_{2}Ins{t}_{it}+{\beta}_{3}(Inno{v}_{it}\times Ins{t}_{it})+\gamma {X}_{it}+{\mu}_{i}+{\lambda}_{t}+{\varepsilon}_{it}& & \end{array}$$ (6) In Eq. ( 6 ), the coefficient \({\beta}_{3}\) captures whether institutional quality changes the effect of Innovation capacity on decoupling. A positive and statistically significant coefficient would suggest that stronger institutions enhance the environmental returns to Innovation. 3.4 Fixed-effects estimation The first stage of the empirical analysis uses fixed-effects estimation. This approach controls for unobserved time-invariant differences across countries, such as geography, long-standing industrial structures, resource endowments, and historical development trajectories. These country-specific factors may influence both Innovation capacity and emissions performance, making fixed effects necessary for reducing omitted-variable bias. The fixed-effects model is specified in Eq. ( 7 ): $$\begin{array}{cccc}& {D}_{it}={\alpha}_{i}+{\beta}_{1}Inno{v}_{it}+{\beta}_{2}Ins{t}_{it}+\gamma {X}_{it}+{\lambda}_{t}+{\varepsilon}_{it}& & \end{array}$$ (7) In Eq. ( 7 ), \({\alpha}_{i}\) represents country fixed effects and \({\lambda}_{t}\) represents year fixed effects. The model estimates whether changes in Innovation capacity and institutional quality within a country over time are associated with changes in decoupling performance. This is important because the fixed-effects approach removes time-invariant differences across countries and focuses on within-country variation. Fixed-effects results are reported using robust standard errors. Additional checks using Driscoll–Kraay standard errors are also conducted to account for heteroskedasticity, serial correlation, and cross-sectional dependence where appropriate. 3.5 Dynamic system GMM estimation The relationship between Innovation capacity and decoupling may be affected by endogeneity. Innovation capacity may influence decoupling, but countries with stronger environmental performance may also invest more in Innovation. Institutional quality may also be endogenous because governance reforms and environmental performance can influence each other over time. In addition, decoupling outcomes may exhibit persistence. To address these concerns, the study employs a two-step System Generalised Method of Moments estimator. The dynamic model is specified in Eq. ( 8 ): $$\begin{array}{cccc}& {D}_{it}=\rho {D}_{i,t-1}+{\beta}_{1}Inno{v}_{it}+{\beta}_{2}Ins{t}_{it}+\gamma {X}_{it}+{\eta}_{i}+{\nu}_{it}& & \end{array}$$ (8) In Eq. ( 8 ), \({D}_{i,t-1}\) is the lagged dependent variable, \(\rho\) captures persistence in decoupling outcomes, \({\eta}_{i}\) captures unobserved country-specific effects, and \({\nu}_{it}\) is the idiosyncratic error term. The inclusion of the lagged dependent variable allows the model to account for possible persistence in decoupling performance. System GMM estimates Eq. ( 8 ) using equations in both first differences and levels. In the differenced equation, lagged levels of endogenous variables are used as instruments. In the level equation, lagged differences are used as instruments. This approach is useful for panels with many countries and relatively short time periods, where persistence and endogeneity may bias standard fixed-effects estimates. The first-differenced form of Eq. ( 8 ) is presented in Eq. ( 9 ): $$\begin{array}{cccc}&\Delta {D}_{it}=\rho\Delta {D}_{i,t-1}+{\beta}_{1}\Delta Inno{v}_{it}+{\beta}_{2}\Delta Ins{t}_{it}+\gamma\Delta {X}_{it}+\Delta {\nu}_{it}& & \end{array}$$ (9) Equation ( 9 ) removes unobserved time-invariant country-specific effects by differencing the data. Lagged values of the regressors are then used as instruments to address potential endogeneity. To reduce the risk of instrument proliferation, the GMM instruments are restricted to lag depths of 2 to 3 and are collapsed. Instrument counts are reported and compared with the number of countries. Keeping the number of instruments below the number of cross-sectional units helps reduce overfitting of endogenous variables and improves the credibility of the Hansen test. Instrument validity is assessed using the Hansen J-test of overidentifying restrictions. The absence of second-order serial correlation is assessed using the Arellano–Bond AR(2) test. The AR(1) test is also reported, although first-order serial correlation may arise mechanically in differenced errors. Therefore, the AR(2) test is the more important diagnostic for evaluating the validity of the moment conditions. Hansen test p-values are interpreted cautiously. They should not reject instrument validity, but implausibly high values may also indicate instrument proliferation. 3.6 Method of moments quantile regression The study further applies Method of Moments Quantile Regression to examine whether the effects of Innovation and institutional quality differ across the decoupling distribution. Mean-based estimators such as fixed effects and System GMM estimate the average relationship between Innovation and decoupling, but they may hide important differences between low-decoupling, median-decoupling, and high-decoupling countries. The quantile model is specified in Eq. ( 10 ): $$\begin{array}{cccc}& {Q}_{\tau }({D}_{it}\mid {Z}_{it})={Z}_{it}^{^{\prime}}{\beta}_{\tau }& & \end{array}$$ (10) In Eq. ( 10 ), \({Q}_{\tau }({D}_{it}\mid {Z}_{it})\) denotes the conditional quantile of the decoupling index at quantile \(\tau\) , while \({Z}_{it}\) includes Innovation capacity, institutional quality, and the control variables. The coefficient vector \({\beta}_{\tau }\) represents the quantile-specific effects of the explanatory variables. Estimates are reported at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. The 10th percentile captures countries with weak decoupling performance, the 50th percentile captures median decoupling performance, and the 90th percentile captures relatively strong decoupling performance. This allows the analysis to assess whether Innovation and institutional quality have stronger, weaker, or more asymmetric effects across different decoupling regimes. The Method of Moments Quantile Regression approach is preferred because it allows for country fixed effects while estimating heterogeneous slope coefficients across the conditional distribution. This is important because pooled quantile regression may confound country-specific structural differences with genuine distributional heterogeneity. 3.7 Estimation procedure and diagnostic tests All estimations are conducted using Stata. The empirical analysis proceeds in four stages. First, descriptive statistics are used to summarise the distribution of the main variables and assess variation across the sample. Correlation analysis and variance inflation factors are used to examine potential multicollinearity among the regressors. Second, pooled OLS and fixed-effects models are estimated to compare cross-sectional and within-country relationships. This comparison is important because pooled estimates may reflect structural differences between countries, while fixed-effects estimates focus on changes within countries over time. Third, System GMM is estimated using the dynamic specification in Eq. ( 8 ) and the differenced form in Eq. ( 9 ). The validity of the dynamic models is assessed using the Hansen J-test, Arellano–Bond serial correlation tests, instrument counts, and instrument-to-country ratios. The interpretation of System GMM results is kept cautious, especially where key variables are statistically insignificant. Fourth, Method of Moments Quantile Regression is estimated using Eq. ( 10 ) to examine whether the effects of Innovation, institutions, and control variables differ across the decoupling distribution. This distribution-sensitive analysis complements the fixed-effects and dynamic estimates by showing whether the drivers of decoupling vary across low-, median-, and high-decoupling countries. These estimation strategies allow the study to examine the Innovation-decoupling relationship from three complementary angles: within-country variation, dynamic endogeneity, and distributional heterogeneity. This strengthens the empirical design and provides a more cautious basis for interpreting whether national Innovation capacity supports growth-emissions decoupling in Sub-Saharan Africa. 4 Results and findings 4.1 Descriptive statistics Table 1 reports summary statistics for the variables used in the empirical analysis. Overall, the data display substantial cross-country and temporal variation, which is a necessary condition for identifying the relationships of interest. The decoupling index has a mean of 0.102, indicating that, on average, countries in the sample exhibit weak relative decoupling between economic growth and CO₂ emissions over the period 2005–2024. However, the wide dispersion, with values ranging from − 6.063 to 4.488 and a standard deviation of 1.379, suggests pronounced heterogeneity in decoupling performance. This variation reflects the coexistence of countries experiencing strong coupling, relative decoupling, and, in a few cases, absolute decoupling. The breadth of this distribution supports the use of both mean-based and distribution-sensitive estimators later in the analysis. The large range, from deep contractions to rapid expansions, highlights the presence of macroeconomic volatility, reinforcing the need to control for growth dynamics and persistence in the decoupling process. The control variables also show considerable variation. Domestic credit to the private sector averages 21.2 percent of GDP but spans from very low levels to over 140 percent, capturing differences in financial depth and access. The globalization index displays moderate dispersion, reflecting differing degrees of trade, financial, and social integration. Public education spending averages 3.85 percent of GDP, with substantial cross-country differences, suggesting heterogeneity in human capital investment that may interact with Innovation capacity. Together, the descriptive statistics indicate that the dataset contains sufficient variability in decoupling outcomes, Innovation capacity, institutional quality, and macro-financial conditions to meaningfully examine nonlinear, dynamic, and heterogeneous effects. Importantly, the wide dispersion in the decoupling index and Innovation measures provides a strong empirical basis for the subsequent fixed-effects, System GMM, and quantile regression analyses. 4.2 Baseline OLS and fixed-effects results The results in Table 2 presents the baseline estimates examining the relationship between Innovation capacity, institutional quality, and growth–emissions decoupling. Unlike pooled quantile regression, this approach incorporates country fixed effects, allowing for distributional heterogeneity in the slope coefficients while controlling for unobserved time-invariant heterogeneity across countries. This is a critical methodological refinement: as our baseline OLS-versus-FE comparison demonstrates (Sect. 4.2 ), pooled estimators in this setting confound within-country dynamics with cross-country structural differences. The same logic extends to quantile estimation, motivating our use of MM-QR rather than pooled quantile regression. The quantile estimates show that institutional quality is positively associated with decoupling across the conditional distribution, although the magnitude declines slightly from the lower to the upper quantile. This suggests that governance improvements support decoupling across regimes, but their marginal contribution may be somewhat stronger in low-decoupling contexts where institutional weaknesses are more binding. The results reveal a striking degree of consistency across the decoupling distribution. Innovation capacity enters with a positive and statistically significant coefficient at all three quantiles, with point estimates of 0.0091 at the 10th percentile, 0.0088 at the median, and 0.0084 at the 90th percentile. The coefficients decline only slightly across the quantiles, suggesting mild attenuation rather than strong distributional asymmetry. This pattern should not be interpreted as strong evidence of nonlinearity, since the squared Innovation term in the dynamic specification is statistically insignificant. Institutional quality exhibits a similar pattern, with coefficients of 0.085, 0.081, and 0.077 at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles respectively, all statistically significant at the 1% level. This pattern indicates that improvements in governance enhance decoupling outcomes uniformly across the distribution, with no evidence that institutions become less effective in high-decoupling regimes. The consistency of these results across the distribution, and their alignment with the fixed-effects baseline, lends considerable robustness to our central finding that Innovation and institutions are complementary positive drivers of growth–emissions decoupling in Sub-Saharan Africa. GDP growth retains a stable positive effect across all quantiles (coefficients of approximately 0.217–0.218), confirming that growth dynamics; independent of the level effects partialled out in the construction of the decoupling index; exert a uniform influence on decoupling outcomes. Importantly, however, the globalization coefficient is negative throughout and becomes progressively more negative at higher quantiles (− 0.018 at the 10th, − 0.027 at the median, − 0.036 at the 90th). This is a substantively important result: greater global integration appears to weaken decoupling, and this effect intensifies precisely in countries that have already achieved relatively strong decoupling. The finding is consistent with carbon leakage and pollution-haven dynamics in resource-exporting African economies, where deeper integration into global supply chains coincides with the expansion of carbon-intensive extractive activities. Credit to the private sector shows a similar; though smaller; negative pattern, reinforcing the case that financial deepening, in the absence of green-finance alignment, may channel investment toward carbon-intensive sectors. Taken together, the quantile estimates support a revised interpretation of H5. We initially hypothesized that Innovation effects would differ markedly across the decoupling distribution. The evidence does not support strong distributional asymmetry in the Innovation–decoupling relationship; rather, the asymmetry is most pronounced in the globalization–decoupling relationship, where the carbon penalty of integration intensifies in high-decoupling contexts. This is itself a substantively important contribution, suggesting that for Sub-Saharan African countries approaching meaningful decoupling, managing the emissions content of external integration may matter more than further marginal increases in Innovation capacity. 4.4.2 Temporal heterogeneity in the innovation–decoupling relationship A natural question is whether the relationship between Innovation and decoupling has been stable across the sample period or has evolved over time. We address this question with two complementary specifications reported in Table 5 . Column 1 examines whether human development conditions the Innovation–decoupling relationship by interacting Innovation with the Human Development Index (HDI). Column 2 tests for temporal heterogeneity using a continuous Innovation × time-trend interaction, where time is centered at 2014.5 (the sample midpoint). We emphasize an important departure from the originally submitted version of this paper. The original specification used a discrete post-2015 step dummy interpreted as an indicator of the Paris Agreement. As correctly noted by an anonymous reviewer, such a dummy in a panel fixed-effects model absorbs every time-varying common shock that occurs from 2016 onward; including the 2014–2016 commodity-price collapse, the COVID-19 pandemic, post-pandemic energy-price volatility, the rapid decline in renewable-energy technology costs, and broader shifts in trade and climate finance; and therefore cannot identify the causal effect of any single policy event. Crucially, our breakpoint sensitivity analysis (Appendix Table 14) further demonstrates that the Innovation × post-period interaction is statistically significant at multiple breakpoints, with the strongest effects at 2010 (p = 0.003) and 2012 (p = 0.025); well before the Paris Agreement. This pattern is decisively inconsistent with attributing the temporal heterogeneity to a discrete policy event and is much more consistent with a smooth, secular shift in the Innovation–decoupling relationship. We therefore adopt the continuous time-trend specification as the primary test of temporal heterogeneity. The results in Column 2 confirm a substantively important temporal pattern. First, the baseline decoupling outcome has been gradually deteriorating across the sample period: the coefficient on the centered time trend is − 0.024 (p = 0.002), indicating that, holding Innovation, institutional quality, and macro-financial conditions constant, decoupling outcomes in Sub-Saharan Africa have weakened by approximately 0.024 units per year. This is consistent with the well-documented stylized fact that emissions growth in the region has outpaced output growth in recent years, driven by rapid urbanization, energy demand expansion, and the carbon intensification of industrial activity. Second; and substantively more important for the central argument of this paper; the marginal effect of Innovation on decoupling has been gradually strengthening over time, with an interaction coefficient of 0.0007 per year (p = 0.037). Cumulated over the 20-year sample, this implies that an additional unit of Innovation capacity in 2024 contributed approximately 0.014 more to decoupling than the same unit of Innovation capacity in 2005; a magnitude comparable to the entire static fixed-effects baseline effect of Innovation reported in Table 2 . Three implications follow. First, the cumulative deterioration of baseline decoupling (a strong negative trend) is being partially offset by a slow strengthening of the Innovation–decoupling channel; Innovation matters more, per unit, now than it did at the start of the sample period. Second, this temporal pattern is smooth rather than discrete, and as the breakpoint analysis in Appendix Table 14 confirms, it cannot be attributed to any specific policy event. Plausible drivers of this secular shift; none of which we can separately identify within the current research design; include the rapid decline in renewable-energy technology costs (solar LCOE fell by approximately 70% over 2010–2024), increased availability of climate finance through bilateral and multilateral channels, and the diffusion of energy-efficient capital goods into African import markets at substantially lower prices than in the early sample period. Third, Column 1 provides no evidence that human development conditions the Innovation–decoupling relationship; the Innovation × HDI interaction is positive but small and statistically insignificant. The methodological implication is straightforward: reduced-form panel models can detect temporal heterogeneity in economic relationships but cannot, by themselves, attribute this heterogeneity to specific policy events. Causal identification of climate-policy effects on decoupling outcomes in low- and lower-middle-income contexts requires identification strategies; such as staggered-adoption difference-in-differences, NDC stringency variation, or synthetic control methods applied to leading reformers; that are beyond the scope of the present study but that we identify as a high-priority direction for future research. The figure should be interpreted as descriptive temporal heterogeneity rather than evidence of a discrete post-2015 policy effect. The results suggest that the Innovation–decoupling relationship has gradually strengthened over the sample period, although the model does not identify the specific policy, technological, or macroeconomic forces responsible for this change. Under a simple mediation interpretation, innovation would first upgrade governance, and the resulting institutional gains would in turn translate into stronger decoupling outcomes. Tables 6 and 7 provide an exploratory two-step assessment of this possibility within a reduced-form panel framework. Within-country variation in innovation does not systematically predict within-country variation in institutional quality over the 2005–2024 window. This is consistent with a long-standing claim in the institutional economics literature that governance quality is slow-moving and shaped by deep historical, political, and structural factors that are largely orthogonal to short- to medium-run changes in measured innovation activity [ 1 , 18 , 23 ]. The first leg of the candidate mediation pathway is therefore not supported by the data. Table 7 returns to the decoupling outcome but now includes innovation capacity and institutional quality jointly. Under strict mediation logic, conditioning on the candidate mediator should attenuate the coefficient on innovation. The opposite is observed: innovation capacity retains a positive and highly significant coefficient (0.008, p < 0.01), institutional quality remains independently positive and weakly significant (0.080, p < 0.10), and the negative globalization effect is preserved (− 0.034, p < 0.01). The two channels do not displace one another. Taken together, the two panels reject the simple mediation account. Innovation does not significantly upgrade institutions (Table 6 ), and conditioning on institutions does not attenuate the innovation–decoupling association (Table 7 ). The evidence instead supports a complementarity interpretation, in which innovation capacity and institutional quality operate as parallel and partially independent drivers of growth–emissions decoupling. Innovation appears to reduce carbon intensity through direct production-side channels; energy efficiency gains, cleaner technology diffusion, and process upgrading; while institutions support decoupling through distinct channels of regulatory enforcement, policy credibility, and long-horizon investment certainty. Neither pathway requires the other to be active, but both can reasonably be expected to compound where they coexist. Two caveats are warranted. First, the test is reduced-form and does not licence strong causal mediation claims; a properly identified mediation analysis would require an instrument for innovation that is plausibly orthogonal to the institutional environment, which is beyond the scope of the present dataset. Second, the null in Table 6 only informs the within-country dynamics observable over 2005–2024; it is silent on the slower historical co-evolution of innovation systems and governance institutions, which may well be linked over horizons longer than this sample can speak to. 4.4.4 Integration of findings The extended results yield three main insights. First, Innovation capacity is positively associated with growth–emissions decoupling in the fixed-effects and quantile regression models, but this relationship is not statistically significant in the dynamic System GMM estimates. This suggests that Innovation may operate more through gradual structural channels than through short-run dynamic adjustment. Second, institutional quality shows a positive and fairly stable relationship with decoupling in the fixed-effects and quantile specifications, although the dynamic model does not detect a significant short-run institutional effect or a significant Innovation–institution interaction. Third, globalization consistently weakens decoupling in the quantile estimates, with the negative effect becoming stronger at higher decoupling levels. Overall, the evidence supports a cautious interpretation: Innovation and institutions matter for decoupling, but their effects are conditional, gradual, and shaped by broader macro-financial and external integration patterns. 4.5 Discussion of results and findings This study set out to examine whether national Innovation capacity accelerates the decoupling of economic growth from CO₂ emissions, and how institutional quality and policy context condition this relationship. The results provide a nuanced and, in several respects, corrective perspective on Innovation-led decarbonization. Rather than supporting a simple Innovation-optimist interpretation, the findings show that Innovation capacity is positively associated with growth–emissions decoupling in the fixed-effects and quantile specifications, while the dynamic System GMM estimates do not detect a statistically significant short-run Innovation effect. This suggests that Innovation may matter for decoupling, but its effects are more likely to operate through gradual structural channels than through immediate dynamic adjustment. 4.6 Innovation, decoupling, and the limits of EKC-style reasoning The baseline fixed-effects results provide initial support for H1, showing that within countries, increases in Innovation capacity are associated with improved growth–emissions decoupling once time-invariant structural differences are controlled for. This contrasts sharply with the pooled OLS results, where Innovation appears negatively related to decoupling. The divergence between pooled and fixed-effects estimates is substantive and reinforces long-standing critiques of cross-sectional EKC analyses. As emphasized by Polat and Çil [ 21 ] and Taşdemir [ 25 ], income and Innovation effects estimated across countries tend to conflate development stage, industrial structure, and energy systems with genuine efficiency improvements. By isolating within-country variation, the fixed-effects results suggest that Innovation can reduce carbon intensity, but only relative to a country’s own structural baseline. However, the dynamic System GMM results qualify the fixed-effects evidence. Once persistence and potential endogeneity are accounted for, Innovation capacity is no longer statistically significant. The squared Innovation term is also negative but statistically insignificant, meaning that the dynamic model does not provide robust evidence of an inverted-U or threshold effect. The implication is not that Innovation is unimportant, but that its contribution to decoupling is not strongly detected as a short-run dynamic effect. This supports a more cautious interpretation in which Innovation influences decoupling through slower structural and institutional channels. These results remain consistent with studies that caution against assuming automatic environmental gains from Innovation. However, the present evidence is more conservative. It shows a positive Innovation–decoupling association in the fixed-effects and quantile models, but no statistically significant nonlinear effect in the dynamic specification. The main implication is that Innovation is relevant for decoupling, but its environmental returns should not be assumed to be immediate, linear, or sufficient on their own. 4.7 Institutional quality as a conditional, not automatic, enabler The role of institutions is more stable in the fixed-effects and quantile results than in the dynamic specification. In the fixed-effects models, institutional quality is positively associated with decoupling, suggesting that improvements in governance within countries support environmental efficiency. The quantile results reinforce this interpretation, showing positive and statistically significant institutional effects across the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. However, the System GMM estimates do not detect a statistically significant short-run institutional effect. This suggests that institutional quality may matter more as a structural enabling condition than as an immediate dynamic driver of decoupling. This apparent paradox is best understood through an institutional–structural lens. Stronger institutions often coexist with deeper integration into global value chains, more complex industrial systems, and higher consumption intensity, all of which can temporarily raise emissions relative to output. As argued by Longhofer and Jorgenson [ 18 ] and Sah [ 23 ], institutional quality shapes long-term environmental capacity but does not guarantee immediate emissions efficiency gains. The exploratory transmission-channel results do not support a simple mediation interpretation in which Innovation improves institutional quality, which then improves decoupling. Instead, Innovation and institutions appear to operate as parallel and complementary drivers in the fixed-effects framework. The weak and statistically insignificant Innovation–institution interaction in the dynamic model further suggests that institutional quality conditions Innovation outcomes over longer horizons than those captured by short-term within-country variation. This helps explain why prior studies focusing on emissions levels often find strong institutional effects, while decoupling outcomes appear more sensitive to structural and policy alignment. 4.8 Heterogeneity across decoupling regimes One of the paper’s important contributions lies in showing that the Innovation–decoupling relationship is not confined to the average country. The MM-QR results show that Innovation capacity is positively associated with decoupling at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. The magnitude declines only slightly across the distribution, suggesting mild attenuation rather than strong asymmetry. This means that Innovation capacity supports decoupling across low-, median-, and high-decoupling regimes, although the effect is modest in size. The stronger distributional asymmetry appears in globalization, whose negative effect becomes larger at higher decoupling levels. Thus, the main heterogeneity finding is not that Innovation becomes harmful at higher decoupling levels, but that external integration imposes a larger carbon penalty in countries with stronger decoupling performance. This finding extends the work of Iqbal et al. [ 13 ] and Aquilas et al. [ 6 ] by demonstrating that Innovation’s environmental role depends not only on technological readiness but also on a country’s position within the decoupling spectrum. It also reinforces recent calls to move beyond mean-based estimators when assessing climate–growth relationships. Innovation policies that are effective in early or intermediate decoupling regimes may be ineffective or counterproductive in more advanced contexts. 4.9 Temporal change in the innovation–decoupling relationship The temporal heterogeneity results suggest that the association between Innovation capacity and decoupling has gradually strengthened over time. This should not be interpreted as evidence of a discrete post-2015 or Paris Agreement effect. Rather, the continuous Innovation × year interaction indicates a smooth temporal shift in the Innovation–decoupling relationship. This pattern may reflect broader changes in technology costs, climate finance, policy awareness, and diffusion of cleaner production systems, but the current model does not identify any single causal mechanism. The evidence therefore supports a cautious claim of temporal heterogeneity, not a causal policy-regime effect. 4.10 Synthesis The results caution against simplistic innovation-led decoupling narratives. Innovation capacity is positively associated with decoupling in the fixed-effects and quantile models, but this relationship is not statistically significant in the dynamic System GMM specification. Institutional quality also supports decoupling in the fixed-effects and quantile results, although its dynamic effect and the innovation-by-institution interaction are not statistically significant. The clearest distributional asymmetry concerns globalization, whose negative association with decoupling becomes stronger at higher levels of decoupling. Overall, innovation and institutions appear to be gradual, context-dependent correlates of decoupling rather than immediate or self-sufficient drivers of emissions reduction. 5 Conclusion This study examined whether national Innovation capacity accelerates the decoupling of economic growth from carbon emissions and how institutional quality and policy context shape this relationship. First, Innovation capacity is positively associated with growth–emissions decoupling in the fixed-effects and quantile specifications. However, the System GMM results do not detect a statistically significant short-run Innovation effect after accounting for persistence and potential endogeneity. This suggests that Innovation may support decoupling through gradual structural channels rather than through immediate dynamic adjustment. Second, institutional quality is positively associated with decoupling in the fixed-effects and quantile models, suggesting that governance capacity supports environmental efficiency across different decoupling regimes. However, the System GMM results do not show a statistically significant short-run institutional effect, and the Innovation–institution interaction is also insignificant. This indicates that institutions may operate more as structural enablers than as immediate dynamic moderators of Innovation. Third, the distributional analysis shows that Innovation capacity and institutional quality are positively associated with decoupling across low-, median-, and high-decoupling regimes. The coefficients decline only slightly across the distribution. By contrast, globalization shows a stronger negative effect at higher decoupling levels, suggesting that external integration may undermine decoupling where countries are otherwise performing relatively better. Overall, the evidence provides partial support for H1 and H3. Innovation capacity and institutional quality are positively associated with decoupling in the fixed-effects and MM-QR estimates, but neither relationship is statistically detected in the short-run System GMM specification. This difference may reflect several factors, including the distinction between within-country associations and short-run dynamic relationships, estimator sensitivity, and the limits of the available time series. It should not be reduced to a single explanation. H2 is not supported because the squared innovation term is statistically insignificant. H4 is not supported because the innovation-by-institution interaction is statistically insignificant. H5 receives qualified support: the clearest distributional heterogeneity concerns globalization, whose negative association with decoupling strengthens at higher points of the conditional distribution. The findings have several limitations. First, the fixed-effects and MM-QR estimates describe conditional associations, while System GMM tests short-run dynamics under stronger identification assumptions; their difference should neither be treated as proof of causality nor framed as a simple refutation of one estimator by another. Second, because the outcome is a generated residual-based index, the GDP-growth coefficient may partly reflect its mechanical connection to the construction of the dependent variable. It is therefore retained only as a short-run control and is not interpreted as a substantive causal effect of growth on decoupling. Third, the sample was exposed to cross-sectionally correlated shocks, including the 2014–2016 commodity-price collapse, COVID-19, and post-pandemic energy-price volatility. Cross-sectional-dependence diagnostics and Driscoll–Kraay inference are therefore given particular weight in the fixed-effects analysis. Finally, the results are most directly applicable to the 47 Sub-Saharan African countries observed during 2005–2024. Different energy structures, innovation systems, and institutional settings may limit generalisation to other regions. Future research should test alternative innovation measures, including R&D intensity and patent applications, use longer panels and cleaner-technology indicators, and apply research designs that can better identify causal pathways. 6 Policy recommendations The findings point to the need for a more careful approach to Innovation policy within climate strategy. Innovation capacity is associated with stronger growth–emissions decoupling in the fixed-effects and quantile models, but the dynamic results suggest that this effect is not automatic or immediate. Policymakers should therefore avoid treating Innovation as a self-sufficient solution to emissions reduction. The central policy task is not simply to expand Innovation activity, but to ensure that Innovation is directed toward measurable improvements in emissions efficiency. A first priority is to strengthen the absorptive capacity of economies, especially in countries where Innovation systems remain weak. This requires investment in human capital, technical skills, energy infrastructure, firm-level capabilities, and standards that support the adoption of cleaner technologies. In many Sub-Saharan African economies, the main challenge is not only producing new technologies, but adapting, diffusing, and applying existing technologies in ways that reduce carbon intensity. Policies should therefore support technology diffusion, demonstration projects, technical assistance, and learning-by-doing initiatives that enable firms and public institutions to use Innovation for environmental efficiency. Second, Innovation policy should be tied more directly to low-carbon outcomes. Public support for Innovation should move beyond broad R&D promotion and focus on areas where emissions intensity remains high. This includes energy efficiency, cleaner production processes, low-carbon infrastructure, renewable energy deployment, and technology upgrading in carbon-intensive sectors. Instruments such as green public procurement, performance-based subsidies, energy-efficiency standards, and targeted support for low-carbon production can help ensure that Innovation contributes to decoupling rather than merely expanding output. Third, financial systems should be better aligned with climate objectives. The negative association between domestic credit and decoupling suggests that financial deepening may weaken environmental efficiency if credit flows into carbon-intensive activities. Governments and financial regulators should therefore strengthen green finance frameworks, including climate-related lending guidelines, green taxonomies, disclosure requirements, and incentives for banks to finance low-carbon investment. The objective should be to shift financial intermediation away from emissions-intensive expansion and toward investments that support productivity with lower carbon intensity. Fourth, institutional quality remains an important but insufficient condition for Innovation-led decoupling. Stronger institutions can support environmental outcomes through better regulatory enforcement, policy coordination, credibility, and long-term planning. However, the results also suggest that institutional effects may operate gradually rather than immediately. Institutional reforms should therefore proceed alongside Innovation policies, with emphasis on regulatory capacity, coordination across climate, industrial, finance, and Innovation agencies, and consistent implementation of environmental standards. These reforms should be evaluated over longer time horizons rather than judged only by short-term changes in emissions outcomes. Fifth, governments should build credible and stable climate-policy frameworks that allow Innovation to translate into decoupling over time. The temporal results suggest that the Innovation–decoupling relationship has gradually strengthened across the sample period, but this should not be interpreted as the causal effect of any single policy event. The implication is that countries need predictable domestic policy environments, including long-term emissions targets, stable regulatory signals, and coordinated climate and industrial strategies. Policy continuity matters because firms and investors are more likely to adopt cleaner technologies when the direction of policy is clear. Finally, the negative relationship between globalization and decoupling highlights the need to manage the carbon consequences of external integration. Trade and investment policies should not be treated as separate from climate policy. Policymakers should complement domestic Innovation strategies with measures that encourage low-carbon production and reduce emissions outsourcing. These may include supply-chain standards, emissions disclosure rules, climate-related provisions in trade and investment agreements, and support for cleaner production among export-oriented firms. Since the quantile results show that globalization has stronger negative effects at higher decoupling levels, countries should use distribution-sensitive evaluation tools to monitor how trade, finance, Innovation, and climate policies interact across different stages of decoupling.
