4. Discussion

The following sections examine approaches to balancing conservation and recreational use, responses to recreational use, conservation activities, and stakeholder involvement in turn. These findings are considered alongside the SEM results to identify management issues and the characteristics of management practice.

4.1 Experiential and educational approaches to balancing conservation and recreational use, and issues surrounding nonimplementation

The widespread implementation of experiential and educational activities indicates that communicating conservation objectives to park users is employed as a means of balancing conservation and recreational use. By contrast, measures such as spatial separation and buffering adjust the spatial contact between conservation targets and recreational activities. Decisions about introducing these measures therefore need to consider the locations of conservation targets and recreational activities, as well as the types of use involved.

Distinguishing the categories of nonimplementation allows two issues to be considered separately: deciding whether an approach is appropriate and establishing the conditions needed to implement an approach considered necessary. For responses classified as "not implemented, with no policy," relevant issues include the information needed to assess appropriateness and the stakeholders responsible for that judgment. For responses classified as "necessary but not implemented," the issue is to examine implementation conditions such as staffing, expertise, and funding. Experiential conservation events require planning and organization as well as knowledge of the organisms involved. Considering how to secure expert advice and people to undertake these activities can therefore help clarify the gap between recognizing a need and putting it into practice.

4.2 Accommodating recreational use and responding to environmental impacts

Responding to park users involves both ensuring safety and everyday convenience and determining the extent to which particular activities are accommodated. The distribution of Q4 responses can be understood as management that adjusts how facilities and activities are accommodated, rather than simply expanding or restricting all park use. Deciding which activities to accommodate while ensuring safety and providing information may characterize how parks undertaking nature restoration respond to users.

When interpreting the absence of trash bins, it is necessary to distinguish a policy requiring users to take their trash home from implementation conditions such as disposal costs and collection arrangements. The appropriate response differs depending on whether a report of nonimplementation reflects a management-policy decision or constraints on implementation. Considering the role assigned to a facility or activity within each park, alongside the number of items implemented, allows a more concrete examination of how recreational use is accommodated.

Responses to impacts after recreational use has been accommodated are another important basis for comparing management practices. Environmental impacts may vary with the type and intensity of use and with environmental conditions (Monz et al., 2013; Larson et al., 2016). The forms of recreational use termed "high-load" in this study therefore also need to be considered in their specific contexts. Interpreting differences in Q4 scores in terms of both the facilities and activities accommodated and responses to their impacts provides a way to examine adjustments that cannot be captured simply by whether use is permitted.

4.3 Implementing conservation activities and maintaining policies and standards

Support for conservation activities needs to address both the introduction of activities not yet implemented and the continuation of ongoing activities. The frequent identification of widely implemented Q2 activities as management issues indicates the value of examining the conditions for their continuation and review after introduction.

For planting based on native species, a management issue is not only carrying out planting but also maintaining criteria for plant selection when plantings are renewed. To consider the difference in implementation rates between this and other Q2 activities, it is necessary to examine both the conditions for securing planting stock and labor and the arrangements through which conservation policies are reflected in day-to-day decisions.

4.4 Stakeholder roles in connecting activities

The stakeholder-specific activity profiles in Q3 provide a starting point for examining management arrangements in terms of surveys and deliberation, fieldwork, and communication with users. To use findings from environmental surveys in management decisions and reflect those decisions in fieldwork and communication, it is necessary to identify who is responsible for linking these different tasks.

Differences in stakeholder involvement between visitor information and displays and environmental education also offer a basis for considering how routine information provision connects with activities conveying specialist knowledge. Research on participation and collaboration emphasizes decision-making processes and relationships among stakeholders (Reed, 2008; Bodin, 2017). Using the stakeholder-specific activity profiles identified here as a starting point, examining whose knowledge informs which decisions and who links those decisions to fieldwork and users can help clarify management arrangements in individual parks.

4.5 Associations of management arrangements and greenness with the three management behaviors

The shared positive associations of social norms with both environment-focused behavior and balance-oriented behavior offer a perspective on stakeholder involvement in conservation activities as a foundation for efforts to balance conservation and recreational use. In the measurement model, relatively large loadings were observed for involvement by public authorities and designated managers and by citizens and experts. How public management arrangements can be combined with citizen and expert participation is therefore an organizational issue relevant to both management behaviors.

The negative association between environmental quality and environment-focused behavior (−0.51) needs to be interpreted in relation to conservation targets and management objectives. The indicators constituting environment-focused behavior include recognition of management issues and funding conditions, as well as activity implementation. Some of these activities, including habitat management, the protection and translocation of rare species, and the removal of non-native species, involve interventions to maintain or restore the environment. Given the nature of these activities, conservation efforts may be directed toward places requiring recovery or adjustment. Together with the positive association with balance-oriented behavior (0.42), the results suggest the value of distinguishing interventions aimed at maintenance and recovery from coordination with recreational use when examining the relationship between greenness and management behavior. Relating NDVI-derived greenness to actual conservation targets and park-specific management practices is needed to develop this interpretation.

The coefficient from social norms to user-focused behavior was negative (−0.18), and the proportion of variance explained in this behavior was also low. Meanwhile, the direct path from user attitudes to basic amenity and safety provision was negative (−0.45). These results concern different analytical levels: a latent variable representing user-focused behavior and an observed indicator related to safety and convenience. Because the latter score combines facility and activity implementation with responses to environmental impacts, examining implementation and impact responses item by item is useful when relating user reactions to management practice.

A more concrete understanding of user-focused behavior also requires attention to proximity to residential areas, park histories, operational decisions by designated managers, and recreational demand. Examining the conditions governing the provision of user services, in addition to the arrangements supporting conservation activities, is a task for future research.

4.6 Integrated discussion: an overall picture of balancing conservation and recreational use and its challenges

This section combines the four sets of descriptive results from Q2–Q5 with the SEM results to examine management practices underlying the coordination of conservation and recreational use and the issues associated with their implementation. The five cases examined in the previous study and publicly available information are used as supplementary sources for considering differences in management conditions.

4.6.1 Components of balancing conservation and recreational use across the four sets of descriptive results

Taken together, the four questions suggest that the characteristics of balancing conservation and recreational use lie in the combination of communicating conservation objectives to users and adjusting the locations and forms of use. Visitor information, displays, and environmental education in Q2, together with awareness-raising and park etiquette promotion and experiential conservation events in Q5, serve the former role. Relating these measures to the accommodation of recreational use and responses to impacts in Q4 and spatial separation in Q5 makes it possible to identify more concretely what is conserved and which uses are accommodated, and how.

Q5 asks about both the provision of nature observation and conservation experience opportunities and adjustments to recreational locations. When comparing approaches to balancing conservation and recreational use, it is useful to consider the content and combination of these practices, in addition to the number of items implemented.

Stakeholder-specific involvement in Q3 and the shared associations with social norms in the SEM provide a starting point for examining the arrangements supporting these combinations. The value of considering the four questions alongside SEM lies in relating the ways conservation and recreational use are connected to the arrangements supporting their implementation.

4.6.2 Issues shared across parks and differences between parks

Considering nonimplementation and recognition of management issues together indicates that management support needs to address not only the content of activities but also the roles involved in assessing and sharing policies and the conditions supporting implementation. The question of who is responsible for native-species planting criteria or policies on spatial separation connects with the Q3 perspective of involvement in surveys, decision-making, and practical work. Expertise, staffing, and funding can also be understood as conditions enabling these roles to be fulfilled in practice.

The way these issues arise differs with location and management context. The five cases in the previous study illustrate differences in management related to location and management stakeholders, including discussions with neighboring residents at Sayama Hill Flora and Fauna Interaction Centre, wetland maintenance and responses to non-native species at Kitamoto Nature Observation Park, and citizen-led operation at Kamogawa Mizubenosato Park (Chen and Fukahori, 2026). Consultation with residents, specialist responses to conservation targets, and sharing policies through citizen activities require different roles. In citizen-led operation, passing on conservation policies to support the continuation of activities is also an issue when considering management arrangements.

Responses to management issues shared across parks therefore also need to be tailored to conservation targets, surrounding land use, and the ways stakeholders are involved. The shared associations with management arrangements and the differing associations with environmental conditions in the SEM also provide a starting point for considering organizational and site conditions together. When drawing on practices from other parks, it is important to consider not only whether activities share the same name but also whether their objectives, decision-making stakeholders, and implementation conditions are relevant to the park concerned.

4.7 Six types for comparing management orientations

The aggregated Q2–Q5 results are organized into six types using four perspectives: (1) management orientation—Environmental Focus, Balanced, or User Focus; (2) the nature of conservation activities; (3) the approach to balancing conservation and recreational use; and (4) the way recreational use is accommodated. These types are an interpretive, descriptive synthesis of the questionnaire and supplementary sources, used to compare the main management characteristics of parks.

4.7.1 Environmental Focus: nature restoration and conservation types

The nature restoration type is management whose primary objective is the recovery of degraded habitats or the creation of new natural environments. Activities such as habitat management and non-native species removal are treated as interventions aimed at restoring nature.

The conservation type is management whose primary objective is the continued maintenance and protection of existing natural environments and species. Activities such as protecting wild plants and animals and conducting environmental surveys are positioned as efforts to safeguard conservation targets over time.

The distinction lies in whether recovery or maintenance is the primary objective. Because habitat management and non-native species removal can serve either objective, it is important to relate the name of an activity to its purpose in each park.

4.7.2 Balanced: experience-and-education, spatial separation, and integrated types

The experience-and-education type seeks users' understanding and cooperation through experiential events, environmental education, and information provision. Its defining feature is the use of conservation activities as a point of contact with users, providing opportunities to engage with nature while communicating the objectives and content of those activities.

The spatial separation type separates places for conservation from places for recreational use so that the two can coexist. The spatial relationship between conservation targets and recreational use is adjusted through the arrangement of use areas, buffers, and similar measures.

The integrated type combines experience and education with spatial separation. It focuses on how educational engagement with users and adjustments to recreational locations are combined within the same park.

4.7.3 User Focus: selective-use type

The selective-use type ensures safety and everyday convenience while adjusting the range of activities accommodated according to their content. It considers measures for safety, information provision, and accessible paths alongside measures regulating the accommodation of eating and drinking, water-based activities, collecting organisms, and other uses.

This type organizes responses to users in terms of differences in what is accommodated and under which conditions. Linking implementation status to the objectives of an activity in each park and responses to its environmental impacts allows a more concrete comparison of the content of user-focused behavior.

4.7.4 Relationships among the six types

The nature restoration and conservation types capture environmental conservation objectives; the experience-and-education, spatial separation, and integrated types capture approaches to balancing conservation and recreational use; and the selective-use type captures how recreational use is accommodated. Where a park exhibits more than one characteristic, it can be described in terms of their combination. Applying these perspectives together makes it possible to compare differences among parks in conservation objectives, recreational activities, and coordination approaches.

4.8 Limitations and future research

This exploratory study is based on managers' responses from a purposive sample of 39 parks undertaking nature restoration. Applying its findings requires consideration of environmental conditions, management arrangements, respondents' roles, and the timing of the survey. The inclusion of parks managed by the same organization should also be considered. Future work should examine the reproducibility of the findings by broadening the geographic coverage and range of management conditions and comparing survey responses with management records.

The indicators encompass the implementation of measures, recognition of management issues, resource conditions, and responses to users, with cross-loadings specified for some indicators. Indicators among both the explanatory and behavioral constructs are also based on responses concerning the same conservation activities. The Q5 indicator composition was revised using the same sample after an initial SEM analysis. Further examination of its reproducibility is therefore needed, with attention to sampling adequacy and item communalities in the factor analysis. Given the model's complexity relative to the small sample and its fit, the SEM coefficients are interpreted as exploratory associations. Future research should refine the measurement of implemented behavior and its preconditions and examine correlation and estimation methods appropriate to the response formats, as well as the effects of coding, including assigning zero to "unknown" and "no response" in Q5. Estimation stability should also be examined using larger samples, replication in other samples, and simpler models.

To translate associations obtained from manager reports at a single point in time into management improvements, it is important to document management histories, ecological conditions, and user evaluations over time. For the six types, relating park-specific practices to management objectives and examining their combinations and outcomes could further develop a framework through which parks with similar conditions can draw on one another's approaches.


5. Conclusions

This study examined conservation, recreational use, and efforts to coordinate the two, together with associated conditions, in 39 urban parks undertaking nature restoration in Japan. Experiential and educational activities and efforts to encourage appropriate user behavior were more widely implemented than spatial separation of activities or facilities. The distribution of responses indicating nonimplementation with no policy versus nonimplementation despite a recognized need also varied by activity. Among conservation activities, planting based on native species had a lower implementation rate than other activities, highlighting the need to consider the maintenance of policies and standards alongside individual tasks.

In the SEM, conservation and balancing efforts both showed positive associations with social norms, which primarily represent stakeholder involvement, whereas the directions of their associations with environmental quality differed. These results indicate the value of considering management arrangements alongside greenness inside and around parks when examining management priorities in relation to conservation targets and types of recreational use. For user-focused behavior, future research also needs to examine additional conditions, including recreational demand and operational decisions, beyond the arrangements supporting conservation.

Management orientations were further organized interpretively into nature restoration, conservation, experience-and-education, spatial separation, integrated, and selective-use types. The contribution of this study lies in examining activity content and its associations with management conditions using common questionnaire items across 39 parks, building on the Environmental Focus, Balanced, and User Focus management orientations identified in the previous qualitative study of five parks. Comparing experiential and educational approaches with spatial coordination in relation to conservation targets, types of recreational use, and management arrangements provides a basis for considering approaches to balancing conservation and recreational use suited to individual parks.
