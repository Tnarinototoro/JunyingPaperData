2.2 Construction and calculation of indicators

Three explanatory latent variables—social norms, user attitudes, and environmental quality—and three behavioral latent variables—environment-focused behavior, balance-oriented behavior, and user-focused behavior—were specified. Tables 1 and 2 present their operational definitions, observed indicators, and calculation methods.

Social norms comprised the involvement of government agencies, designated managers, citizens, experts, and other stakeholders in conservation activities, together with the breadth of complaint types concerning vegetation and wildlife management. Stakeholder involvement was measured as the proportion of stakeholder–activity combinations for which involvement was reported. User attitudes were constructed primarily from managers' reports of the volume and resolution of requests and complaints, together with greenness outside the park. Ecology-related complaint volume was measured using four-category ratings of complaint volume, and conflict resolution difficulty using four-category ratings of resolution status.

Environmental quality was represented by the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) within and outside each park. NDVI is calculated from near-infrared and red reflectance and provides an indicator of vegetation condition and change (Tucker, 1979; Pettorelli et al., 2005). In this study, mean NDVI values within and outside parks were separately standardized as Z-scores and used as proxies for greenness.

R = {a + b + (6 − c)/6}/3

For a and b, “yes” was coded as 1 and “no” as 0; c ranged from 0 to 6. The number of budget-shortage items was reverse-coded and converted to a 0–1 scale, after which the three components were averaged with equal weights. 2.3 Analytical methods and treatment of unknown responses

First, responses to Q2, Q4, and Q5 were summarized as numbers and percentages of parks. Implementation and recognition of management issues in Q2 were tabulated separately. Percentages were calculated using all 39 parks as the denominator, including unknown responses for each item. Responses to impacts in Q4 were likewise expressed as percentages of all 39 parks. Q3 allowed multiple responses, and the activity profile for each stakeholder type was summarized using within-row percentages of reported involvement.

In the descriptive summaries, “unknown” in Q5 and “implementation unknown” and “issue recognition unknown” in Q2 were presented as separate categories. In Table 4, the category “implemented; no response on impacts” in Q4 was distinguished from nonimplementation and included in the number of implementing parks. The coding in Table 2 was applied to the SEM composite scores.

Second, SEM was used to examine associations between the three management behaviors and the explanatory conditions. The questions elicited different types of information, including activity implementation, stakeholder involvement, funding, requests, and complaints. Indicator construction therefore reflected the purpose of each measure. The content classifications and calculation methods in Table 2 were used to summarize the extent of activity implementation and issue recognition in Q2, and funding availability and areas of shortage in Q6. As Q4 asked about perceptions of and responses to the impacts of implemented measures, its scores combined implementation and impact responses. Indicators from the other questions were similarly constructed using the methods in Tables 1 and 2 to reflect the information collected, such as proportions of stakeholder involvement and ratings of requests and complaints.

Q5 covered different measures, including spatial coordination, regulation of recreational use, and experiential and educational activities, under the common purpose of reconciling conservation and recreational use. When the item composition of the two Q5-based observed indicators was reconsidered on an exploratory basis following the initial SEM results, exploratory factor analysis was used as an auxiliary analysis to examine how the measures grouped together.

Both the eight-item and seven-item analyses used the 0–3 item scores shown in Table 2. The number of factors was fixed at two. Principal axis factoring based on the correlation matrix was used, followed by Varimax rotation with Kaiser normalization. The maximum number of iterations was set to 25 for both extraction and rotation. Missing-value handling was set to listwise deletion; zeros assigned to unknown responses or no response were not defined as missing and were included in the analyses. Coefficients in the output were sorted by magnitude, and those with absolute values below 0.30 were suppressed.

In the initial eight-item analysis, the absolute rotated loadings of item 3 on both factors were below 0.30. The remaining seven items were therefore reanalyzed using the same settings. Based on the output and item content, two composite indicators were constructed as mean scores: one comprising items 2, 6, and 7, and the other items 1, 4, 5, and 8, as shown in Table 2. Item 3 was retained in the descriptive summaries.

Exploratory factor analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 25, and SEM using IBM SPSS Amos 26. The same survey data were used to revise the Q5 item composition and reestimate the SEM. The final model was treated as an exploratory model of associations.

The model comprised three explanatory and three behavioral latent variables, with 16 unique observed variables. Breadth of complaint types, outside-park greenness, and outreach-oriented management behavior were each specified to receive loadings from two latent variables. For basic amenity and safety provision, a direct path from user attitudes was specified in addition to the loading from user-focused behavior. Model fit was assessed using χ², degrees of freedom, CMIN/DF, GFI, AGFI, and RMSEA. Path coefficients, loadings, and squared multiple correlations (SMCs) were interpreted on an exploratory basis as associations between management responses and conditions.

2.4 Use of the previous study and publicly available information

The previous qualitative study of five cases (Chen and Fukahori, 2026) informed questionnaire development and the interpretation of management issues. Information on conservation activities, facilities, recreational use rules, and management stakeholders published on the study parks' official websites was used as supplementary material to understand management practices. Quantitative comparisons were based on responses to the present questionnaire.

In the discussion, the Q2–Q5 results were considered from four perspectives—management orientations, the character of conservation activities, approaches to balancing conservation and recreational use, and acceptance of recreational use—and presented as six interpretive types.

3.6.1 Model fit

The assessment of model fit differed across indices. The model was not rejected by the χ² test at the 5% level, although GFI and AGFI indicated room for improvement. Given these fit indices and the sample size, the measurement and structural components are considered separately below, with the results treated as exploratory.

3.6.2 Relationships between latent variables and observed indicators

The relationships between explanatory latent variables and observed indicators showed several patterns. Indicators of stakeholder involvement in conservation activities had relatively large loadings on social norms, whereas indicators of complaint volume and resolution status had relatively large loadings on user attitudes. The loading from environmental quality was lower for within-park greenness than for outside-park greenness. The SMC for within-park greenness was also low, at 0.02.

Among the behavioral latent variables, environment-focused behavior corresponded relatively strongly to direct ecological management behavior; balance-oriented behavior to management–experience–empathy alignment and outreach-oriented management behavior; and user-focused behavior to tolerance of active, high-load recreational use. In contrast, the model explained little of the variation in environmental conservation awareness and the safety measure implementation rate.

Among the cross-loading indicators, breadth of complaint types corresponded more strongly to user attitudes than to social norms, and outreach-oriented management behavior more strongly to balance-oriented behavior than to environment-focused behavior. Outside-park greenness had similar loadings on environmental quality and user attitudes. Thus, some shared indicators had similar relationships with two constructs, whereas others corresponded more strongly to one.

3.6.3 Associations between management conditions and management behaviors

The structural component showed associations shared by conservation and balancing behaviors, as well as associations whose direction differed by behavior. The coefficients from social norms to both environment-focused behavior and balance-oriented behavior were positive. In contrast, the coefficient from environmental quality was negative for environment-focused behavior (−0.51) and positive for balance-oriented behavior (0.42).

The proportions of variance explained also differed among the behaviors. They were relatively high for environment-focused behavior and balance-oriented behavior, whereas R² for user-focused behavior was only 0.03. The coefficient from social norms to user-focused behavior was also negative, unlike those for the other two behaviors.

The direct path from user attitudes to basic amenity and safety provision was negative (−0.45), indicating an association with this composite score for safety and amenities.
